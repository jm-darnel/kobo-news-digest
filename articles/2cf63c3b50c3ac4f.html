The achievement gaps in NYC's public schools, stat by stat

By Joe Hong · Gothamist · November 27, 2026

It’s the height of high school and middle school application season in New York City. Applications are due Dec. 4 and Dec. 11 respectively.

Students are writing earnest application essays, preparing art portfolios and recording drama audition videos. It’s a complicated, stressful process for both students and parents. Critics say it can worsen existing educational inequities.

Gothamist crunched a wide range of education data, which shows that yawning achievement and opportunity gaps still plague the school system.

Black students have left the city’s schools at disproportionate rates and are far less likely to get an offer from one of the city’s highly competitive specialized high schools. Test scores and graduation rates are significantly lower for Latino and low-income students. And students who are white, Asian and Pacific Islanders or from more affluent families are more likely to be in gifted and talented programs and get into their preferred high schools.

Nicole Brownstein, a spokesperson for New York City Public Schools, said the city will continue investing in standardized, research-backed literacy and math programs to help close these gaps. She pointed to higher test scores for Latino and Black students in eighth grade as signs of progress.

“We know this work has just begun and is far from over,” Brownstein said in an emailed statement.

Here’s Gothamist’s deep dive into the city’s education stats:

Overall enrollment decline

Let’s start with who’s actually in the system.

For close to a decade, the number of students in public schools has been shrinking in the city and across the country, largely the result of low birthrates and more families choosing private schools or homeschooling, according to one study by Brookings.

Here in the five boroughs, enrollment has dropped 9% since 2016, but some students have left the city’s public schools more than others. Black enrollment has plummeted 22% over the past 10 years. White enrollment has only declined by 15% over the same period.

A 2026 study by the advocacy group Available to All found that New York City has the most segregated school system in the United States. Black students are more likely to go to majority Black schools. And the accelerated decline in Black enrollment means those schools are losing the funding that’s tied to enrollment. Less funding means schools are less equipped to support the students who remain.

“Historically there’s been a lot of divestment in Black communities,” said Kathy Hill, an education researcher at NYU. “It affects a lot of institutions, including the schools.”

3-K and pre-K enrollment

While overall enrollment has shrunk, the number of the city’s youngest learners has continued to grow. Enrollment in 3-K has increased since the program launched as a pilot in fall 2017 and has since expanded to every school district in the city. The number of students in 3-K is 13% higher this year compared to 2021, even as Pre-K enrollment for 4-year-olds has shrunk slightly over the same period.

Neither program is compulsory in New York. But education research shows that early childhood education programs can significantly boost cognitive development and academic achievement. One 2020 study found that students living in poverty can especially benefit.

But not all groups are signing up at the same rate. While Pre-K enrollment has increased among Asian and Pacific Islander, Latino, white and economically disadvantaged families, according to city data, fewer Black students are now enrolled in pre-K compared to five years ago.

Halley Potter, an education advocate and a former policy director at the Century Foundation, said that decrease is likely due to the disproportionate decline in Black student enrollment overall.

“A lot of it has to do with Black families moving out of the city because it’s unaffordable,” Potter said. “It might also intersect with the exodus of families with young children.”

In an effort to address inequities in early childhood education, the Mamdani administration earlier this year doubled the initial expansion plan of 3-K, adding 2,000 more seats.

Standardized test scores

Students in grades three through eight are required to take standardized tests each year in math, English language arts and science. Test scores have long fluctuated, but the data shows a persistent gap between white and Asian and Pacific Islander students on one side, and Black and Latino students on the other. As of 2026, white elementary-school students had the highest test scores for English language arts, with pass rates that were 25 points higher than Black students and 30 points higher than Latino students.

Aaron Pallas, an education professor at Columbia University’s Teachers College, said he’s grown disillusioned over the public education system’s failure to close achievement gaps for test scores, both nationwide and in the city. He said educational disparities take time to address and require a level of political will that is rare to find in most elected representatives.

He said closing the gap would require officials to play the long game and remedy poverty through affordable housing and lowering the overall cost of living. However, Pallas said elected officials often need quick wins, and short-term agendas don’t amount to long-term systemic change.

“Most things change slowly,” he said. “But politicians are often being pressured to show immediate results.”

Middle school admissions

New York City students typically attend an elementary school close to their homes. But as they near promotion into middle school, fifth graders and their families have some choice in where to go next.

Students get priority admissions in their neighborhood middle schools, but they can also apply to any school city wide, including gifted and talented programs and magnet schools. Admissions are largely based on grades earned during fourth grade.

Asian and Pacific Islander students are least likely to be placed in one of the three schools at the top of their lists. In 2026, 88% of Asian and Pacific Islander students got an offer from one of their top three. Compare that to 96% and 97% of Latino and Black students and 92% of white students.

Low-income students also matched to their top picks at a slightly higher rate than those from more affluent households.

For many students and parents, staying close to home is a priority. Some kids may help with family childcare responsibilities. Competitive middle schools are largely concentrated in Manhattan, which makes them less of a viable option for families living farther away in the outer boroughs.

High school admissions

High school admissions outcomes follow similar patterns. At 69%, Asian and Pacific Islander students again had the lowest rate of matching with one of their top three choices in 2026. Black and Latino students had higher match rates of 88% and 89%, respectively.

Asian and Pacific Islander students, Potter said, likely had lower match rates because, as data shows, they’re more likely to apply to selective programs.

But for students from low-income families, the trend is reversed compared to middle school admissions. Those kids were less likely to match with one of the top high-schools on their lists. This year, 73% of students living in poverty matched with one of their top three choices, whereas the rate was 85% for students not living in poverty.

Students receive a lottery number that, in part, determines their chances of getting into a program of their choice. They’re also relegated to tiers based on their grades in seventh grade. Kids can also sit for essays as part of the admissions process. And some submit portfolios and audition videos for art, drama, dance and other programs.

Potter added that making it easier for Black and Latino students to attend more selective high schools could help integrate schools.

“A system with as much choice as New York City’s also creates more segregation,” she said.

Gifted and talented programs

Disparities are starker for admissions to gifted and talented programs, especially at the five K-8 and K-12, gifted and talented schools that accept students from across the city, including NEST+m, or New Explorations into Science, Technology, and Math, on Manhattan's Lower East Side.

These five schools serve more white students and fewer economically disadvantaged students compared to the city’s overall student population, according to city enrollment data.

Standardized test scores at these schools are also significantly higher. According to Gothamist’s analysis, over 94% of students at the city’s five gifted and talented schools met or exceeded standards in English Language Arts and over 96% met or exceeded standards in math in 2026.

Mamdani has signaled a willingness to phase out gifted and talented programs. Former Mayor Bill de Blasio had also proposed phasing them out in 2021. De Blasio's successor, Eric Adams, suspended that plan indefinitely.

Specialized high schools

White and Asian and Pacific Islander students who took the test were offered spots at much higher rates in 2026, but Black students were disproportionately less likely to receive an offer.

Among those who took the test, 17% were Black. But Black students only made up 3.5% of the offers. Stuyvesant High School, one of the largest specialized high schools, accepted only three Black students in 2026.

In response to these long-standing inequities in selective programs, Mamdani has suggested using other factors, instead of just a single test, to consider students for admissions.

AP exams

Pass rates for Advanced Placement courses offer a snapshot into how well students are prepared for college. Those who pass more AP exams are more likely to finish college, according to some research.

Black, Latino and economically disadvantaged students were less likely to pass an AP exam in 2025. White and Asian and Pacific Islander students were more likely to score higher.

Students from low-income families had a pass rate of 58%, while students from more affluent households passed 75% of the time.

Graduation rates

Since 2021, the city has made little progress in increasing graduation rates, especially for historically disadvantaged students. The disparities for Latino, Black and economically disadvantaged students have grown since the pandemic. Last year, all three groups, as well as white students, reached a five-year low in graduation rates.

But compared to 20 years ago, the city has made considerable progress. In 2006, Asian and Pacific Islander students’ graduation rate was 10 percentage points lower than it was in 2025. Rates for Latino and Black students were both more than 15 points lower.
