Thanksgiving is a time for understanding meaning and cherishing memories

By Christina Greer, Ph.D. · Amsterdam News · November 24, 2026

When I was younger, we were taught about the pilgrims and the constructed narrative about the first Thanksgiving in America, but as I got older, I learned more about indigenous peoples who extended their hospitality to strangers who did not know how to navigate or survive the new terrain. This reality definitely complicates the contrived narrative so many American schoolchildren are taught year after year.

As an adult, I choose to think of Thanksgiving as a time for gratitude. Not just for the food on the table, but for the entire year that has passed. I give thanks for traditions new and old, and for the loved ones who can celebrate another year. This year, I will celebrate with my family and enjoy my mother’s southern cooking and my father’s grilled smoked turkey.

Since I was a little girl, I looked forward to my mother’s macaroni and cheese. I remember her “letting me” cut the cubes of cheese when I got old enough to handle a knife and I felt so proud to contribute to a dish everyone raved about. I have fond memories of my father smelling like smoke from the grill as he checked on his slow-smoking turkey, slipping me “chef samples” as he cut the turkey for the platter.

Last year, I hosted friends. We ordered Indian food and I brought out my finest dining set to serve our takeout as we laughed at our new tradition that involved zero cooking or cleanup. Thanksgiving is what we make it, no matter where we are or what we eat. It’s the gratitude that makes the holiday so special.

This year, I have a few close friends who are celebrating their first Thanksgiving without a loved one. That empty chair may be a glaring reminder of the grief they are still carrying. I hope we can drop a text or a quick call to remind our friends and loved ones that they are in our thoughts during what may be a difficult time. New traditions may involve new food and new people, but they may also involve adjusting to who is no longer there. I am wishing everyone a Happy Thanksgiving, or Happy Indigenous Reflection Day. However you celebrate this year, may you find joy, peace, and gratitude.

Christina Greer, Ph.D., is a professor at Fordham University; author of the books “How to Build a Democracy: From Fannie Lou Hamer and Barbara Jordan to Stacey Abrams” and “Black Ethnics: Race, Immigration, and the Pursuit of the American Dream”; senior policy fellow at Brooklyn Org; and co-host of the FAQ-NYC podcast.
