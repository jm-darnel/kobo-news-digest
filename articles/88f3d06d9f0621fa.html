NYC bill would give crash victims online access to NYPD investigation updates

By Charles Lane · Gothamist · November 26, 2026

New Yorkers seriously injured in traffic crashes and certain family members could get online access to updates about police investigations under a bill the City Council's public safety and transportation committees are set to consider at a joint hearing in December.

It would not change how police investigate crashes or require the department to post police reports online, according to the bill text. The NYPD could also withhold information that would interfere with an investigation or prosecution or that is exempt from disclosure under state public records law.

Tomasina Byron said she supports the bill.

Her mother, Judith Byron, 75, was killed in a hit-and-run crash in Sunset Park in October 2025. Police arrested a 33-year-old man more than a year later and charged him with leaving the scene and failing to obey a traffic signal, according to the NYPD.

Byron said the detective handling her mother's case was caring and always responded to her messages. But she said she had to keep contacting him for updates.

“I’d call him every day. I’d call until he answered the phone or I’d leave a text message or voicemail,” she said. “In my case, someone killed my mom, so I was relentless.”

An NYPD spokesperson did not respond to multiple messages seeking comment on the legislation.

Councilmember Mercedes Narcisse, who sponsored the bill, said constituents come to her office struggling to obtain information after crashes.

“For someone that’s dealing with all the anxiety, they don’t know what to do and how they go to the next step, having that information can be a game-changer,” she said.

Transportation Alternatives spokesperson Alexa Sledge called the proposal a “huge step forward” for crash victims.

Corey Hannigan, 36, the organization's policy director, said he was riding an electric Citi Bike in a bike lane on 42nd Street in Sunnyside on Nov. 6 when a driver struck him and left the scene. He spent the night at Elmhurst Hospital.

Hannigan said police told him his crash report would be ready within 72 hours. He said it wasn't available by then and that a detective didn't contact him until about six days after the crash.

“Unless I pester the detective and I don’t want to annoy him, I don’t know what he’s finding,” Hannigan said, adding that he went from store to store looking for surveillance footage himself. “All I know is what I found and what I sent him. And it goes into a black box.”

Hannigan said he went to his precinct on a cane to obtain his police report. He said the delay also complicated his efforts to arrange an MRI through no-fault insurance.

Narcisse said the bill does not currently require police reports to be posted on the proposed website, but she wants to add that provision and expects resistance from the NYPD.

Kate Brockwehl, co-chair of Families for Safe Streets New York, was struck by a driver while crossing Houston Street in SoHo in 2017. Weeks later, after obtaining a paper police report and filing for no-fault coverage on her own, an MRI revealed injuries that required knee surgery.

Brockwehl said having information about crash investigations and victims' rights more readily available would help people navigate the aftermath of a collision.

“Being hit by a car pushes you into this administrative hurricane that you’re in for years,” she said. “This bill would really restore some dignity and give back some time to people hit by cars.”
