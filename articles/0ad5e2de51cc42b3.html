N.Y. lawmakers question farm funding access as state looks to expand urban agriculture

By Nái Rhoden · NY1 · November 18, 2026

NEW YORK — New York lawmakers pressed the state’s agriculture commissioner Wednesday for more detail about who is receiving farming grants, as the state looks to expand urban agriculture and improve access to fresh food.

At an Assembly hearing in New York City, Commissioner Richard Ball described investments that include agricultural job training in Niagara County. He also acknowledged that efforts to fund organizations supporting Black farmers took a year or two to get started.

Assemblymember Khaleel Anderson, a Queens Democrat, asked how funding from several state farming grant programs was divided between urban and rural growers.

“Just want to know what percentage of dollars, amounts of those funds went specifically to urban growers versus rural growers,” Anderson said.

He also asked how many recipients were Black farmers.

Ball described separate funding programs serving different groups of growers but did not provide the full breakdown during that exchange. When Anderson followed up about support for Black farmers, Ball said the state initially struggled to find organizations qualified to accept funding.

“We’re doing better every year,” Ball said. “It took us a year or two to get started, because finding a group who was qualified for the state by the state to accept funding was a big challenge.”

Ball said the state’s Agriculture and Food Systems Workforce Development Grant Program recently awarded $3 million statewide. Some projects will support training specifically related to urban agriculture.

“This includes projects in both New York City and Niagara County, and others that will provide skill building and training, specifically for urban agriculture,” he said.

Separately, Ball said the state’s Urban Farms and Community Gardens Grant Program has dedicated more than $4 million over three funding rounds. Those projects support food production and distribution, among other activities.

Ball also pointed to free soil testing and training for community gardeners. He said the state’s Community Gardens Task Force has recommended reducing barriers to funding and improving coordination between growers and government agencies.

Lawmakers also explored options for growing food where land is expensive, including vertical farming, which involves growing crops in stacked layers, often indoors.

Ball said growing food in controlled environments can work, but requires technical expertise and presents financial challenges.

Both houses of the Legislature have passed A10703, a bill directing the agriculture commissioner to study vertical farming’s effects on access to locally grown food, employment, educational opportunities and the environment.

As of Nov. 18, the public legislative record listed the measure as having passed both houses, with no governor’s signature recorded.

If enacted, it would require a report to the governor and legislative leaders within one year, including recommendations for legislation and administrative programs.

For existing farming programs, Anderson’s questions sought a clearer accounting of where state assistance is going as lawmakers consider how to expand support.
