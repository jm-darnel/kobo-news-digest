From the Ground Up: Here’s What It Takes to Build, Build, Build Affordable Housing

By Samantha Maldonado · The City · November 16, 2026

Facing down New York’s housing crisis, city leaders of all stripes are pushing three words: build, build, build. But building affordable housing is a complex and time-consuming process.

But it takes years of work, millions of dollars and countless hours traversing red tape before new apartments are ready for tenants.

As part of The City Reporter’s series on the New York City affordable housing lottery, we took a look at a fairly typical 100% affordable housing project in The Bronx to show exactly what goes into making a new building like this one real — even before it’s listed on Housing Connect.

New York City severely needs to build more affordable housing, and those in power feel the crunch. On his first day in office, Mayor Zohran Mamdani said he’d quicken the pace. The day he took office, he convened a Streamlining Procedures to Expedite Equitable Development (SPEED) Task Force, whose recommendations promised to slash eight months from the time it takes to build affordable housing by focusing on interagency communication, financing, permitting, construction and lease-ups. On the state level, Gov. Kathy Hochul and the Legislature also reformed the environmental permitting process to accelerate housing development.

At Twin Parks Terrace, a 182-unit complex in The Bronx’s Fordham Heights, here’s what it took to transform a parking lot into the 14-story affordable apartment building: navigating multiple agencies, $81 million and almost a decade of work.

“There are no easy sites in New York. Things go from difficult to nightmare, and this was not a nightmare of a project,” said Eli Weiss, principal at Joy Construction, which oversaw the project with Settlement Housing Fund.

“The journey for any project is many years,” said Emily Lehman, Settlement’s vice president of real estate, who formerly worked at the city housing department for more than a decade. “The timelines we saw for this particular project might look different for a project that was awarded today.

2017: The proposed idea

2018: The concept gets a green light

HPD and NYCHA decided to move forward with Settlement’s proposal. The development team celebrated, and then they got to work.

So began what is called the “predevelopment timeline,” where several processes happen simultaneously so that everything is ready to put shovels in the ground as soon as the team locks down financing.

Danois Architects started advancing the project’s designs, which needed approvals from HPD and the Department of Buildings.

Meanwhile, environmental review took place, which analyzed impacts on air quality and noise, among other conditions. There was a mountain of paperwork to fill out and submit to various entities, including reviews for compliance and integrity.

At the same time, the team figured out how to finance the project with dollars from multiple sources. The general contractor priced out the project’s construction, a process that influenced both the financing side and the designs, too.

Each financing partner had a checklist with hundreds of requirements for the team to complete.

“I remember at the time saying that my checklists were having baby checklists,” said Judy Herbstman, president at Settlement.

2020: Money locked in, shovels in the ground

This marked the end of the predevelopment timeline, which took two years — “pretty fast by today’s standards,” Lehman said. “Two years is roughly the minimum the project spends in predevelopment, but it could take longer depending on the specifics of the project and subsidy availability.”

When the team closed, they signed a 99-year lease with NYCHA for the land, a process that included coordination with the federal Department of Housing and Urban Development. In the development world, leases set for 99 years are very common.

2021: Construction continues

Weiss, of Joy Construction, said starting in late 2020 meant the “initial craziness” of the Covid-19 pandemic had ebbed a bit. Though supply chains were snarled and shipping delays were common, the team had planned its supplies in advance and they were ready to arrive by the time the building’s foundation had been poured. And labor restrictions had eased up, too.

“There were two sides to Covid,” Weiss said. “There was everything you read about labor shortages, but there also wasn’t a lot of construction going on in the city. There was less traffic in the city. I don’t want to say Covid was helpful, but I think it netted out.”

Digging the foundation took about eight months, which was fairly typical for a project, Weiss said — even though challenges crept up during the excavation on The Bronx’s rocky ground. The team had to redesign the foundation because the soil was different than expected. The team also had to pay for a generator to power most of the construction work after Con Ed was unable to provide temporary power for many months. The generator’s gas was costly, Weiss said.

After the foundation, the team constructed the building itself, topping off at 14 stories five or six months later.

Another three to four months after that, the team enclosed the building, making sure it was weathertight, Weiss said. That allowed work to start on the interiors, including kitchens, bathrooms and floors.

2022: Marketing preparation

As construction continued, Settlement began to prepare for marketing the apartments in the building as part of the housing lottery on Housing Connect. The team worked with HPD to prepare advertisements for how the building would be shown to potential tenants.

2023: Construction is substantially completed, and the housing lottery opens

By February, applicants could apply to live in Twin Parks Terrace through the housing lottery on Housing Connect. There were 135 apartments priced for households making between $22,700 for one or two people, and up to $160,720 for a family of seven.

In addition, 46 apartments were set aside for formerly homeless New Yorkers, who were referred to Settlement by HPD.

“You get the list of people and start qualifying them so that people are ready to sign leases as soon as the building’s ready to open,” Herbstman said. “Then you continue qualifying households and signing leases after you get your temporary certificate of occupancy.”

Meanwhile, as construction neared an end, the team began closing out permits with various agencies and other entities. For instance, it needed Con Ed to turn the utilities on and to get a certificate of occupancy inspection with the Buildings Department, a key step for a building to be tenant-ready.

That same month, the lottery closed. By the fall, the first tenants started moving in.

Residents continued moving in.

2025: The building reaches full occupancy

After most of the tenants moved in and the building was operating for about three months, the team converted to permanent financing, which allowed them to pay down the construction loans and borrow with a lower interest rate while also unlocking more money for the project.

Today

Now, Twin Parks Terrace stands tall, wedged up a slope on a busy avenue. The building has play areas for children, laundry machines and bike storage. There’s a discount store on the ground floor, which also features another retail space for lease.

On a weekday afternoon, individuals and families streamed in and out of the building.

One of them was a tenant named Veronica Lopez. The sexagenarian moved into her one-bedroom apartment about three years ago, after seven years of applying to the housing lottery.

“The apartments are beautiful. When I came in, I was the first one in my apartment,” Lopez said. “It was clean, new. Everything was perfect.”

With a laugh she added, “It still is perfect because I have OCD.”
