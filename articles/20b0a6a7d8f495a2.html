What the Yankees are getting in lefty swingman Brent Suter

By Michael Zeno · Pinstripe Alley · November 24, 2026

After re-signing Tim Hill, bringing back David Bednar on the qualifying offer, and signing old friend Jonathan Loáisiga to a two-year deal, the Yankees brought in left-handed swingman Brent Suter on a one-year deal with a club option for 2028.

Originally selected in the 31st round of the 2012 MLB Draft by the Brewers, Suter has spent a decade in the league for five different teams, turning into a journeyman in his 30s, going from Milwaukee to Colorado to Cincinnati to Anaheim to Atlanta.

Above all else, the veteran is a model of consistency. Despite playing all sorts of roles in all sorts of ballparks, including the bandbox at Great American Ballpark and the thin air in Coors, he’s posted a sub-4 ERA in nine of his 11 seasons and rebounded from his worst year in 2025 (4.52 ERA in 67.2 innings) with a workmanlike 3.36 ERA in 91 innings.

For those unfamiliar with who he is as a pitcher, he’s a soft-throwing damage-controller. The 37-year-old has gotten by despite throwing a crisp 88 on his fastball and sinker, which he throws 60 percent of the time combined, by complementing them with a changeup and slider. He used to be a heavy fastball thrower (throwing it over 65 percent of the time from 2017-22), but has turned to his sinker as a reliable weapon against same-hand pitching, registering a +14 Run Value on the pitch over the last two seasons.

Suter doesn’t generate a lot of chases, whiffs, or strikeouts, but he does it more than your traditional soft-throwing lefty thanks to his offspeed. He’ll throw his changeup, sinker, and four-seamer all a third of the time against righties, but becomes more of a sinker-slider pitcher against lefties. The slider has a 32.5 percent whiff rate, but also allows the hardest contact.

Speaking of hard contact, he gives up very little of it. In his 11-year career, he’s been in the 92nd percentile or higher in average exit velocity nine times and in hard-hit rate seven times. What really intrigues the Yankees, though, might be the strides he’s made with his ground ball rate, which has spiked to a career-high 50.6 percent with his increased sinker usage in 2026. The Yankees’ 46.2 ground-ball rate as a bullpen last year ranked third in baseball, and Matt Blake loves his sinkerballers.

His most valuable trait for the team might be his versatility. He can be a fireman against lefties, eat innings in low-to-medium leverage, or make a spot start if needed. Think Ryan Yarbrough’s role over the last two years. Of course, the team’s copious rotation depth would make needing Suter for more than doubleheaders or spot starts a disaster, but he’s able to do it if needed. In 2026, he consistently threw between 35 and 50 pitches per outing.

While guys like Hill and Yarbrough used funky arm angles to be especially effective against lefties despite their low velocity, Suter has always been a neutral-splits pitcher. He had one of his better years against lefties in 2025, but that regressed in 2026. It’s vice versa against righties. Talk about a mixed bag.

He also brings a fair amount of personality to a locker room that sometimes feels too buttoned up. We’ve seen some colorful characters in the bullpen in recent years with Tommy Kahnle and Luke Weaver… but Suter might be 1-of-1 here in the “reliever odd-ball” competition:

His salary shouldn’t affect anything for the Yankees, but the addition of a club option continues an interesting trend. All three of the proper reliever contracts the team has agreed to this offseason have included a team option, as both Hill and Suter will have one for 2028, and Loaisiga will have one for 2029. Between the four reliever signings, that’s now over $38 million allocated to the bullpen so far this offseason.

So, with the other needs, does that mean they’re done? In my view, at least, not necessarily.

Suter might seem like a roadblock for those hoping the Yankees pursue Padres’ flamethrower Adrian Morejon, but that likely isn’t the case. Retaining Hill, signing Suter, and pursuing another lefty does seem odd with the presence of Brent Headrick as a high-leverage option already, but it’s probable that this is merely just a Yarbrough replacement as a multi-inning lefty.
