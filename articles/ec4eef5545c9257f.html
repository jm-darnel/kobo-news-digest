Lights Out: Broken NYC Streetlights Surge As Repair Times Get Longer, Data Shows

By Kennedy Sessions · The City · November 17, 2026

New York City is taking longer to fix broken streetlights, with the average time to complete repairs hitting its lowest rate on record, city data reveals.

An analysis by The City Reporter of Department of Transportation data found the number of 311 complaints for out-of-service streetlights has surged to 27,609 so far this year — a 6% increase from 2025 and 23% increase from 2024. The outages are causing safety concerns among residents and elected officials, who point to the risk of traffic crashes, crime and trips and falls in the dark, especially as the sun sets earlier during this time of year.

Some streetlights have been out for months, and several New Yorkers said their requests for service from the city have often dragged out or gotten no response.

“The setting, when you come out [of] the train station, it’s almost like a horror flick,” said commuter Jonny Pastrana on Wednesday night at Flatbush and Ocean avenues in Brooklyn, where a light pole across from the Prospect Park subway stop had long been out. “The lights is off. ‘Thriller’ video, yeah, that’s what it feels like.”

While the number of broken streetlight complaints so far in 2026 is down 31% from the five-year high of more than 40,000 in 2022, DOT spokesperson Vin Barone acknowledged the most recent winter factored heavily into the repairs backlog.

“Well-lit streets are essential to the safety and comfort of New Yorkers and they deserve nothing less,” he said. “This winter’s historic snowstorms and freezing temperatures took a toll on our aging street lighting infrastructure — some of which dates back to the early 20th century.”

‘It’s Like Pitch Black’

Bay Ridge resident Dimitri Kontakos said one streetlight on his 81st Street block between Fourth and Fifth avenues remained out of commission after a January snowstorm until crews removed the light earlier this month.

He compared the drawn-out response to “pulling teeth.”

“At night, it’s like pitch black,” Kontakos said. “I get that they’re busy and I understand it’s not going to happen the next day, but it’s frustrating for sure.”

Brendan Bolger, 80, said he and neighbors on Dahlgren Place in Bay Ridge have repeatedly contacted 311 about two streetlights that have been out for months, leaving him to feel “a little leery.”

“They said there were major problems with the light further down the block, that it’s a lot more work than just changing the bulb,” he said.

“Some repairs are straightforward, such as replacing a luminaire or changing a fuse, while more complex cases may involve electrical issues at the base of a pole, foundation work, utility obstructions or ConEd coordination to restore power,” it said.

In a statement, Con Edison said it works with the city to “get streetlights back in service as soon as possible, as we have a shared commitment to public safety.”

‘Bread-and-Butter’ Constituent Services

DOT’s Barone said the city has committed to replacing all LED lights citywide as part of its Vision Zero plan to eliminate traffic deaths. The Mamdani administration is also aiming to develop a connected streetlight network in the coming years to automatically detect outages and speed up repairs while improving visibility in high-risk locations, including beneath elevated trains and highways.

State Sen. Andrew Gounardes said he and City Councilmember Kayla Santosuosso — both Democrats who represent Bay Ridge and parts of Dyker Heights — have asked DOT to conduct a “thorough review” of the streetlight repair backlog because of what they said were more than 130 inoperable lights in Brooklyn Community Board 10 as of the end of October.

“Those are typically very easy things to solve. It’s like a bread-and-butter constituent service case,” Gounardes said. “Someone calls in, ‘This is a problem, here’s my 311 number, can you help me figure it out.’ We do — usually, it’s pretty routine.”

At Flatbush and Ocean avenues, Fox White said the broken lights add to the stress of crossing a “gnarly intersection.”

“This area in general is just kind of dark,” White said. “I don’t notice the streetlights in particular, but it does make the area kind of gnarly.”
