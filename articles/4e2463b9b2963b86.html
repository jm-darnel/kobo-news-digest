Exploitation or Opportunity? The Fight Over Amazon’s New York Drivers

By Greg David · The City · November 24, 2026

“ATTENTION: NYC Council could add up to $5 per order,” a pop-up notice on the Amazon app and website said, threatening higher costs if the City Council passes a bill that would ban the retail giant from using subcontractors to deliver packages across the five boroughs.

A bright yellow button below the message urged customers to “Make your voice heard today.”

It’s the latest escalation in the company’s more than $5 million crusade to block a bill by Councilmember Tiffany Cabán (D-Queens) that seeks to force Amazon to directly employ thousands of drivers it currently subcontracts to a constellation of independent “delivery service partners.” Cabán says her bill would make it easier for workers to organize and file wage and safety complaints against the company.

Amazon, in turn, has threatened for months that if the bill succeeds, it will be forced to move its delivery operations outside of the city and fire thousands of workers, while shuttering its delivery service partners — many of whom it describes as minority-owned small businesses.

Here’s what to know about the battle pitting Mayor Zohran Mamdani, much of the City Council and the Teamsters union against Amazon, the city’s business community, and dozens of local delivery businesses.

What Is the Delivery Protection Act?

Depending on who you ask, the bill that’s going before the City Council is either a major step towards protecting thousands of New York delivery workers, or an overreach by labor-friendly lawmakers that will result in massive job losses and higher costs for consumers.

At its core are claims that the people delivering packages for Amazon in New York are employees in all but name, wearing Amazon uniforms, following routes set by Amazon, and adhering to Amazon policies, all while on the payrolls of small independent delivery businesses.

Proponents of the bill say that direct employment by Amazon will give workers greater protection from abrupt layoffs and better protection from, and compensation for, on-the-job injuries. Delivery owners say that the same laws and workplace rules that would apply to Amazon already apply to them.

Objections to Amazon’s arrangements echo longstanding efforts around the country by unions and progressive politicians to make the parent companies of franchise operators, like McDonald’s, responsible for their franchisees. All such efforts up to now have failed.

Conflicting Claims on Workers

“These workers are being exploited,” Samuel Levine, commissioner of the Department of Consumer and Worker Protection said at a conference last month.

Defenders of the companies paint a very different picture.

“These are true entrepreneurs who have worked very hard to build legitimate businesses that do follow the rules and they should be allowed to continue,” said Jessica Walker, president of the Manhattan Chamber of Commerce, who is vocal in her support of the delivery companies.

Amazon has rejected claims that its model makes these entities little more than — as the mayor and some top aides have called them — “shell companies” that shield the online behemoth from accountability.

“These aren’t shell companies — they’re 4,500 entrepreneurs across 22 countries who’ve built real businesses, generated $75 billion in revenue, and created hundreds of thousands of jobs,” said Amazon spokesperson Steve Kelly, citing nationwide numbers.

“The vast majority are running profitable, growing operations — and profitability has improved year over year,” he said.

Small Business, Big Business

Among them is Rudy Cazares, a former Marine Corps officer who said his company, Cazar Logistics, will go out of business if the City Council gets its way.

“Delivering packages for Amazon has given opportunities for individuals like myself to be successful and provide an opportunity for individuals who wouldn’t have this opportunity if they applied to corporate America,” he said.

Owners of delivery companies say they don’t recognize the way Mamdani and proponents of the bill have portrayed them.

One owner left a job in finance to start a company that pays its drivers more than $27 an hour and offers a $5,000 tuition reimbursement. A driver for another company left FedEx because, he says, his Amazon delivery company pays better. Other workers say their companies offer chances for advancement that they can’t get in the corporate world because they lack college degrees.

“I think that small businesses have a purpose and they certainly offer a benefit for me as a person with not a huge intellectual background but a breath of lived experience,” said Shannon Lindley, a high school graduate who is general manager at a Midtown logistics hub. “I can get somewhere in this small company.”

While Amazon has threatened to shut down delivery operations in New York City should the bill pass, workers say the company already regularly yanks jobs away. In August, the Teamsters charged Amazon and 13 subcontractors with violating the state’s WARN Act by failing to give the approximately 1,300 workers at least 90 days’ notice of their impending terminations, according to a complaint filed to the state labor department.

Earlier this month, Amazon severed its contract with a Delancey Street-based subcontractor that provided same-day delivery on electric cargo bikes. Two hundred workers were laid off overnight. Notice of the impending layoff does not appear on the state’s WARN notice dashboard, in another apparent violation of state law, Streetsblog reported.

“They have these random ads, talking about ‘We care so much about Black and brown entrepreneurship’ — they just fired 200 black and brown workers in a company owned by a Black man,” one of the laid-off workers, who spoke on the condition of anonymity, told The City Reporter. The worker says he supports the Delivery Protection Act because it would allow workers like himself to directly hold Amazon accountable.

One expert compared Amazon’s forceful objection to the bill with Uber’s push to block a measure that raised drivers’ minimum pay rates. Just as Amazon warned of higher delivery costs, Uber said it would have to pass on fees to its passengers as well — and at one point threatened to shut down operations in New York.

“Amazon gets away with making claims that are just totally false,” said Elizabeth Pancotti, vice president of policy, advocacy and research at the Groundwork Collective, a left-leaning think tank. “Uber made the same empty threats and look at that — it is easier than ever to get an Uber ride in New York City.”

From the Trenches

Jorge Arias, who until recently worked at a Maspeth-based delivery service partner, said he and his coworkers were pulled into a meeting on the dangers of the Delivery Protection Act. He still supports the bill.

“They said if the Delivery Protection Act passes, we’re going to be like the UPS drivers: ‘They don’t have new vans, but you have new vans,’” recalled Arias. “And I’m thinking, you know, most Prime vans are broken.”

Local delivery companies say they’re providing good, flexible jobs at good wages.

Logistics veteran Christine Chen set out two years ago to take advantage of Amazon’s enormous market share. While Amazon says it costs about $30,000 to establish a delivery company, Chen put in more than $60,000 to get Highgate Logistics off the ground — money well spent, she added. She uses bikes to deliver packages in Harlem.

Staffing up for the holiday season, Chen now employs about 220 people earning $21 an hour, or $4 more than the city’s minimum wage. She offers health care, dental and vision insurance, plus a 401K match.

Devon Smith, 23, a dancer with the Alvin Ailey Dance Theater, has been working for Chen since June. He works mornings so he can attend rehearsals in the afternoon, earning about $700 for 30 hours a week.

“As artists, we work hard but it costs money to survive in the city,” he said. “Artists are terrible at jobs, but this is something we can do. It’s not the same thing every day and people interact with you — which is rare in New York City.”

Council Speaker Julie Menin is reportedly moving to finally put the bill up to a vote on Dec. 3. The speaker, a former city consumer protection commissioner, has not taken kindly to Amazon’s tactics.

“To put out an ad that falsely claims that if you purchase something on Amazon you’re gonna get socked with a $5 fee apiece, every single time, is completely misleading and deceptive, and it does not add to the negotiation we’re trying to have,” Menin said on Thursday in response to questions from The City Reporter.

“I have made very clear from the start that we are not going to do something that will cause us to lose those jobs in New York City,” Menin said.

Meanwhile, Mayor Mamdani hasn’t been shy about using his physical and digital bully pulpit in support of the bill. On Monday, he said there was “a worker safety crisis” and that the DPA would “put in place some basic guardrails to regulate what is an expanding last-mile industry.”

Is This Going to Become Law?

In its current version, the bill has enough supporters in the Council to ensure its passage, and Mamdani has endorsed the measure as it stands today.

But Menin is reportedly weighing changes before it is put for a vote, including a compromise that would still allow third parties to handle package deliveries so long as they are “true independent contractors,” City & State reported.
