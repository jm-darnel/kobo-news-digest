'Cameron Winter at Carnegie Hall' is a coronation. A performance at Carnegie always is.

By Sonia Rao · Gothamist · November 20, 2026

Bob Dylan was only 22 years old when he first performed at Carnegie Hall’s main auditorium in 1963. But his youth didn’t deter the respect the solo concert garnered. In a glowing review, The New York Times critic Robert Shelton referred to Dylan as “unlike any poet or song writer to be heard today.”

“He is assuming the role of a radical spokesman,” Shelton wrote. “The adulatory reception of his listeners indicated that he was speaking for them, too.”

Echoes of Dylan’s early acclaim resounded throughout Carnegie Hall’s Stern Auditorium decades later, when the singer-songwriter Cameron Winter commanded the stage last December at the grand old age of 23. Winter, a drawling baritone whose talents have been lauded ever since he broke out as the lead singer of the New York rock band Geese, sat at a Steinway & Sons piano with his back to the audience as he performed stripped-down versions of songs from his 2024 debut album “Heavy Metal.” The hall reached its nearly 3,000-person capacity. But the rapt audience listened in near silence, which was only broken by jubilant applause after each of the dozen numbers on his setlist.

Simon Vozick-Levinson understands the comparison. But as Rolling Stone's deputy music editor, who wrote a September cover story on Geese’s skyrocketing success, he also wonders whether it even makes sense to measure Winter against Dylan.

“Pretty much every songwriter who’s emerged in the last 60 years gets compared to Bob Dylan if they get good enough,” Vozick-Levinson said in a recent interview. “I think the thing that’s most notable about [Winter] is how little he sounds like any of his influences. You can hear little bits of Dylan or Tom Waits or Leonard Cohen in there, but … he sounds like himself, and that’s what’s most impressive.”

So, setting Dylan aside — what about Carnegie Hall? Plenty of artists have played on that stage, but what does it mean for Winter to have done so while recording a live album and performing for the cameras of a celebrated director in the midst of his own momentous awards season? (Anderson captured Winter’s show during a brief break from promoting “One Battle After Another,” which won the Best Picture at the Oscars three months later.)

I recently visited Carnegie Hall to meet with Kathleen Sabogal, director of the concert venue’s Rose Archives and Museum. She gave me a tour of the grandiose Stern Auditorium, descending from the balcony to the parquet level. Gazing upon the rows of lush velvet seats as an audience member, the hall can seem rather imposing. But the curved space is designed to feel intimate from the performer’s perspective. “When you’re on stage,” Sabogal said, “it kind of feels like it’s hugging you.”

Next, Sabogal accompanied me to the museum. After pointing out a few concert flyers — including one for Dylan’s first-ever formal concert, held in 1961 at the smaller, now-defunct Carnegie Chapter Hall; tickets cost $2 each — she showed me a list she had compiled of artists she believed to be Winter’s predecessors as rising stars at the main hall. Joan Baez played her first solo performance there at 21; James Taylor at 22; Neil Young at 25; Joni Mitchell at 25; Carole King at 29 — all of which unfolded between the years 1962-1971, before the explosion of rock musicians who played the hall over the following decade.

“It feels like a pinnacle,” Sabogal said. “It’s an important place to play. Also, acoustically, it’s a perfect hall for singer-songwriters … just playing the guitar or the piano.”

When Rechtshaid saw Winter at the first of his two sold-out shows at Greenpoint’s St. John’s Lutheran Church in April 2025, he knew he admired the performer’s live sets enough to attempt to record the following night’s show.

“There was a magic in the room that compelled me to go Alan Lomax style,” Rechtshaid said, referring to the ethnomusicologist known for his field recordings of 20th-century folk music. “I showed up the next night with a tape machine and two mics.”

Rechtshaid mentioned Winter while casually hanging out with Anderson (whom he called “my boy Paul”). The filmmaker listened to Winter’s music and told Rechtshaid he was intrigued by the contrast between the studio recording of “Heavy Metal,” which features sparse instrumentation, and Winter’s live performances, which were even leaner. Anderson signed on to capture the concert after it was confirmed Winter had booked Carnegie Hall. He recruited fellow director Benny Safdie and other camera operators to shoot the December show with five Panavision 35mm cameras — cumbersome equipment that Rechtshaid and Safdie said underscored the team’s deep commitment to the task.

On the day of the concert, Anderson asked Rechtshaid whether everything looked good on stage. Rechtshaid remembered looking at the Steinway piano and wondering why it was positioned backward, facing the wall. Anderson said he wasn’t sure.

“Then it was just like, ‘Okay, I guess this is what he wants,’ you know?” Rechtshaid said. “Suddenly we’re filming Cameron at Carnegie Hall with his back to the audience.”

Sabogal, the Rose Archives director, also wondered why Winter sat this way. “But that’s the beauty of Carnegie Hall,” she said. “You don’t have to follow tradition.”

It takes an especially skilled performer to connect with nearly 3,000 people at once — let alone all the theatergoers who will join them a year later. Vozick-Levinson, of Rolling Stone, credited Winter’s enchantment of the Carnegie Hall audience to his “remarkable vulnerability and openness” as a lyricist and performer.

More than 60 years after Shelton referred to Dylan as “an angry young man with a guitar,” New York Times film critic Manohla Dargis reviewed “Cameron Winter at Carnegie Hall.” She described Winter’s demeanor at the piano as “borderline monastic,” noting that he barely smiles or banters with the crowd. He behaves as an “especially serious classical concert pianist,” she writes.

People have spun all sorts of theories online about how Winter’s cult following came to be, given that, as Dargis notes, his music “is an acquired taste.”

But that’s what people said — and continue to say — about Dylan, too. His quirks, off-putting to some, were eventually overpowered by the strength of his songwriting. With that in mind, the younger man’s popularity doesn’t seem all that complicated. Vozick-Levinson put it simply: “It’s just that the songs really are that good.”
