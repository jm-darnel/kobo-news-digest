Mamdani lays out plan to close Rikers by 2032, after years of delays

By Ryan Kost · Gothamist · November 20, 2026

Mayor Zohran Mamdani’s long-awaited plan to close Rikers Island, released Friday, would empty the island’s jails by the end of 2032, moving the detainees held there to four new borough-based jails and three hospital units while turning the island itself over to climate and sustainability projects.

At the center of the plan is a sharp reduction in the city’s jail population over the next several years, to about two-thirds of the current 6,600 — a benchmark some advocates worry may be hard to meet and sustain in the decades ahead.

Even so, the proposal is the clearest sign yet that the first-year mayor intends to keep his campaign promise to shut down one of New York City’s most notorious institutions, known nationally for its chaos and dysfunction.

“Our administration is determined to meet our moral and legal obligation to close Rikers Island once and for all,” Mamdani wrote in a letter that opens the plan.

But advocates like Elizabeth Glazer, who helped write the original closure plan as former Mayor Bill de Blasio’s criminal justice director, warn that without broad reform across the Department of Correction, the city “risks merely exporting the violence of Rikers into new ZIP codes when the neighborhood-based facilities are completed.”

At least nine people have died in the Department of Correction’s custody this year, and not all of them on the island. In August, one man died in a new medical treatment outpost at Bellevue Hospital, the first facility to open as part of the replacement system for Rikers. Two others died at borough courthouses.

She described it as “a pulling together of work that has been agreed to [a] long time ago that just never was prioritized under Eric Adams, and some expansions into key areas that are going to help us achieve the target jail population.”

Darren Mack, co-director of Freedom Agenda, which has long pushed to close Rikers, said advocates were excited by the strategy.

“We can finally remove this moral stain from our city, once and for all,” Mack added.

“We will not transfer the historic and endemic failures of Rikers to new locations across New York City,” the plan promises.

Decades of calls for a closure

Calls to close Rikers date back to at least the late 1970s, when then-Mayor Ed Koch first floated the idea of replacing the massive jail complex with smaller jails across the city’s boroughs. Closure gained real traction in the 2010s, inspired in part by the story of Kalief Browder, a Bronx teenager sent to Rikers in 2010 after being accused of stealing a backpack. He spent three years on Rikers, much of that time in solitary confinement. Eventually, his case was dismissed. Browder died by suicide two years later.

By 2017, a commission led by former Chief Judge Jonathan Lippman came to the conclusion that “Rikers must be closed.” De Blasio, who had previously resisted calls to build an entirely new system separate from the island, finally endorsed the project, calling Browder’s death “a wake-up call to this city.” Two years later, the City Council formally voted to move to the new borough-based jails even over fierce opposition from neighborhood groups.

At Rikers, out-of-sight, out of mind is the norm for both people in our custody and our staff.

As all of this played out, human rights abuses at Rikers mounted, and the city entered into a consent decree with a federal court over the use of force on the island. The judge in that case appointed a monitor to track the department's ongoing reform efforts.

“Close Rikers” advocates have long argued that the island itself was central to the dysfunction. The complex, for instance, sits on a gas-emitting former landfill. Buildings can’t be adequately cooled or ventilated. And thousands of cell doors are broken. What’s more, the island can only be reached by a single road, and a trip using public transit can take at least an hour and a half, which presents issues for families, lawyers and those who work there.

“At Rikers, out-of-sight, out of mind is the norm for both people in our custody and our staff,” Correction Commissioner Stanley Richards said in a video released with the plan.

Initially, the closure seemed within reach. During the COVID-19 pandemic, the jail population fell below 4,000 for the first time since the 1940s, the result of unprecedented cooperation between defense attorneys, prosecutors and court administrators to slow the spread of the coronavirus. The progress, however, was short-lived. Under the Adams administration, the jail population rebounded, borough-based jail construction lagged and costs nearly doubled.

Meanwhile, conditions on Rikers have only festered. In January, the federal judge overseeing reforms handed broad control of the jails to a remediation manager, Nicholas Deml.

Reducing the jail population

Under Mamdani’s new plan, the borough-based jail system would come online gradually over the next six years. His administration has taken a few steps already, such as hiring the city’s first "Close Rikers Czar" and opening the Bellevue treatment unit. Two additional hospital units, one in the Bronx and one in Brooklyn, are expected to open by 2028. The first new jail facility would open in Brooklyn in 2029. The others would follow in 2031 and 2032.

Rikers facilities would be emptied and decommissioned as new beds become available. Altogether, the new facilities would have room for 4,500 people.

In order to make the transition work, the city will have to move quickly and forcefully to reduce the jail population. Under the plan, the city is aiming to get the jail population down to no more than 5,500 by the time the Brooklyn facility opens in 2029 — and then to 4,100 by the end of 2032.

In order to hit those figures, the administration is committing $290 million to expand diversion programs, supervised release, re-entry support and faster case processing.

One of the biggest challenges, according to the plan, is the slow pace of the city’s courts. The jail’s population didn’t grow as a result of more arrests, the plan argues, but rather because people are waiting longer for their cases to resolve.

In New York City, the median time between a felony arrest and a case’s final disposition is 431 days, roughly four months longer than in the rest of the state. At least 1,400 Rikers detainees have been awaiting trial for more than a year.

“We do not want any New Yorker to spend a day longer in detention than necessary,” Mamdani said in the release video.

Chief Administrative Judge Joseph Zayas said in a statement included in the plan that the courts “must not accept a status quo that tolerates excessive delay.”

Achieving this sort of population reduction will take cooperation on the scale of what happened during the COVID-19 pandemic, said Glazer, who is also the founder of the Vital City think tank and policy journal.

“In the same way that the police department has CompStat [a public data reporting system] about crime, the city needs to have a kind of jail stat about who’s in jail and why, how many people are coming in, and how long are they staying,” Glazer said. “That’s what drives the jail population, and that has to be looked at every single day.”

'Only half the challenge'

In his own letter attached to the plan, Richards, the first formerly incarcerated person to lead the Department of Correction, notes the new facilities “represent only half the challenge.”

“We must also build a safer, more supportive culture within our jails today, so that it is firmly established when we make the transition,” he wrote.

To that end, the plan pledges to replace the island’s 2,450 defective doors, improve sanitary conditions and “reimagine” staff training.

Much of that has been mandated by Deml, the remediation manager, who earlier this year noted that the Department of Correction struggled to perform basic correctional functions. The plan also accounts for a $50 million investment in programming for people in custody that Deml had recommended.

Christopher Rodriguez’s death at Bellevue in August illustrates the shortcomings of DOC training and protocol, regardless of facility. According to a Board of Correction report obtained by Gothamist, officers at the new unit had skipped required overnight tours in the time before the 36-year-old was found unresponsive. Six officers were suspended.

Unions will be central to any reform, and the plan names the Correction Officers' Benevolent Association as a partner, noting the department has successfully worked with unions in the past to improve working conditions. COBA President Benny Boscio did not respond to requests for comment.

Nurse, the councilmember, blamed the union for years of stalled reform, but noted the remediation manager has the power to circumvent it.

“We have the right conditions right now,” Nurse said, adding that the mayor and the City Council are aligned.

“If we fail to do this, if we fail to successfully transition to these borough-based jails, it will be a huge embarrassment to the city,” Nurse said.
