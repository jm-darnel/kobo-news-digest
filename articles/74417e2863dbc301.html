Yankees Potential Free Agent Target: Adrián Morejón

By Jeremy Nielsen · Pinstripe Alley · November 24, 2026

It’s worth noting that of the quartet of signed relievers, only Bednar posted an above-average strikeout rate in 2026. This stands consistent with a more recent preference the Yankees have exhibited for ground ball pitchers than guys with pure wipeout stuff. But let’s not kid ourselves: a successful bullpen needs to rack up the whiffs (especially if the Yankees still want to rely on defense which can be charitably described as ‘inconsistent’).

Luckily, the top relief arm on this year’s free agent market can provide strikeouts by the truckload—and more to the point, is coming off a multi-year stretch as an elite late-inning performer. That would be lefty Adrián Morejón, who has spent his entire professional career with the San Diego Padres.

2026 Statistics: 76 games, 86 IP, 2.83 ERA, 2.37 FIP, 2.64 xFIP, 30.4 K%, 8.0 BB%, 1.08 WHIP

2027 FanGraphs Depths Charts Projections: 79 games, 79 IP, 2.83 ERA, 2.86 FIP, 29.0 K%, 8.3 BB%, 1.14 WHIP

But as it turns out, he’s not just great at missing bats. He has also run an outstanding 53.4 percent ground ball rate in that span—offering the best of both worlds. In the event you manage to hit one of Morejón’s offerings, it usually doesn’t wind up going very far. The best part? While most free agent relievers typically hit the market in their early-to-mid 30s, Morejón will be entering his age-28 season in 2027.

Morejón’s signature pitch is his sinker, which he throws roughly half the time. The sinker has been key to his evolution from prospect to All-Star, and has been his most used offering since 2024; it’s where all those worm-burners come from, after all. His best option against right-handed hitters is his changeup, which has obliterated any semblance of a platoon advantage for opposing lineups. The league collectively hit .156, slugged .182, and missed 56 percent of the time against that thing in 2026. His off-speed option against lefties is a slider, which tails away from them toward the opposite batter’s box. Morejón nearly abandoned his standard four-seam altogether, but brought it back into the fold this past season, where it performed well as a wrinkle to throw off hitters who were expecting the two-seamer.

Morejón can typically be relied upon to get more than three outs: in 2026 manager Craig Stammen used him for more than one inning 26 times. Only two relievers last year tossed more than Morejón’s 86 total innings. In case you’re worried that the workload took its toll down the stretch, he actually pitched markedly better in the second half. There just aren’t very many demerits to this guy’s profile, anywhere.

So make no mistake: Morejón’s getting a bag from somebody. The question is: will it be from the Yankees?

Ever since the messy conclusion to Aroldis Chapman’s Yankee tenure in 2022, the Bombers have been reticent to drop a hefty chunk of change on relievers. The thinking is easy to understand: they’ve had tons of success pulling pitchers off the scrap heap. Developing overlooked arms on rookie deals is good business, and it’s a business in which they excel. Faced with that, why allocate resources to a relief pitcher which could otherwise go to one of the Yankees’ more pressing needs at second, third, or center field?

As expected, there are already plenty of salary projections out there for Morejón. On the lower end, Jon Heyman has predicted 4 years and $72 million ($18 average annual value), while Tim Britton is far more bullish, forecasting a five-year deal for Morejón with a $22 million AAV, adding up to a good bit over $100 million in total salary. Personally, I believe Britton’s estimate will be closer to reality, due to his age, consistency, lack of recent serious injuries, and a weak free agent market. The only relievers in Morejón’s airspace—guys like Alex Vesia and Bryan Abreu—are older, and haven’t pitched as consistently great as Adrián has.

Obviously, the Yankees are more than capable of paying Morejón whatever the market rate winds up being for his services. I am still uncertain that they will be the team to do it, but I’m not convinced that their success with reclamation projects is a good enough reason on its own not to sign him. After all, we’ve seen in recent years that the relief crew they leave harbor with in March is rarely good enough to make it to July without some serious reinforcement. You could pass on Morejón in the offseason, but remember that you did if you have to part with more young talent to make your bullpen shipshape again.
