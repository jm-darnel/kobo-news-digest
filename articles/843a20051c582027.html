Firefighters, EMS call for staffing increases to combat fires connected to lithium-ion batteries

By Karen Juanita Carrillo · Amsterdam News · November 26, 2026

Jointly held by the Committee on Fire and Emergency Management and the Committee on Housing and Buildings, the hearing showed that there is a disconnect between the laws proposed and what emergency workers believe they need to do their jobs. Firefighters, fire officers, and paramedics insisted that new regulations will accomplish little without the staffing required to enforce fire codes and get water onto fast-moving blazes.

Council Speaker Julie Menin said that she convened the oversight hearing in response to the surge in fires.

“The numbers in terms of fatalities and injuries are just completely unacceptable,” Menin said. “This is a crisis of epic proportions. The council has passed bill after bill after bill, yet the problem is getting worse, not better. We want to have a real, rigorous oversight hearing about why this is happening.”

Councilmember Joann Ariola, chair of the Committee on Fire and Emergency Management, noted that “as e-bikes, e-scooters, and powered mobility devices have become more prevalent, the city has experienced a rapid increase in lithium-ion battery-related fires both in residential and commercial properties.”

FDNY Commissioner Lillian Bonsignore testified about the severity of the surge in fires. The city has recorded 90 fire fatalities so far in 2026, up from 63 in 2025 and 77 in 2024. Incidents involving lithium-ion batteries have grown steadily, rising from 268 in 2023 to 279 in 2024 and 292 in 2025. As of Nov. 13, the department had already logged 421 lithium-ion fires this year. Direct battery-related fatalities dropped to one in 2025 from 18 in 2023, but climbed to nine this year, according to the FDNY — a total Speaker Menin estimated at 12.

“This is a concerning increase, and we have intensified our efforts to educate and enforce around this issue,” Bonsignore testified, attributing part of the surge to older, uncertified equipment still in use.

“The FDNY’s lithium-ion battery task force visits every bike shop in the city and responds to complaints and tips about potentially unsafe batteries,” Bonsignore added. “This group performed a total of 843 lithium-ion battery-related inspections last year and has performed nearly 500 such inspections this year. So far, we have cracked down on the sale of uncertified batteries and bikes to the point that we now only rarely come across individuals in those shops selling uncertified products.”

Bonsignore said FDNY is urging everyone to alert the department if they learn of unscrupulous sellers and that when inspectors get complaints, they respond within hours. “However, we also know that there are thousands of bikes and batteries in use that were purchased prior to strict regulations going into effect. As these products age, they are likely contributing to the increase in fires,” Bonsignore said.

However, frontline inspection and emergency response workers testified that their workforce is stretched dangerously thin.

A Bureau of Fire Prevention official said the department’s e-safety task force consists of only “one chief inspector, one deputy chief inspector, one supervising inspector, [and] eight actual inspectors,” along with two fire marshals paired daily with the Department of Consumer and Worker Protection, leaving just 11 inspectors targeting battery compliance in all five boroughs.

Rank-and-file firefighter representatives have told the Amsterdam News that senior inspectors at the Bureau of Fire Prevention have been sidelined from public safety campaigns and official recognition.

When firefighters enter burning structures, they say they need the engine company staffing to fight the fire. Uniformed Firefighters Association President Robert Eustace, a 25-year veteran of the Bronx fire service, directly addressed Ariola’s opening question about whether the city is doing enough. “Councilmember, you started this conversation, this hearing today, by asking, ‘Are you doing enough? Are you doing everything?’ That answer is clearly no,” Eustace testified. “These lithium batteries — they start off, they go off like a blowtorch. They’re faster than anything we’ve had before.”

Eustace said the city needs to restore a fifth firefighter to every engine company and that a matter of seconds can determine survival when toxic battery fires erupt. “We are talking about pennies on the dollar here,” he reiterated. “Seconds matter.”

Jack Splain of the Uniformed Fire Officers Association warned that lithium-ion blazes pose tactical hazards in residential buildings, especially overnight when e-bikes block single-staircase exits. “Upon arrival on scene, our members are met with an advanced fire that is extending from its ignition source to the building’s upper floors and possibly to other nearby structures,” he said.

Splain called on lawmakers to lobby state leaders in Albany to close a legal loophole allowing potentially defective batteries to be stored in commercial shops directly beneath residential apartments.

Paramedic Christian Bahr-Lopez pointed to the ongoing shortages among emergency medical service personnel, testifying that “our EMS workforce continues to face staffing and retention challenges.” He urged the council to pass Intro 1182, which would establish an independent EMS department separate from the FDNY.

Lawmakers agreed that legislation alone cannot curb the rising death toll from fires without increased enforcement personnel. “We must have enforcement of our city’s laws for them to be effective,” Ariola concluded. Councilmember Julie Won agreed, noting, “Passing legislation is only half the battle. We also need meaningful enforcement.”
