Why Brooklynites Jeffries and Schumer are still favorites to lead Democrats in Congress

By Brigid Bergin · Gothamist · November 12, 2026

This column originally appeared in The Politics Brief, our weekly newsletter on the people, power and policies that shape New Yorkers' lives.

Outside the Beltway, there’s been an outpouring of pushback against electing House Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries as speaker and Senate Minority Leader Charles Schumer as majority leader. But political observers say it’s what happens inside the Beltway that counts, and that the two Brooklyn-based politicians are unlikely to be knocked off their leadership perches.

One big reason: no one has formally stepped up to challenge either lawmaker.

In the House, Jeffries' election as speaker would mark historic firsts. He is poised to become the first Black speaker of the House and the first speaker from New York City. While a handful of newly elected Democrats have not committed to supporting him — including incoming local Reps. Claire Valdez and Darializa Avila Chevalier — no other lawmaker has emerged to challenge him.

Rep. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez, who sits decidedly to Jeffries’s ideological left but has said she will support him, went on Instagram on Monday to explain how the internal leadership election works. The real vote takes place in a private Democratic caucus meeting through a secret ballot, she explained. If no one is challenging Jeffries, then members have no one else to vote for.

“ When you vote, it’s not this person's name, ‘yes or no’. That's not how this ballot looks,” Ocasio-Cortez said. “ The question needs to be, ‘Who do you actually want for the speakership? Who is running?’”

Democrats are expected to hold nearly 260 seats when the next session begins in January. Jeffries only needs 218 to be elected Speaker. That means he has three dozen seats worth of negotiating room — a much more comfortable margin than House Republicans had in recent years.

“I think anything over 230 makes Hakeem’s life a lot easier A) in terms of getting elected, and B) in terms of passing bills without having to cater to the left wing of the caucus as much,” said Adam Carlson, head of the progressive polling firm Zenith Research.

In the Senate, Schumer’s prospects are more complicated.

Vermont Sen. Bernie Sanders, the de facto leader of the left, said he would not be supporting Schumer and that “the time is now for change.” He held a press briefing Tuesday with Michigan Sen. Elissa Slotkin, who said she was undecided.

There are rumblings that the 75-year-old veteran lawmaker may face a challenge from Maryland Sen. Chris Van Hollen, who told reporters this week that he was in “listening mode” and had not ruled out running against Schumer.

“ If Van Hollen really throws his hat into the ring and goes full bore, I think he's the only one who can theoretically put together a coalition that could work, because he's an insider,” Carlson said.

New York’s junior Sen. Kirsten Gillibrand, who served as chair of the Democratic Senatorial Campaign Committee, said she was not "privy" to any conversations with Van Hollen and was standing firmly by Schumer.

“ He's done a great job marshaling our caucus these past two years to be laser-focused on cost, corruption and chaos,” Gillibrand said. She credited him with supporting candidates across the country who flipped at least seven Senate seats from red to blue, raising over $500 million for candidates.

“ I think people have a lot of gratitude towards Senator Schumer,” Gillibrand said, “and I think he'll win any Majority Leader race he has.”

Schumer would need a majority of the Democrats 54 to 56-member caucus to elect him majority leader. That’s roughly 29 votes.

Jeffries voiced strong support for his fellow Brooklynite.

“ Chuck Schumer was the quarterback of a team effort led by Senate Democrats and incredible candidates who ran all across the country,” said Jeffries, citing wins in Texas, Maine, Michigan, Iowa, Kansas, South Carolina, Georgia and potentially in Alaska and Mississippi.

“I think the results of that election, the outcome, the decisive victory speaks for itself,” said Jeffries.
