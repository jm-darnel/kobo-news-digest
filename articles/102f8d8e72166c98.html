National Urban League’s new civil rights museum tells the Black Northern civil rights story

By Karen Juanita Carrillo · Amsterdam News · November 12, 2026

Black history in the North began long before Southern families boarded trains for the Great Migration. Generations of Black people had been enslaved in the North, fought for their freedom here, and built communities. In its retelling of the African American Civil Rights struggle, the National Urban League’s new Urban Civil Rights Museum in Harlem (UCRM) will center the lives of Black northerners during its grand opening and ribbon-cutting on Nov. 14,

Located on the fourth floor of the NUL’s Empowerment Center at 117 West 125th Street, organizers say the UCRM is New York City’s first museum dedicated to showing how Northern communities resisted injustices.

“There needs to be something else because the southern Black experience is not the totality of the Black experience,” Marc H. Morial, NUL’s president and CEO, told the Amsterdam News.

“The southern Black experience is not the totality of the Black experience.” Marc H. Morial, NUL president and CEO

“What existed in the North? Slavery! It was in every single state, except maybe Vermont,” Morial explained. “Black communities included enslaved communities and free communities, and then coming into the 20th century, there was the movement of people, some six million who left the South, coming to the North as a part of the Great Migration, and the communities and the neighborhoods they created led to new cultural, political, and economic traditions.”

Morial said the idea for the museum came up during NUL’s 2010 centennial celebration, when exhibits shown at a mobile museum proved that there needed to be a permanent institution documenting Northern civil rights history. The new museum is in NUL’s headquarters where they also offer affordable housing, nonprofit office spaces, and retail space. “We own this building; we own this museum,” Morial noted, while emphasizing that ownership gives NUL the ability to create a museum that shows an unfiltered historical interpretation of Black civil rights.

Five centuries, eight galleries

“When you walk in, there’s lots of colors and textures; it feels very contemporary,” she said. “We wanted people to start with the past but then also make connections to the present, to their present lives, and to think about the future and the kind of change they wanted to see in the world, they want to make, they want to participate in, and how to just reflect on becoming potential agents of social change.”

Freedom Square introduces visitors to the museum through an immersive film. Changemakers Murals greet them in the lobby.

“Roots of the Urban North,” covering 1500–1900, looks at the transatlantic slave trade, Northern slavery, the Underground Railroad, and free Black settlements.

“The Great Migration,” covering 1900–1970, follows two waves of migration that brought nearly six million Black people from the South to Northeastern, Midwestern, and Western states.

“The Harlem Renaissance,” covering 1920–1940, explores cultural and political expression.

Displays show issues of the NUL’s “Opportunity: A Journal of Negro Life” and the famed Black newspapers like the Amsterdam News, Pittsburgh Courier, and Chicago Defender, which, a UCRM wall label says, “actively shaped the world about which they reported.”

“Fighting for Justice,” covering 1940–1960, and “Finding Our Way,” covering 1970–present, show the changing civil rights struggles, political empowerment, and contemporary movements. “Seat at The Table,” the resource gallery, asks visitors to think about their own participation in the struggle.

“Every era looks different, and we wanted to kind of show those differences throughout the gallery,” Scott explained. In every era, Black people have faced new challenges: “The injustices, although connected, look different in each era. And so, the ways that people creatively resisted and worked against it to secure their civil rights all looked very different.

“It includes activism, depending on how people defined it. Some people protest; some people advocate for certain kinds of legislation to be changed. In the 19th century, as people were being enslaved and trying to become free, civil rights could look like running away from enslavement, becoming a fugitive, breaking an unjust law, or working within the law to petition [for] your freedom or purchase your freedom. But it could also look like learning to read when reading was illegal, or building a free Black community, or building a church that you can worship in safely and not in a segregated or second-class fashion.”

Scott worked with the exhibition design firm Local Projects to match stories up with photographs, objects, projections, music, and digital displays throughout the museum. UCRM’s inaugural rotating exhibition, “Thriving! Harlem as Homeplace,” will be on display Nov. 14 through May 17, 2027. It honors neighborhood places and the people who sustained them. It features historic housing footage, maps, and a photography-studio recreation that allows visitors to see Harlem through the lives of its everyday residents.

“We’re very proud to be in Harlem,” Scott said. “You probably know this already, but the National Urban League began in Harlem in 1910. So building this building [and] opening the museum is a homecoming for us. It’s like a homecoming.”
