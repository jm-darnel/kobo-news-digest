Meet the Syracuse tile guy with a monopoly on NYC subway mosaics

By Stephen Nessen · Gothamist · November 16, 2026

When the colorful, intricate tile mosaics featuring the names of subway stations like Columbus Circle or Cortlandt Street need to be replaced, the MTA relies on just one man to get the job done.

He lives 250 miles north of the city in Syracuse. He’s 73 years old. And he wishes the MTA would understand you can’t rush art.

“They'll say, ‘Oh, don't you have that just laying around? Can't you put it together?’ And I go ‘No,’” said Robert Shenfeld, who has a monopoly on restoring the MTA’s century-old tile mosaics. “Every station was done differently, and that was done for a reason, so that people would know their stop.”

MTA officials acknowledged Shenfeld doesn’t always work on their preferred timeline. But they said they have no other choice.

“Sometimes there's a tension between getting the work done faster and cheaper, as we're always intending to do, and ensuring that it's done well and better,” said MTA head of construction Jamie Torres-Springer.

Shenfeld says his ceramic studio is the only one on the planet capable of recreating the intricate tile designs that are a signature of the sprawling mass transit system. His workshop — tucked down a gravel road next to a motel and an open field — is filled with a team of artists who are the masters of the city’s subway tiles.

He’s recreated mosaics at 50 subway stations, including the recently renovated Borough Hall in Brooklyn, his biggest project ever. His ongoing mosaic projects include 14th Street Union Square, Jamaica-Van Wyck in Queens and Parkchester in the Bronx.

Shenfeld previously worked as a school art teacher, but in 2008 he bought the tile-making equipment from a woman in New Jersey who previously oversaw the MTA’s mosaic restoration work.

“In order to do mosaics with the MTA, you have to have done mosaics with the MTA,” he said. “So the only way for me to do it was to buy the company that was making them.”

His team painstakingly replicates the exact colors and designs of mosaics the MTA needs replaced. The work is so detailed and labor intensive that Shenfeld’s team regularly blows deadlines imposed by the transit agency. But Shenfeld notes you can’t rush perfection. And the MTA has little recourse because of the Syracuse studio’s monopoly.

Shenfeld’s replacement of the mosaics at Borough Hall weren’t ready by the time the MTA cut the ribbon on the overhauled station earlier this year, so crews put up a poster until the mosaic was ready to be installed a month later.

“A lot of ceramic artists do mosaic, but having the equipment, the knowledge of how that goes together and the knowledge of the color range, I'm probably the only one I know in the country or maybe the world that can do it,” he said.

During a recent visit to Shenfeld’s studio, it was clear why the work takes so long.

“The original glazes were lead-based, so we can't use that,” he said. “So we do things that are close to that.”

Some minerals used in the glazes are harder to come by these days, like lithium, which creates a matte surface on the tiles. It used to cost $10 dollars a pound, but now goes for $200 a pound because it’s in high demand for rechargeable batteries.

”Silica, that's another one, the prices have skyrocketed,” Shenfeld said.

Shenfeld’s team mixes and matches minerals, and then fires them in kilns.

John Das, one of Shenfeld’s longtime employees, did most of the work matching the colors on the Borough Hall mosaic. He said the MTA didn’t make the job easy.

“They didn't send us the nice and clean picture,” Das said.

He used a computer to recreate how the sign likely looked when it was new — in 1908. It took two years to complete, but his two brothers, wife and brother-in-law all helped.

Das, 39, arrived in Syracuse in 2008 as part of a resettlement of 60,000 Nepalis to the U.S. who’d been kicked out of Bhutan. Shenfeld hired him three years later.

Shenfeld said the prospect of his retirement has been a source of consternation with MTA officials.

He recalled telling NYC Transit president Demetrius Crichlow, “You know I’m 73, I might retire, but I don't think so. What would you do? And he goes, ‘You're not retiring.’”
