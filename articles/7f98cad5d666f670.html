Yankees Birthday of the Day: Lefty Gomez

By Matt Ferenchick · Pinstripe Alley · November 26, 2026

However, you cannot win 27 World Series title by throwing out just anyone on the mound. There are still plenty of all-time Yankee greats that were pitchers. One of them was Lefty Gomez, a Hall of Famer in his own right.

Vernon Louis “Lefty” Gomez Born: November 26, 1908 (Rodeo, CA) Died: February 17, 1989 (Greenbrae, CA) Yankees Tenure: 1930-42

Gomez was one of a number of Yankees of his era to come from California, as he was born there in 1908. His father was of Spanish-Portuguese descent and was a cowboy on a farm north of San Francisco. The kid later known as “Lefty” grew up riding horses and milking cows.

In baseball, Gomez grew up playing local sandlot games and was pretty quickly identified as a talent. By 14, he was already getting noticed by the Pacific Coast League’s San Francisco Seals, although they passed on the young, still scrawny Gomez at that time. After his high school career ended, the Seals finally agreed to ink Gomez, although the pitcher had to win over his father, who wished for Lefty to go to college to become an engineer.

Upon reporting to the Yankees for 1930, the team had similar concerns about his frame. They sent Gomez to a “health farm” during spring training. However, he impressed enough to start the year in the big leagues with the team. However, he struggled during his rookie campaign. Not only was he a rookie, but Gomez had suffered an injury after getting hit in the face with a liner back in spring training. Doctors suspected that botched dental work in the aftermath of that had caused an infection that was still bothering the pitcher. Gomez eventually got all his upper teeth pulled and replaced and eventually got sent down to the minor league St. Paul Saints.

After a year there, Gomez came back up to the Yankees in 1931, albeit put in the bullpen by new manager Joe McCarthy. However once again, Gomez battled his way into the rotation by simply being too good to deny. By the end of the season, Gomez had won 21 games while posting a 2.67 ERA.

Now a full-fledged star of the rotation, Gomez won another 24 games in 1932, although stats like ERA had him a bit below league average for the season. However, his knack for winning got Gomez the start in Game 2 of that year’s Fall Classic, and he delivered. Gomez threw a complete game allowing just two runs, only one of which was earned. The Yankees won the game and the series in a sweep over the Cubs.

Over the years, Gomez became a star for the Yankees as they became baseball’s preeminent dynasty. Gomez helped the Yankees to four more World Series titles over the course of his career, as they won a then-record four consecutive titles from 1936-39. Not only did Gomez win five championships in five trips to the Fall Classic, but he put up a perfect 6-0 record as a pitcher. That’s both a World Series and an MLB postseason record for the most wins by a pitcher without having ever taken a loss.

Individually, Gomez made seven consecutive All-Star teams from 1933-39. His best year came in 1934, when he won a league-leading 26 games, while also besting baseball in stats like ERA en route to the classic pitching Triple Crown (he won another in 1937), as well as modern ones, including 8.4 rWAR.

Gomez also became known as one of the most colorful characters in baseball, getting the nickname “Goofy.” While Yogi Berra has the most notable quotes of anyone in Yankees’ history, Gomez shouldn’t be far behind . Some of his best include replying to a question about whether he would brush back — another thing he was known for — his own mother with a pitch by saying, “You’re damn right I would. She’s a good hitter.”

Once while facing Bob Feller, Gomez — a horrifically bad hitter, even for a pitcher — lit a match. When asked by the umpire, “Do you think that match will help you see Feller’s fast one?” Gomez said, “No, I’m not concerned about that. I just want to make sure he can see me.” While filling out a job application following his retirement, Gomez listed the reason for leaving his prior employment as “Couldn’t get anybody out.”

In 1939, Gomez suffered an arm injury while covering first base, and that would be the beginning of the end for him. The injury began to affect his pitching motion and he was limited to just 263.2 total innings over the course of the 1940-42 seasons. He did pitch well in the middle of those seasons in 1941, but McCarthy somewhat controversially didn’t use him in the World Series victory over the Dodgers.

Gomez eventually found his way into coaching and managed in the Yankees’ minor league system, including helming a team with a young Whitey Ford. He later left coaching and had a sales job with Wilson Sporting Goods. Thanks to his affable nature, he also became a popular dinner speaker and talk show guest. Gomez was voted into the Hall of Fame by the Veterans Committee in 1972. He eventually settled back in his native California, where he resided until passing away in 1989 at age 80.

Lefty Gomez was an undeniably great pitcher. However, it’s debatable whether he would’ve been as well remembered had he not been a member of those great Yankees teams and was putting up those stats for a middling one. However, he was a reason those Yankees’ teams were as good as they were. After all, you can’t win championships with outstanding talent on just one side of the ball.
