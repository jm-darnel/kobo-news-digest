MTA Drivers Taking 50 Sick Days a Year May Be to Blame For Your Canceled Bus

By Jose Martinez · The City · November 18, 2026

Close to 1,000 bus operators call out sick in excess of 50 days a year, MTA officials said Wednesday, charging that no-show “bad apples” have left riders hanging on nearly 120,000 trips this year alone.

“Don’t milk the city and state and regional taxpayer so you can sit at home or go to Florida or whatever,” Janno Lieber, MTA chairperson and chief executive, said in response to a question from The City Reporter.

Just under one in 10 bus operators take more than 50 paid sick days a year — with limited consequences because of a difficult disciplinary process, Lieber said.

Those days add up to a lot of cancelled trips — and taxpayer money. The MTA said it loses $20 million for every day in lost productivity across its entire workforce.

MTA Chief Financial Officer Jai Patel outlined how most employees are expected to be available for service 227 days a year out of 261 potential workdays.

“In reality, the latest data puts us at about 201 days, with about 19 sick days taken and another seven for workers’ comp and other reasons,” she said.

MTA data shows that as recently as 2013, the number of productive days per employee had climbed as high as 207. That number plummeted to 193 during the COVID-scarred year of 2020, when transit ridership plummeted during the pandemic even as workers kept the subways and buses running.

Officials said the frequent use of sick days comes with a high cost for commuters and the MTA’s finances, citing how the transportation authority has to rely on overtime and reserve bus operators to fill shifts.

But Patel said that some bus operators are “basically whole again” by taking an overtime shift that makes up for sick days.

“We’ve seen that persistently in sort of the bad actors in that sick-leave policy,” she said.

“Many of them are doing the right thing and showing up to work day in and day out,” Lieber said. “But about a thousand of the operators are taking a significant number of sick days, over 50 days per year.”

That has amounted to 118,000 canceled trips so far this year, he added, because “there was no bus operator available to drive the bus.”

“It burns everybody else who’s showing up to work,” Lieber said.

Transport Workers Union Local 100, which represents the largest number of MTA bus drivers, declined to comment through a spokesperson. The union is in the midst of contract negotiations with the MTA.

“We are working hard to get the TWU contract settled,” Lieber said. “I think we are making progress and I’m not ready to say anything more specific than that, but that is very much a priority.”

MTA Board Member Marc Herbst said he was “shocked” by the high sick-leave numbers.

“If I had a job where I only had to show up four days a week and not even include vacation and personal time, that’s unbelievable,” he said.

“Some people work at odd hours when the express bus may run only hourly,” said Vittorio Bugatti, founder of the Express Bus Advocacy Group. “For those people working at night and heading to Manhattan, they may be forced to take an expensive Uber if they live somewhere where there literally is no alternative like a subway.”

Lieber said it’s very difficult to discipline or fire workers who frequently call out sick. He blamed “too many roadblocks” in the disciplinary process against absentee bus drivers for the low worker-availability rates, saying it’s “really hard to get those people out the door.”

“We need to cut through all that and have a short, predictable disciplinary process,” he said. “It will be fair for them because they will hopefully get the message and come to work.”

“Or they can go get work at another place, they’re not ready to work at the MTA,” Lieber added. “Go find another job.”
