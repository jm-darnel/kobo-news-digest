Queens DA Katz seeks to block prosecutor watchdog investigating wrongful convictions

By Graham Rayman · Gothamist · November 25, 2026

After taking office as a reformer, Queens District Attorney Melinda Katz is seeking to block a new state watchdog from investigating the wrongful convictions of three men who spent decades in prison for the murders of a check cashing store owner and an off-duty detective.

In the suit filed under seal, Katz argues the Commission on Prosecutorial Conduct “suffers from a fatal constitutional defect.” Gothamist obtained a copy of the lawsuit, which centers on the commission’s demands for records tied to the wrongful convictions of George Bell, Rohan Bolt and Gary Johnson.

Legal observers pointed to Katz’s suit against the prosecutorial watchdog as evidence she’s become more resistant to wrongful conviction cases in recent years.

“Katz won her election based on a promise of transparency and review of prosecutorial misconduct — two commitments she has abandoned,” said Ron Kuby, a lawyer who represents clients with wrongful conviction claims.

“She has consistently rejected transparency when it would embarrass the office and she has consistently defended corrupt prosecutors even when she agreed to vacate the convictions they wrongfully caused.”

Katz’s lawsuit, filed earlier this month, was first reported by the Queens Daily Eagle. The nature of the commission’s probe into the Bell, Bolt and Johnson case is unclear.

When Judge Joseph Zayas tossed the men’s convictions, he wrote that the case “was not an isolated instance of misconduct, but part of a larger pattern of behavior that was calculated to deprive the defendants of fair trials.”

Katz touted the exoneration of the men in 2021, saying in a press release that “There can be no true justice, in Queens County or anywhere, unless we hold ourselves to the highest standards facilitating the process by which justice is sought.”

No prosecutors were ever disciplined in connection with the Bell, Bolt and Johnson case. In August, a state grievance committee found “insufficient evidence” prosecutors violated rules or law governing attorney conduct.

Katz argues in the lawsuit that the prosecutorial conduct commission has no authority to investigate prosecutors who have left the DA’s office or died. The suit also states grievance committees have authority over attorney discipline — not the commission.

Russell Neufeld, a former attorney-in-charge of the Legal Aid Society’s Criminal Division, described the Bell case as “one of the most egregious examples of prosecutor misconduct in the past 30 years.”

“The DA’s office is doing everything they can to stop the inquiry. It means they will do whatever they can do to protect the misconduct,” he said.

“The entire reason the commission was set up is because the grievance committees don’t discipline prosecutors for misconduct.”

Brendan Brosh, a spokesperson for the Queens DA, defended the office’s record.

“District Attorney Katz is deeply committed to righting the grave injustices of wrongful convictions and imprisonment,” Brosh said.

Then-Gov. Andrew Cuomo signed the commission into law in 2018 after years of alarm across the state over cases of prosecutorial misconduct that sent men to prison for decades for crimes they did not commit.

Katz’s suit states the commission only began operations in 2024.

So far, the commission has released just two annual reports and one public finding — censure of Monroe County DA Susan Dooley for ignoring a police traffic stop.

But in recent months, the commission has been ramping up its inquiries.

Last week, the commission sued the Suffolk DA Ray Tierney and police in state court alleging they were violating the law by refusing to comply with the subpoenas.

On Monday, Orange County DA David Hoovler sued the commission, alleging its subpoenas to his office are improper. It’s not clear what cases the commission is investigating in both counties.

“This appears to be a coordinated effort by the prosecutors to undermine an entity that the legislature found was a necessary response to the problem of prosecutorial misconduct,” Ross Firsenbaum, a lawyer for the commission, said. He emphasized he was only commenting on the Suffolk and Orange County disputes due to confidentiality rules covering the Queens case.

“We support the Queens DA’s efforts to ensure that proper disciplinary procedures are followed,” Tierney said in a statement to Gothamist.

“The association’s position is that all attorneys practicing in New York are subject to the authority of [the courts system's] grievance committees, which are justly empowered to censure, suspend, and disbar. We respect that jurisdiction and those processes to improve the profession of law.”

Katz was elected in 2019 under a banner of reform following the 29-year tenure of Brown, a tough-on-crime DA whose office was exposed in a series of reversed homicide convictions and multimillion-dollar settlements for bending and breaking rules.

Katz promised “dramatic and immediate change” and created the borough’s first Conviction Integrity Unit.

From 2020 through 2022, the unit backed exonerations in nine cases, including Bell, office press releases show.

Since Katz won re-election in 2023, the unit's output has appeared to slow. The unit has not put out a press release since that year.

Brosh, the Queens DA spokesperson, countered that across her nearly seven-year tenure, Katz has vacated 17 convictions based on evidence pointing to innocence or other errors that had been overlooked.

He said 100 additional convictions have been vacated based on unreliable evidence provided by law enforcement officers.

Allen Porter, who was convicted in 1992 of a Queens double homicide, and his lawyers say they had a frustrating experience with Katz’s office. After they had a decadelong battle with the DA for records, Katz's Conviction Integrity Unit agreed to take the case in 2021.

Porter’s team said the probe inexplicably stalled two years later. Porters attorneys said they grew so fed up they withdrew from the Conviction Integrity Unit process and instead filed a motion with a judge to vacate Porter’s conviction.

Katz’s office was compelled to turn over key documents, and in January Judge Michelle Johnson ordered Porter be released from prison after more than three decades.

“It is not just the amount of undisclosed evidence that is alarming, it is the people’s continued attempts to justify it and explain it away,” Johnson said from the bench. “The cumulative violations cannot be understated.”
