'They’re blowing this': Amazon's push against NYC delivery worker bill may be backfiring

By Walter Wuthmann · Gothamist · November 20, 2026

Amazon issued an ultimatum to New Yorkers on Thursday, warning its deliveries would get more expensive if lawmakers pass a bill forcing the company to hire many of its contract delivery workers as regular employees.

People across the five boroughs who opened their Amazon apps to order dish soap or dog treats were greeted by a new banner ad with the word “ATTENTION” bolded and highlighted in red.

“The NYC Council is considering Intro 518, legislation that could increase fees for New Yorkers by up to $5.00 per order,” the ad reads. “This bill would raise costs just when we need to make New York more affordable.”

But the move appears to be backfiring by antagonizing Council Speaker Julie Menin, who could soon bring the measure to a vote.

Menin, a moderate Democrat who is often viewed as an ally of the city’s business class, in recent days grew noticeably frustrated in her public statements as the e-retail giant ramped up its lobbying efforts against the bill, called the Delivery Protection Act.

“Amazon’s campaign is nothing short of a cynical ploy to trick New Yorkers,” Menin said at a press conference Thursday. “It is a last-ditch effort to try to escape the responsibility to the very workers and customers who have made the firm’s success possible.”

Supporters of the bill immediately cried foul.

“Amazon is a multitrillion-dollar company,” Mayor Zohran Mamdani posted on social media. “If Jeff Bezos adds $5 to your order, it’s not because of City Council's efforts to protect workers' rights and keep our streets safe. It's greed.”

“It's a bunch of BS,” said Queens Councilmember Tiffany Cabán, the measure’s lead sponsor. “We knew that they were getting desperate.”

Amazon argues the system empowers small businesses and ensures packages are delivered quickly and at low cost. The company points to a study they helped fund that found the Delivery Protection Act would raise costs for customers by about $400 to $660 a year.

“As the City Council continues to review this bill, we want to be transparent with our customers about what it would cost them if it passes as written — which is why we’re doing this,” Amazon spokesperson Kelly Nantel said in a statement. The company has threatened to move its warehouses out of New York City if the bill passes.

Supporters of the bill argue the measure won’t increase costs for customers, but would improve livelihoods for thousands of delivery workers. Delivery drivers organized by the Teamsters union say the system allows Amazon to escape liability for unsafe work conditions and faulty vehicles. Advocates on the City Council say the bill would force accountability by making Amazon hire its own drivers.

“ There's no world where this common-sense bill would cost Amazon $5 for every single delivery,” Cabán said in an interview. “This is just an example of corporate bullying. They want to bully New Yorkers with junk fees.”

City lobbying data shows the Five Borough Jobs Campaign, a group almost entirely funded by Amazon, has spent over $8 million this year opposing the bill.

In 2015, then-Mayor Bill de Blasio and members of the City Council tried to institute a cap limiting the number of for-hire vehicle licenses issued to companies like Uber and Lyft. Uber hired Bradley Tusk, a tech startup PR adviser and investor who managed Mayor Michael Bloomberg’s 2009 re-election campaign, to help sink the proposal.

Tusk describes in his book "The Fixer: My Adventures Saving Startups from Death by Politics," how he flooded the zone with negative TV, radio and digital ads while also ordering his team of lobbyists to “suffocate” individual councilmembers and their staffs. Similar to Amazon issuing a notice to its customers, Uber added a feature in New York known as “de Blasio view” to its app that told users to expect extensive wait times for a car if the legislation passed.

In an interview with Gothamist, Tusk said while Amazon’s strategy may appear similar on the surface, the company was failing to deliver a credible message.

“Nobody believes that Amazon's going to stop delivering in New York,” he said. “So saying, ‘Oh, well, it might be a little more slower,’ or, ‘It might be a little more expensive’ — that doesn't move people.”

Tusk said that Menin could’ve been a sympathetic ear. Instead, he said the negative ads appeared to be driving her closer to politicians to her left, like Mamdani and Cabán, both members of the Democratic Socialists of America.

“Julie is no pushover,” Tusk said. “They’re blowing this.”

While supporters of the Delivery Protection Act like the Teamsters have called for an immediate vote on the bill, Menin has pumped the brakes, saying the legislation needs changes to address legal concerns.

Menin insists the changes won’t weaken the core intent of the bill, but protect it from potential legal challenges. A spokesperson for Cabán’s office said they expect the proposal to come to a vote by the end of the year.

“I have a very clear message for Amazon,” Menin said direct-to-camera during her Thursday press conference. “If you think that that is going to deceive New Yorkers and intimidate the City Council out of passing basic worker and consumer protections, you are dead wrong.”
