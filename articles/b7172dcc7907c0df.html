NYC renters face ‘higher fees, reduced housing access’ due to co-signer company, lawsuit says

By David Brand · Gothamist · November 24, 2026

New York City tenants often face a familiar predicament when they try to rent an apartment: Landlords typically require them to earn an annual income of at least 40 times the monthly rent—or find a wealthy co-signer with that kind of cash.

It can be a challenge when median asking rents hover around $5,000 in Manhattan and $4,000 in Brooklyn, according to listings site StreetEasy, but when median income sits at only about $80,000, Census data shows . For New Yorkers without a parent, aunt or family friend to co-sign the lease, a host of financial companies have entered the market offering to guarantee the rent for an upfront fee.

Now a class action lawsuit filed in Manhattan federal court charges one of the largest of those companies, TheGuarantors, with inflating renters’ costs by using exclusive agreements with landlords that prevent tenants from seeking lower prices elsewhere. The lawsuit comes as Mayor Zohran Mamdani has sought to crack down on fees facing tenants.

Department of Consumer and Worker Protection spokesperson Milo Gringlas said the agency “is aware of consumers who have allegedly been mandated by landlords to enroll in these third-party guarantor products as a condition of renting their apartments, and we remain concerned about the potential for deception and consumer harm as a result.”

Tenant Naifa Al-Anbar said in her suit, filed last Wednesday, that she was required to pay TheGuarantors for its “rental approval service” in order to rent a Manhattan apartment in June because the landlord had an exclusive agreement with the company.

Those deals lead to “higher fees, reduced housing access, and a complete throttling of consumer choice,” her lawsuit claims

Al-Anbar’s attorney, Blake Yagman, said the rent security services are crucial for many tenants in a city where rent prices continue to surge, but that TheGuarantors agreements with landlords allow it to set prices higher than other companies and prevent tenants from shopping around for cheaper options.

“When you’re engaging in exclusive dealing, you don’t have price competition,” Yagman said, adding that tenants “don't get access to be able to shop around and use another guarantor or surety system.”

TheGuarantors’ senior vice president for sales, Jesse Schmidt, told Gothamist that year that the company had agreements with the owners and managers of about 700,000 apartments in New York City. It is not clear how many of those agreements were exclusive deals with landlords that barred tenants from using other competitor companies.

Schmidt at the time touted his company’s product as a key resource for renters who can’t otherwise meet the landlords’ income requirements or find a co-signer.

“Students, freelancers, people in between jobs, folks who are coming from overseas and don't have U.S. credit history, these are all good examples of people who really may not have had a good option before to qualify for the home that they wanted to live in,” he said

TheGuarantors did not respond to requests for comment about the new lawsuit and its claim that exclusive deals force tenants to pay higher fees.

Yagman, the attorney representing Al-Anbar in the lawsuit, said he also once used TheGuarantors to secure an apartment after graduating from law school. He said he has researched the company and found that it has increased its prices after landing more exclusive agreements with owners and managers. He said he wants a judge to ban exclusive deals and allow tenants to have access to a range of third-party guarantor services.

CoSign’s Nov. 11 lawsuit, filed in Delaware, accuses TheGuarantors of using “exclusionary contracts and smear tactics” against competitors to gain an overwhelming market share and control two of every three “rental approval services” contracts nationwide.

New York City and state officials have sought to crack down on other types of tenant fees in recent years. A suite of 2019 state tenant protection laws set limits on the amount that a landlord could charge for a security deposit and established a 14-day period for landlords to return the money, or provide formal reasons for withholding it.

Last year, the City Council approved a measure prohibiting brokers from charging tenants a fee – typically set at 15% of the annual rent – unless the tenants explicitly hired them for their services. The measure, known as the Fairness in Apartment Rental Expenses Act, was intended to limit upfront costs for renters and shift the burden to landlords.

And earlier this year, Mamdani launched a series of tenant resource fairs, known as “Rental Ripoff” hearings, with the stated goal of learning about fees tenants had to pay in addition to their rent.
