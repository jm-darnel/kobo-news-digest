Black and Hispanic Staten Islanders get nearly half of obscure pedestrian law tickets

By Ben Feuerherd · Gothamist · November 19, 2026

NYPD officers don’t issue many “pedestrians on roadways” criminal tickets. Most New Yorkers probably don’t even know that offense exists.

It criminalizes walking in the street when a sidewalk is available – an offense that sounds similar to jaywalking, which was legalized in the city in 2024 in part because lawmakers said its enforcement disproportionately targeted Black and Hispanic residents.

So far this year, officers have issued 68 criminal summonses for pedestrians on roadways, according to researchers at John Jay College of Criminal Justice. Of those, officers wrote 30 in the 120th precinct on Staten Island, all but one to Black and Hispanic people, the researchers found. The disparity has caused residents, researchers and attorneys to question if those groups are being unfairly targeted.

Gothamist has previously reported that police have disproportionately targeted residents of some city neighborhoods as part of a quality of life crackdown. Some residents and police reform advocates have criticized the NYPD and Mayor Zohran Mamdani for prioritizing such enforcement, saying it’s a waste of resources and disproportionately affects low-income people of color. Prior to his election, Mamdani campaigned on moving police away from low-level enforcement so officers are more able to focus on solving larger crimes. Earlier this month, the department issued guidance to officers instructing them not to enforce offenses including spitting and engine idling in certain circumstances.

“ That reminds me of stop and frisks,” said Asar Glenn, 45, a Staten Island resident of the sprawling Park Hill Apartments, near where officers have issued six pedestrian tickets so far this year. “ I think they just want to get IDs off of people that they stop, see if they got a record or whatever. It's like fishing.”

NYPD spokesperson Brad Weekes defended the ticketing in a statement and said the offense being enforced is not the same as jaywalking.

"These summonses were not connected to jaywalking and were the result of legitimate law enforcement activity," Weekes said.

Officers from the 120th precinct, which covers Staten Island neighborhoods including Tompkinsville, New Brighton and Stapleton, have written more than 44% of the pedestrians on roadways tickets citywide, according to the analysis by researchers at John Jay College. Of those ticketed for the offense in the precinct, 26 were Black, three were Hispanic and one person was white, the researchers said.

Officers in the precinct with the second highest number of such tickets — the 33rd precinct in Washington Heights — have issued a total of five tickets for the offense so far this year. In all of 2025, officers from the 120th precinct issued 11 of the summonses.

‘Hot Spots’

Matthew Weiss, a New York attorney who has specialized in traffic infractions for decades, said in the 30 years he has practiced law, he’s never defended a client against a pedestrians on roadways ticket.

Under the law, if a sidewalk is available, pedestrians are required to walk on it and not walk in the roadway, Weiss said. If there is no sidewalk, pedestrians are required to walk as far left as possible on the road or on the shoulder facing oncoming traffic.

“Pedestrians that receive such a ticket should argue for dismissal … if in fact they're just crossing the street, not walking along on the street,” Weiss said. “If they go to court and make that legal argument it has strong potential for being successful in my opinion.”

Michael Rempel, a researcher who worked to compile the summons analysis at John Jay College, said the infraction is strikingly similar to jaywalking.

Rempel noted that he and the other researchers identified “hot spots” within the 120th precinct where police officers wrote a disproportionate number of tickets for the offense. They include intersections near large housing complexes, including the Stapleton Houses, a NYCHA development, the Park Hill Apartments, and intersections near single-level attached homes along Jersey Street on the border of the St. George and New Brighton neighborhoods.

“They are all in urban places,” Rempel said. “It confirms that we're not dealing with really fast roads where someone walking in the road is creating a public safety danger to themselves.”

‘Tension’

Criticism of how police respond to low-level offenses in this section of Staten Island is not new. The issue exploded more than a decade ago when an NYPD officer killed 43-year-old Eric Garner with a chokehold after stopping him for apparently selling loose cigarettes.

White residents make up the largest racial group on Staten Island’s North Shore, and nearly a quarter of residents identify as Black. Asian residents and residents of other races make up another 27% of the population, according to numbers released by the State Comptroller’s Office in 2024.

“Time and time again, we’ve seen the NYPD disproportionately target Black, brown, and low-income people with low level enforcement under the guise of quality-of-life policing,” said Daniel Lambright, director of Criminal Justice Litigation at the NYCLU. “Mayor Mamdani must act now to bring an end to these racist broken windows policing tactics.”

Residents who spoke to Gothamist near the streets where the tickets were issued had a mix of opinions about the cluster of pedestrians in roadways tickets that were issued in their neighborhood.

On a recent afternoon, Mildred Cosme was walking down Jersey Street near where police officers have issued six of the pedestrian tickets so far this year.

She said seeing police makes her feel safer when she is running errands at a nearby Family Dollar or when she’s heading to the laundromat.

“I don't like to walk here around certain hours,” she said. “It concerns safety.”

Across the street from the Stapleton Houses, Steven Velez, 36, said he wished police would focus on larger crimes in the neighborhood, noting the number of walls around the NYCHA development that are covered in memorial murals in honor of residents killed in the neighborhood.

Velez recalled that police officers stopped one of his coworkers and accused him of trespassing as the man was leaving Velez’s building recently.

“You over here stopping a guy from trespassing when he wasn't trespassing,” he said. “ It's kind of stupid to be honest with you.”

Outside of the Park Hill Apartments, Glenn added that he’s concerned for young people in the neighborhood who may be stopped by police for low-level offenses.

“ There's always going to be tension between the community and the police,” he said. “The older residents might feel a little comfortable 'cause they don't come outside, they don't get hassled as much, but the younger people, they always going to feel it.”
