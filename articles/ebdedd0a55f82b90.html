Lesbian wine bars are having a moment in New York City

By Giulia Heyward · Gothamist · November 16, 2026

There's a new wine bar in Bushwick — the menu is seasonal, the mood is intimidate and the room is buzzing with the kind of conversation best held while lounging in cozy furniture under dim lighting.

Lesbians and other queer women have made Saint James Libations a go-to spot in the little over a year that it’s been open.

But this isn’t just any lesbian bar. There’s exposed brick on the walls and Lauryn Hill is playing on vinyl while friends, lovers and temporary strangers are throwing back glasses of wine in lieu of tequila shots.

“We just wanted to create something really beautiful, especially for our age and our demographic, which is 30-plus, where it’s not just all parties,” said Lola Memberr. Memberr and her partner, Akila K. Stewar,t are the Black lesbian couple behind the space. “In the queer community we love to party and stuff, but we wanted to do something a little bit more third space-y, more of a focus on wine, focus on hospitality.”

A look at the event calendar at Saint James Libations shows that there’s always something going on, from its jazz nights to its “WLW” — or women loving women – parties on Saturdays to its happy hour with $2 oysters and discounted glasses of wine.

And while the vibe at Saint James is unique, it is a part of a larger trend of wine bars, clubs and other upscale cocktail spaces that are specifically designed with the LGBTQ+ community in mind. It’s a cultural phenomenon that seems largely feasible in only a small handful of U.S. cities, according to Stephanie Bonvissuto, a women and gender studies professor at Hunter College.

“Let’s remember what a third space is, right? It’s not a working space, it’s not a home space but it’s a space for communities to come together to share ideas, to build community,” Bonvissuto said. “So New York City has a longstanding history of literally — and figuratively — making room for that.”

It wasn’t that long ago where it didn’t seem as if there was any room at all. The number of lesbian bars across the U.S. had slowly dwindled from roughly 200 in the 1980s to less than 20 in 2020, a year that saw businesses of all kinds shutter their doors en masse. By 2021, there were only three spaces dedicated to queer women left in New York City: Ginger’s Bar, in Park Slope, and Cubbyhole and Henrietta Hudson, less than a mile away from each other in the West Village.

But at least a dozen new spaces operated by queer women have opened across the U.S. in the last half decade, with a particular resurgence of post-pandemic spaces for queer women in New York City. The city now boasts three different queer women-owned sports bars — Blazer’s, Athene Keke’s and Wilka’s. There’s Ligaw, a speakeasy in the Lower East Side operated by a couple, and a self-proclaimed “dyke bar for the queers” known as The Bush in Bushwick to name a few that have set up shop in recent years.

It’s partly fueled by what Bonvissuto, who is a queer trans woman, refers to as a politically fraught period in which the rolling back of federal protections for LGBTQ+ people is heightening the urgency to find some semblance of community.

“I think that there is a strong correlation between politically repressive times and this desire for community space, the desire to see ourselves, to be around people who are speaking our language, right?” Bonvissuto said. “Part of culture is a shared vernacular, and we want to see that, hear that and be a part of that.”

After her favorite cafe-slash-bar in Ocean Hill closed, Gia Ramos was on the hunt for a space where she could be surrounded by other queer people. That’s when she heard about a Black lesbian owned wine lounge-slash-bookstore opening up in Bedford Stuyvesant.

This piqued the curiosity of Ramos, 28, who said that wine has been her de facto drink of choice as someone who was not a heavy drinker. Within months, she became a regular at Gladys Books & Wine, often swinging by multiple times a week.

“I love music, but sometimes you just want to be able to vibe, and I feel like that’s what wine bars kind of represent,” Ramos said. “It still kind of gives a vibe, but not too much anxiety.”

When Tiffany Dockery first opened Gladys, she envisioned a space that would cater to the “Black auntie aesthetic.”

“The design of the space was meant for Black women to walk in and think of that auntie who might have moved away to the big city and created a life all on her own,” she said.

And while she originally envisioned catering to a clientele of Black queer women in their late 30s to mid 40s, she's been surprised to even see some people in their twenties who have also flocked to the space as well.

“After COVID, I think a lot of us went through this reckoning with our relationship with alcohol because that’s what you did during COVID,” she said. “But now I don’t really want to have a strong drink, but I still want to have something in my hand. I think wine lends itself to a more elevated culinary experience.”

Like Saint James Libations, Gladys Books & Wine hosts events geared toward putting people in a room where they have no choice but to talk to each other, ranging from speed-dating events, wine tasting events and book discussions. Its aim is to make it very clear who the bar is interested in attracting.

“I’m very strong about us not just being lesbian-owned but a lesbian space,” Dockery said. “I think they’re a part of our history and I think we need them to continue the larger social project of liberating ourselves, which is not just at the core of being a lesbian, but also being queer.”

For Megan Utter, 32, starting a wine club gave her the opportunity to create the third space that she wanted to see.

It all started when she posted about wanting to start a wine club on Lex, an app that acts as a figurative bulletin board for people wanting to post missed connections, reselling tickets sold-out queer events or needing to borrow power tools. The response was encouraging enough for Utter to host the first event in which thirty people showed up.

Chardonnay’s premise was simple. Each month, Utter works with a wine bar to host a pop-up event for queer people — although its audience leans towards lesbian women and other sapphic wine lovers — and can essentially take over any given space to create a pop-up queer wine bar.

“I just feel like the nature of wine bars is where I feel the most comfortable because they're very approachable,” Utter said. “I love a party, but it's just hard to walk up to someone and be like, ‘Hey! What’s your name?’ And I feel like in wine bars it's quiet enough where you can, like, sit in a corner and get to know someone.”

She said half of attendees come alone and often leave with a new friend, or two.

Utter said she’s been able to build nearly her entire friend group through the wine club. A friend of hers recently started dating someone she had met at one of the Chardonngay events.

Melanie Johnson, 34, said she found out about Chardonngay two years ago while scrolling through the comments on a TikTok video about meeting other queer people in the city.

Johnson said she had already been exposed to wine tasting events while studying abroad in college, so plopping herself into a seat surrounded by other strangers wasn’t unfamiliar.

It was at the third event that Johnson said she met a couple other queer attendees who invited her to go to a couple events for Pride afterward. Those same people have formed the basis of a friend group she’s been able to sustain since then.

She’s been to roughly half a dozen Chardonngay events since — and it has continued to fuel her social calendar. She’s even run into a couple familiar faces — which has prompted a couple “I didn’t know you were also queer?!” or a “Wait! What are you also doing here?” moments.

“This year I met another girl and we've hung out a ton this summer since meeting, and I can already tell we're gonna be good friends,” Johnson said. “So it's the gift that keeps on giving, for sure.”

Back at Saint James Libations, Stewart and Memberr are already reminiscing on the completion of an opening year.

“What's kept us driving and kept us going is just the immense amount of love and support that we've been given from the community,” Stewart said. She said that patrons often tell her and Memberr that they look forward to ending up at their wine bar to cap off every single week.

It’s a sentiment that makes a lot of sense to the pair.

“I think now I just wanna hang out, listen to good music, and just be in a space where I don't feel that I'm too old to be in, that I belong there,” Memberr said. "And I think one thing we do in the queer community is we know how to party. We know how to create culture.”
