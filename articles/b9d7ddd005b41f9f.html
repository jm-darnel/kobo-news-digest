Women’s Media Group (WMG) Celebrates Marie Dutton Brown’s Contributions To Publishing And Literacy At Harlem’s Red Rooster

By Eartha Watts Hicks · Harlem World · November 15, 2026

By Eartha Watts Hicks | Harlem World Magazine

She’d described her in words I have never forgotten: “Marie is my guardian angel.” When someone is introduced that way, I immediately understand that their life’s work reaches far beyond professional accomplishments.

Marie Dutton Brown entered publishing at a time when opportunities for Black writers were limited. Her contributions began long before contemporary Black literature became a publishing trend. Her work helped bring some of the most influential voices in American literature to wider audiences, and her professional journey is closely intertwined with the evolution of Black publishing in America.

On this Tuesday afternoon, November 10, 2026, Women’s Media Group (WMG) hosted a three-course luncheon, panel discussion, and literary conversation at Harlem’s iconic Red Rooster restaurant, located at 310 Lenox Avenue. The event, A Legacy in Storytelling: Celebrating Marie Dutton Brown, gathered roughly 200 women (and a few gentlemen) in publishing, including authors, educators, industry professionals, friends, colleagues, family members, and mentees, at the Red Rooster Ginny’s Supper Club.

Throughout her career, Marie Dutton Brown recognized talent and helped writers and publishing professionals find a place where their work could be seen, valued, and accepted. The gathering in Harlem reflected that influence, not only in the number of people who attended, but in the affection and gratitude they brought into the room.

Guests enjoyed a delectable menu of Jerk Salmon Bowl with Seasonal Greens, Garlic Mash, and Chef’s dessert selection, Chocolate Mousse. Publicist Yona Deshommes, owner of Riverchild Media, opened the event with a solemn mezzo-soprano selection to honor our ancestors.

Abby West served as moderator. She is Vice President and Editorial Director of the HarperCollins imprint, Amistad, which maintains an important publishing legacy associated with the works of Zora Neale Hurston. The panel included Malaika Adero, founder of Adero’s Literary Tribe, a boutique literary agency specializing in book and author development; Christine Pride, award-winning bestselling author of We Are Not Like Them, You Were Always Mine, and All the Men I’ve Loved Again; and Anita Kopacz, a former magazine executive and award-winning author whose books include Shallow Waters, The Wind on Her Tongue, and A Taste of Honey.

Women’s Media Group President Emerita Jodi Brockington, one of the luncheon organizers, emphasized that honoring Marie Dutton Brown is about more than recognizing an extraordinary career. Brown’s accomplishments demonstrate how one determined advocate can help reshape an industry.

“Marie Dutton Brown didn’t just open doors in publishing. She made sure others could walk through them, build careers, tell their stories, and positioned them to open doors for the next generation,” Brockington stated.

Harlem itself has served as an incubator for powerful storytelling, generating a legacy of writers and artists who have understood, since the Harlem Renaissance, that telling our own stories is fundamental to preserving our history and affirming our humanity. In Harlem’s historic Sugar Hill neighborhood, Brown established her own literary agency in 1984 and became a formidable advocate.

For nearly six decades, the legendary editor, literary agent, mentor, and cultural advocate dedicated her professional life to expanding opportunities for Black and Brown writers. Her efforts helped ensure that stories reflecting the African American experience reached broader audiences, reminding us that the visibility many Black authors enjoy today was built through persistence.

Brown has worked with an extraordinary range of writers, editors, and cultural figures, including celebrated artist and author Faith Ringgold, poet and cultural historian Quincy Troupe, author and human rights advocate Randall Robinson, and cultural leader Marta Moreno Vega. Her advocacy helped many Black authors gain visibility in an industry where their work was often overlooked or undervalued.

Marie Dutton Brown’s contributions continue to shape careers and create opportunities whose full impact may never be measured. Before Black commercial fiction became a recognized market category and long before digital technologies transformed publishing, she distinguished herself as a publishing professional who helped writers develop their gifts while offering guidance and encouragement to those determined to succeed.

This work remains especially important today. Across the country, far too many Black grade-school students struggle to read at grade level. At a time when Black history faces renewed challenges and books are being removed or contested in schools and libraries, Black teachers, librarians, parents, and students need access to books that reflect Black lives, preserve Black history, and affirm their place in the world.

Yet, on a Tuesday afternoon at Ginny’s Supper Club, the room was fully booked, filled with laughter, reminiscing, and stories rooted in the long struggle to ensure Black voices are heard and Black books reach readers. Women who entered publishing fresh out of college shared memories of searching for a foothold in the industry. Writers who spent years refining manuscripts before seeing them on bookstore shelves shared their experiences, as well. A few gentlemen joined the many women in publishing who came together for the occasion.

Marie Dutton Brown was previously awarded Women’s Media Group’s Media Trailblazer Award. Despite important advances in representation, access to publishing resources, professional networks, and marketing opportunities, ensuring sustainable literary careers remains an ongoing concern. The organization is now launching The Marie Dutton Brown Legacy Series to bring together influential creators, leading voices, and visionary thinkers to celebrate literature, encourage meaningful professional connections, and advance a more equitable publishing industry through programs designed to help bridge those gaps.

As memories and reflections filled Red Rooster, one fact became unmistakable: Marie Dutton Brown’s contributions extend far beyond the titles she helped bring into print or the careers she helped launch. They live on through the people she encouraged and the opportunities she created for future generations. For that, in the middle of a busy Tuesday, her community enthusiastically gathered to say thank you.

For independent media organizations such as Harlem World Magazine, whose mission includes documenting the people, institutions, history, and cultural achievements of Harlem, Brown’s example reinforces the importance of community-centered storytelling. Her career reminds us that the responsibility to preserve our narratives cannot rest solely with major publishing houses or established cultural institutions.

By establishing an ongoing initiative in Brown’s name, Women’s Media Group is helping carry forward the principles that have guided her work. This event was sponsored by Penguin Random House, Hay House, Elevations Travel, and Knashi, with special thanks to Red Rooster Harlem.
