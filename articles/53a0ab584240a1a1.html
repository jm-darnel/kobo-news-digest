1 in 8 NYC public school students were homeless last year

By Jillian Jorgensen · NY1 · November 10, 2026

One in eight New York City public school students was homeless at some point during the last school year.

That amounts to 138,000 students — a figure that is down from about 154,000 the year before.

“The number remains incredibly high, and much higher than it was ten years ago. So this is not, you know, listen, I hope it keeps ongoing in that trajectory. But this is not something like the city has solved family homelessness by any stretch of the imagination,” Jennifer Pringle, the project director for learners in temporary housing at the organization Advocates for Children, said.

It is the eleventh year in a row that more than 100,000 public school students across the five boroughs were homeless.

Of those children, 50,700 spent time in a shelter; another 81,100 were doubled up — sharing someone else’s housing after losing their own. The remainder were in other forms of temporary housing, according to Advocates for Children, which compiles the information each year.

“The data doesn’t tell us where the students went from the previous year. So it’s hard to say exactly why, but there was the largest decrease in the number of students in [shelters],” Pringle said.

In a statement, Schools Chancellor Kamar Samuels says he is working with partners in other city agencies and advocacy groups to ensure students have educational stability.

“Supporting these students is both a priority and shared responsibility, and over the last few years we’ve worked with sister agencies to adjust policies so they are better for families — from eliminating the requirement that children must attend all housing meetings to standing up community schools in districts with high concentrations of shelters,” he said. “As this report shows, this work is far from over, and we’ll continue to work in partnership to bolster resources, address ongoing challenges, and ensure that our most vulnerable students have the support they need to receive a rigorous, high-quality education.”

According to data, students who are homeless, especially those in shelters, have worse outcomes in school than their peers with stable housing. In the 2024-2025 school year, more than 60% of students in shelters were chronically absent. One in five transferred schools mid-year.

“That kind of instability is not surprising when you have 37% of families placed in shelter in a different borough from where the kids go to school,” Pringle said.

“If an appropriate unit became available closer to the school right now, there’s no way to flag those families and ask the family, ‘hey, we found a unit that’s closer to your school, are you interested in a different shelter placement?’ Which is really a lost opportunity for everybody,” Pringle said.

Add in the city’s troubled student transportation system, and students in shelter often struggle to get to their original schools — facing very long yellow school bus rides, once they actually get assigned to a school bus route.

“If everything works perfectly — and I mean everything works perfectly — you’re looking, generally, a minimum of a month from the time you enter shelter to the time you get a bus to your school of origin. That is way too long for a child to wait,” Pringle said.
