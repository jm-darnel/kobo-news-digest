New Urban Civil Rights Museum Opens In Harlem, Spotlighting The North’s Untold History

By Ann Brown · Harlem World · November 9, 2026

By HWM

Harlem is adding another important cultural destination to its historic landscape with the opening of the Urban Civil Rights Museum, a new institution dedicated to exploring the often-overlooked history of the Civil Rights Movement in the northern United States.

Established by the National Urban League, the museum is located inside the organization’s Manhattan headquarters and officially opens Nov. 14. Spanning nearly 12,000 square feet, the museum features permanent and rotating exhibitions examining the people, movements, and cultural contributions that helped shape the fight for racial equality in the Northeast and beyond.

Never miss new stories in Harlem

“We want people to know that they are key participants,” said Jennifer Scott, the museum’s founding executive director and chief curator, during a preview with The Associated Press. Scott, a public history scholar with extensive experience preserving African-American history, led the development of the museum’s permanent installation and inaugural exhibitions.

Visitors will encounter multimedia presentations covering centuries of history, beginning with the transatlantic slave trade and continuing through the first and second waves of the Great Migration. Exhibitions also explore the Civil Rights Movement and its continuing influence on contemporary society.

Through photographs, audio recordings, speeches, oral histories and interactive displays, the museum encourages visitors to connect historical events with present-day issues. Feedback walls invite guests to share their own experiences, including participation in protests and reading banned books.

National Urban League President and CEO Marc Morial said locating the museum in Harlem was intentional. Situated near iconic cultural institutions such as the Apollo Theater and the Schomburg Center for Research in Black Culture, the museum joins a neighborhood deeply connected to African-American history, activism, and artistic expression.

Morial said the institution will preserve the stories of the people who fought for equal opportunity, including individuals and communities from diverse backgrounds who stood alongside African Americans in the struggle for civil rights.

For Harlem, the Urban Civil Rights Museum represents more than a new cultural attraction. It is an opportunity to recognize the region’s contributions to the freedom struggle and encourage future generations to understand their role in shaping history.

As Scott emphasized, history is not finished. The museum’s work, she said, is “just getting started.”
