Yankees Birthday of the Day: Joe DiMaggio

By Kevin Winterhalt · Pinstripe Alley · November 25, 2026

Today marks the birthday of one of baseball’s most famed historical figures. If all Joe DiMaggio had ever done was put together a 56-game hit streak and marry Marilyn Monroe, his place in the sporting zeitgeist would be assured. But there was obviously more to the life and career of the man christened as “The World’s Greatest Living Ballplayer” in 1967.

With that fame comes a nearly infinite number of articles and musings on DiMaggio, including the one I authored as part of PSA’s Top 100 Yankees of All-Time . He is the baseball version of the U.S. Civil War. Far in the past but endlessly fascinating. Rather than re-hash his biography or look at his streak 75 years later, I would like to celebrate The Yankee Clipper’s birthday with another important moment in his legendary career. It seems apropos, considering we are a week away from the owners locking out the players in an endless pursuit of a salary cap for reasons of franchise valua…. er, competitive balance, apparently.

Author’s Note: Though I have not included individual citations, all contemporaneous references are thanks to The New York Times and its TimesMachine archive.

Joseph Paul DiMaggio Born : November 25, 1914 (Martinez, CA) Died : March 8, 1999 (Hollywood, FL) Yankees Tenure : 1936-1951

If you’d rather read about DiMaggio’s streak or a condensed bio of his spectacular career on his birthday, go ahead. I will not begrudge you. Both subjects fascinate me. DiMaggio is easily my favorite historical Yankee.

I am picking up his story after the 1937 season. DiMaggio had just authored a phenomenal sophomore campaign, one of the greatest initial encores in Yankee history. A .346 average, 46 HR (Joe D once remarked he could have hit 70 that season if not for Yankee Stadium’s cavernous dimensions), 167 RBI, and AL-leading .673 SLG. You pick your stat. He finished as the runner-up for the AL MVP, behind Charlie Gehringer of the Detroit Tigers.

He also felt like he deserved a pay increase. Decades before players achieved free agency, in Joe D’s time the Reserve Clause tethered players to their clubs until the team decided to trade them or let them go.

DiMaggio had angled for a raise after his rookie season. Though the historical sources are uncertain on the exact number, he signed for either $15,000 or $17,000. But after an historically great second season, he decided to go back to the well and ask for another pay increase.

Storm clouds appeared in early January. On the 7 th , the New York Times reported that a salary war might be brewing. “Word was received here that Joe DiMaggio was beginning to formulate a pretty good idea of his worth,” John Drebinger wrote. “He [DiMaggio] intimated that he doubted whether even the inviting sum of $25,000, if offered, would be sufficient.”

For the Yankees, who had gone through contentious negotiations (or as contentious as they could be when one party held virtually all the leverage) the offseason before with Red Ruffing, Lefty Gomez, and Lou Gehrig, this had to be nightmare fuel. If they caved to DiMaggio, who else would demand more money?

A week later, it looked like there was reason for optimism. Or, a cynic might suggest, a clever PR ploy was afoot. Yankees general manager Ed Barrow announced that the club was offering significant raises to its key players, including DiMaggio, and they expected the players to be satisfied. Notably, he declined to specify how much the club was offering.

By late January, the Yanks’ exact offer to DiMaggio was public. After the superstar met with Colonel Jacob Ruppert, the Yankee owner, the latter told the Times the club was offering DiMaggio a $10,000 raise. “I think you’d be satisfied with it,” Ruppert told the reporter.

I am sure he would be satisfied with that salary. Too bad the reporter was not a preternaturally talented athlete and one of the great baseball players of all times. In case you were wondering whether baseball owners have always tried to manipulate public opinion against the players, it has always been a thing. Not new.

By mid-March, it was ugly. On the 13 th , a reporter asked Ruppert about DiMaggio and his contract. In a tirade that would make Randy Levine proud, the Yankee owner went off on his superstar.

“DiMaggio is an ungrateful young man, who is very unfair to his team-mates, to say the least. He wants $40,000 and I’ve offered him $25,000 and he won’t get a button over that amount. Why, how many men his age earn that much? As far as I’m concerned that’s all he’s worth to the ballclub and if he doesn’t sign we’ll win the pennant without him. DiMaggio should have reported to Joe McCarthy by now. Instead, he’s still skulking in San Francisco, carrying around in his pocket the contract we sent him three weeks ago.”

Thanks, Colonel. I’d much rather you pocket the $15,000. You’re the reason I go to the games, after all. DiMaggi-who? To Joe’s credit, he hit back a couple of weeks later. When asked about Ruppert’s statement that the Yanks would be fine without their superstar outfielder, DiMaggio responded: “The contract for $25,000 they sent me is gone with the wind. Just say I’ve lost it. They’re going to pay my price or else.”

With no leverage other than sitting out the 1938 season, in mid-April DiMaggio caved and accepted the Yankees’ $25,000 offer. Yankees fans, in a breathtakingly misplaced display of their loyalty, booed DiMaggio when he returned to the field. The Yankee Clipper, sportswriter Joe Posnanski remarked decades later, never forgot it.

I am not trying to make DiMaggio out as an early hero in the long history of player versus owners in baseball. I have never seen anything to suggest he was trying to unionize players, or that he was worried about anyone’s salary but his own.

But with a lockout looming next week and the MLB PR machine in full gaslighting mode, the birthday of one of the greatest players in the history of America’s pastime seems like a fitting time to remember that though the names and faces change, the fight remains. If the MLBPA sticks to its guns, I will not boo the players when they eventually take the field again.

I will give them a standing ovation. The same way I applaud DiMaggio sticking up for himself 88 years ago. Happy 112 th birthday, Joltin’ Joe. You are still my favorite.
