In Harlem, The Trade War Reaches The Dance Floor

Harlem World · November 23, 2026

By HWM

For Harlem, the fallout from President Donald Trump’s tariffs is not just a story about imported goods. It is also about people deciding not to come.

A striking example emerged in 2025, when organizers postponed the world finals of the International Lindy Hop Championships planned for the neighborhood after international dancers began withdrawing, saying they felt unwelcome in the United States. The Associated Press reported that tariffs, immigration crackdowns and Trump’s rhetoric toward other countries were contributing to that broader chill in international travel.

The reporting did not quantify how much Harlem businesses lost from the postponed competition, but the episode illustrates how a dispute over trade can reach a neighborhood’s cultural economy.

A trade dispute becomes a travel decision

Tariffs are only part of the explanation. Tourism experts interviewed by the AP and Gothamist described a combination of trade tensions, political uncertainty, rising travel costs and anxiety about treatment at the U.S. border. That distinction matters: the available evidence does not show that tariffs alone caused Harlem’s tourism difficulties. Instead, they have become part of a wider political message that some prospective visitors interpret as hostility toward foreigners. In the case of the Harlem dance competition, organizers specifically described participants feeling unwelcome.

Elsewhere in New York City, the financial consequences were already visible. In August 2025, Matt Levy, who operates the Queens-based company Spread Love Tours, told Gothamist that Canadian school groups normally brought his business about $35,000 annually. He expected that revenue to fall to $5,000 that year. With fewer bookings, he said, he was less likely to hire freelance guides and more likely to lead tours himself. His experience was not a Harlem-specific measure, but it showed how lost international visits could translate into less work for local tourism businesses.

Fewer overseas visitors, but not a citywide collapse

Visitor spending also increased overall, reaching $55.6 billion. Those results make it inaccurate to describe New York’s tourism economy as collapsing. But they do not erase the importance of the international decline: overseas travelers accounted for roughly half of the city’s tourism spending, according to the tourism organization. A business dependent on foreign tour groups or international events can struggle even while the city’s overall numbers improve.

“…every Harlem restaurant, shop or cultural venue experienced the same downturn.”

An invitation competing with Washington’s message

New York’s tourism organization responded to the international slowdown with a campaign called “With Love + Liberty, New York City,” emphasizing the Statue of Liberty and the city’s welcoming identity. “Our welcoming spirit will not waver,” tourism chief Julie Coker said in a statement reported by Gothamist.

In March 2026, the organization forecast that international visitation would recover to 12.9 million during the year. That was a projection, not a completed-year result, and it offered no guarantee that individual neighborhoods or businesses would recover at the same pace.

For Harlem, the postponed championship captures the central tension: a neighborhood can offer an international cultural gathering, but it cannot control the national climate in which visitors decide whether to attend. Trump’s tariffs are part of that climate—not the sole cause of the tourism slowdown, but one element of a broader confrontation whose consequences have reached a Harlem dance floor.
