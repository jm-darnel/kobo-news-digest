Yankees Birthday of the Day: Gary Sheffield

By John Griffin · Pinstripe Alley · November 18, 2026

Even in his prime, Gary Sheffield, signed as a free agent during the 2003-2004 offseason, was not quite the same physical specimen that the Yankees’ current pair of power-hitting right fielders, Aaron Judge and Giancarlo Stanton, are. Baseball-Reference lists the latter two at 6-foot-7, 282-pounds and 6-foot-5, 245-pounds, respectively, while Sheffield checks in at a “measly” 5-foot-11, 190-pounds. And yet, much like Judge and Stanton, you had to pay attention every time Sheffield came to the plate — and if you were in the ballpark, for your own safety as well. While we, alas, do not have Statcast data from the mid-2000s, third base coach Luis Sojo’s tendency to stand everywhere but the coach’s box with Sheffield at the plate is a testament to the violent way the ball rocketed off his bat.

Gary Antonion Sheffield Born: November 18, 1968 (Tampa, Florida) Yankees Tenure: 2004-06

Born and raised in Tampa, Florida, Sheffield came from a family of New York baseball royalty; his uncle is Doc Gooden, who spent time with both the Yankees and Mets and who celebrated his own birthday back on Monday . From his youth, he excelled on the diamond, being named to a star-studded Belmont Heights Little League All-Star team that went to the Little League World Series finals in 1980. In high school, he was both a third baseman and a pitcher, hitting 15 home runs and hitting the upper 80s with his fastball en route to being named the Gatorade National Player of the Year.

Selected with the sixth overall pick of the 1986 MLB Draft, Sheffield opted to forego college — he had a commitment to play for the Miami Hurricanes — to begin his professional career in the Milwaukee Brewers organization. That would turn out to be the right call, as just two years later, in his age-19 season, Sheffield got the call to The Show as part of September call-ups. He would go on to spend the next three seasons in Milwaukee, but unfortunately for everyone involved, it did not quite go according to anyone’s plan. Initially brought up as a shortstop, he was not very good defensively, and was ultimately moved over to third base — a decision he criticized at the time. Eventually, the Brewers had enough and traded him to the San Diego Padres, a move that Sheffield had been angling for; he admitted later that he had deliberately committed errors in the field in order to force a trade.

That combination of production and headache would become the defining feature of Sheffield’s career, and part of why he would ultimately become one of the rarest of players: an All-Star journeyman. He put together a breakout campaign in his first season in San Diego, posting a 168 OPS+ with 33 homers and 34 doubles while winning the batting title. The following year, he was traded to the Florida Marlins for a package that included future Hall of Fame closer Trevor Hoffman.

Sheffield would go on to spend parts of six seasons with the Marlins, more than any other team he would play for. During that time, he posted a .288/.426/.543 slash line, swapped his infielder’s glove for an outfielder’s one and became a right fielder full-time, went to two All-Star Games, signed a pair of contract extensions — a a four-year, $22.5 million deal in 1993, and a six-year, $61 million extension in 1997 — and helped lead Florida to their first World Series win in franchise history.

And so, naturally, what do the Marlins do with arguably their first real star? They trade him, just like they traded Kevin Brown and Moises Alou, as they committed to blowing up their team. And so Sheffield ended up in Los Angeles, acquired in exchange for Mike Piazza (who himself was a Marlin for just a week) that was allegedly part of TV rights negotiations. He spent three and a half seasons in LA, posting a .312/.424/.573 slash line, but much like he did with the Brewers, Sheffield eventually burned bridges. This time, the right fielder complained about his contract, the organization’s decisions, his teammates, his coaches, and pretty much everything else under the sun, until eventually the Dodgers traded him to the Braves.

Sheffield remained a dominant hitter with Atlanta, slashing .319/.412/.562 across the final two seasons of the extension he had signed three teams ago in 1997. A free agent for the first time in his life ahead of his age-35 season, he inked a three-year, $39 million contract with George Steinbrenner, who overruled Brian Cashman’s deal with the younger Vladimir Guerrero.

History can debate whether or not Steinbrenner should have ignored nostalgia and let Cashman make the decisions, but for his first season in pinstripes, Sheffield was certainly worth the price (even if he did finish as the runner-up to Guerrero for the AL MVP). In a lineup that included Derek Jeter, Jason Giambi, Alex Rodriguez, Bernie Williams, Hideki Matsui, and Jorge Posada, it was Sheffield who was the anchor of the lineup, leading the team in OPS+ (141), home runs (36), and runs (117) as he was shuffled between the third, fourth, and fifth spots in the order. His dominant performance continued in the postseason, too, as he went 14-for-48 with two homers and seven RBI; unfortunately, he got cold at just the wrong time, with just one hit in 17 plate appearances across the final four games of the ALCS we don’t discuss.

Sheffield’s second season in the Bronx was largely still excellent, but the cracks were beginning to show. His overall numbers were only slightly worse than the previous year, as he slashed .291/.379/.512 (a 137 OPS+). He struggled defensively, however, and failed to show up in October. More significantly, he got into an altercation with a fan at Fenway in April, and a magazine article saw him criticize his teammates, as the previous cycle of Sheffield continued.

After his Yankee career ended, Sheffield played for three more seasons, spending two years in Detroit and one in Queens. He remained a powerhouse at the plate, slashing .253/.358/.437 and totaling 54 home runs (bringing him up to a career total of 509) as a part-time designated hitter and, when with the Mets, pinch hitter. He spent the 2010 season out of baseball, and after flirting with a comeback, officially retired ahead of the 2011 campaign.

Two decades after the end of his Yankees career, Sheffield remains a rather controversial figure in the Yankees universe. He was a fun player to watch, with a batted ball profile that would have been fascinating in the Statcast era. At the same time, though, he was part of some of the most dysfunctional and poorly constructed Yankees rosters, and may or may not have been one of the reasons the clubhouse dynamic was so awful after his departure that the Yankees sought to sign CC Sabathia for his leadership qualities just as much as his dominant left arm.
