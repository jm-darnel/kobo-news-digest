Group looking to open NYC's primaries tries again, over Mamdani's pushback

By Brigid Bergin · Gothamist · November 20, 2026

A group seeking to end New York City’s closed primaries filed a new batch of 75,000 petitions with the City Clerk this week, marking its latest push to open city primaries to voters of all parties.

New York City is famously blue, with registered Democrats outnumbering Republicans six to one. So the city’s Democratic primaries tend to be the unofficial general elections for many candidates, cutting out a wide swath of voters who are not registered Democrats.

But they have a big opponent in Mayor Zohran Mamdani, who has said he thinks the city’s current primary system is working well. He won his party’s nod last June with 56% of the vote, beating former Gov. Andrew Cuomo by nearly 13 percentage points.

Their proposal would make the June primary a preliminary election, open to all registered voters, who would then use ranked-choice voting to pick up to five candidates in order of preference. The top three finishers would advance to a final election in November that would also use ranked-choice voting.

“ Voters who pay taxes should participate in all elections, period,” said Anthony Thomas, the executive director of Unite NY. “And right now, we've got 1.1 million and growing New Yorkers who can't participate,” he said, a reference to the number of registered voters who are not affiliated with a political party. Those voters are referred to by the Board of Elections as “blanks.”

Proposals to reform the city’s partisan election system have been debated for decades, particularly among the city’s independent voters. And as the ranks of unaffiliated city voters continue to swell, good government groups have also voiced support for opening primaries to all registered voters.

But the Mamdani administration, which was swept into office with record-high voter turnout, has remained opposed to these changes. This is Unite NY’s second attempt this year to force a question on the ballot about opening up all city elections to unaffiliated voters.

Typically, voters weigh in on ballot questions set by a charter revision commission, made up of appointees by the mayor or the City Council. Mamdani appointed a commission earlier this year that ended up putting five ballot questions before voters. Voters adopted all five earlier this month.

But another way for New Yorkers to get a question on the ballot is to gather thousands of signatures from registered voters here in the city.

Last July, Unite NY filed more than 45,000 petitions trying to force a question onto the ballot about the city’s election system. A month later, the city clerk tossed the group’s first set of petitions after the Board of Elections said they contained an insufficient number of valid signatures. Unite NY filed a lawsuit over the decision and asked the court to order the board to review the signatures line by line. The parties were last in court in October for oral arguments.

A spokesman for the city Law Department said it was still awaiting a decision in that case. The city clerk did not respond to a request for comment on the new batch of petitions. The Board of Elections declined to comment, citing the ongoing litigation.

Unite NY is a nonpartisan political organization that has been advocating for election reforms statewide for nearly a decade. The group was founded by Martin Babinec, a technology entrepreneur and venture capitalist who lives in the small city of Little Falls in Central New York, more than 200 miles from New York City. Voting records indicate Babinec, who is registered as unaffiliated with a party, spent hundreds of thousands of his own dollars on the cause.

Government reform advocates in the city say Unite NY is ultimately fighting to strengthen the city’s democracy.

“Unaffiliated voters are actually the fastest-growing segment of the electorate,” said Ben Weinberg, director of Public Policy at Citizens Union, which supports open primaries. “They're young. They're diverse. They're politically engaged. They want to vote in the primaries, but under the current system, we are not letting them be part of our local democracy.”

He pointed to recent voter analysis from the New York City Campaign Finance Board that found that voters under 30 made up nearly a quarter of the unaffiliated voters, which means those voters could not participate in the primary election.

Reinvent Albany, another nonpartisan nonprofit focused on government reform, found New York City is an outlier among the 100 biggest cities when it comes to holding closed primaries for its municipal elections. Only six big cities — Buffalo, Philadelphia, Pittsburgh, Charlotte, Baltimore and New York City — hold closed primaries.

Then-Mayor Eric Adams, who was the first sitting mayor in modern New York City history to be criminally indicted, also appeared on the ballot on two independent lines. A Gothamist investigation found fake and fraudulently obtained signatures on his petitions.

Cuomo won 41.3% of the final vote, to Mamdani’s 50.8%. Adams, who suspended his campaign just weeks before the election, won only 3%.

When asked for comment, mayoral spokesperson Dora Pekec referred to remarks Mamdani made over the summer shortly after Unite NY filed its first set of petitions seeking to force a ballot question. At the time, he was asked whether he thought this was an effort to keep a candidate like him from winning a primary.

“I am quite content with the system of primaries that we have thus far,” Mamdani said in July. “I think that we've seen it's a system that's been in effect for quite some time. And it's also a system that doesn't preclude greater participation.”
