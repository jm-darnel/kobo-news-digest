These New Yorkers ditched their smartphones for a month. No, seriously.

By Willow Higgins · Gothamist · November 23, 2026

In an event space atop a warehouse in East Williamsburg, people lined up to check their phones at the door.

Being phone-free for the night was a requirement for those who wanted to party with the roughly 100 New Yorkers who had just given up their smartphones for a month. Friends and families were invited to celebrate along with them — and see a showcase of hobbies and art projects that were picked up during the month instead of screens.

In one corner of the dimly lit room, people practiced their yo-yo skills. Someone else passed out bowls of homemade chicken soup. Others, lubricated by Modelos and boxed wine, made artsy stickers formed from pictures of a black and white cat. But the main attraction was the dance floor, which thudded to the beat of a DJ, who spun actual vinyl.

“Free your phone and ur a-- will follow,” the party invite advertised.

Peabody said she was initially surprised that demand was so high in a city as plugged in as New York. But, it makes sense, she later concluded: Being on your phone all the time is isolating.

“People need this space to not feel alone,” she said.

During the October effort, phone-free crews met weekly in small groups, or pods, based on where they live. The idea, Peabody explained, is that as New Yorkers detox from their smartphones, they can lean on their neighbors and get to know each other in the process.

Groups congregated across boroughs, in neighborhoods like the East Village, Astoria and Fort Greene.

“It's kinda like Alcoholics Anonymous for smartphones and social media,” said Axel Berlin, who participated in the Month Offline challenge in Chelsea.

‘People do feel overwhelmed’

Caitlin Begg studies movements like these for a living.

According to survey results Begg recently presented at a Princeton University TEDx Talk, the average person uses eight different methods of communication daily, including social media, Slack and other apps. More than 90% of respondents reported checking their smartphones within 15 minutes of waking up.

“People do feel overwhelmed,” Begg said. “That’s just a lot.”

Taking a break from your phone in hyperlocal groups, Begg said, makes particular sense in a city like New York.

“ We live in such an incredibly vibrant city,” Begg said, “and because of work constraints or just being in the routine of life, sometimes people are just going from place to place… you know, a slop bowl place and back to their apartment.”

She added, getting off your phone is “about pushing people past their comfort zone, and I think pods really encourage that pro-social behavior.”

Joining the the 'flipside' scene

On a balmy evening earlier this fall, dozens of New Yorkers congregated in the plaza of Tompkins Square Park to kick off their detoxes.

(The phones are provided by dumb.co, a flip phone company that some of the Month Offline organizers started after the Month Offline challenge took off. For those looking for a permanent switch, the $20 phone is also available for purchase – and advertised on flyers across New York City.)

“Building the capacity for technological resistance on literally the neighborhood level, I think is the most important place to build it,” said Damian Thomas, who led the Astoria pod and is the director of the Unplatform Project, a tech literacy initiative to get people to divest from big tech.

In Tompkins Square, under the park’s street lights, the facilitators of each neighborhood pod met with those who signed up to participate. Throughout the course of October, the leaders would host pods in their living rooms, bookstores or other neighborhood hangouts.

But pod facilitators told Gothamist that the weekly meetings are run with their own flair.

Thomas, whose group met weekly at the Museum of the Moving Image in Astoria, said at one meeting that he assigned his group to find a new movie, show or book without recommendations from social media. During another week, everyone had to find a new spot in their neighborhood without the help of Google Maps.

Quinn Winters, who helped lead the Greenpoint pod, said he realized that New York seems smaller when you’re not staring down at a screen all the time.

“Now I'm seeing the same people over and over again, and I'm actually able to wave and say hi,” Winters said.

But being without a smartphone is a unique challenge in a city that runs on apps like Google Maps, Apple Pay and DoorDash.

“Someone pointed out very reasonably like, ‘Oh, I use the Citi Bike to get around all the time, and I realized that I didn't have a smartphone on me, so I couldn't check one out,’” Thomas said.

He added, “People rely on the Citi Bike system to get around. That's a way that our public infrastructure now is locking people into the usage of these devices.”

Some people who signed up for the challenge didn’t complete it.

Facilitators of several pods told Gothamist that attendance to the weekly meetings was lower than they hoped — if there were 10 people in a pod, sometimes only six would show up.

Berlin, from the Chelsea pod, told Gothamist that, frankly, unplugging is hard.

“I think it's very likely that people, people got dragged back into their smartphone lifestyle,” he said.

Begg, the researcher, said giving up your smartphone isn’t for everyone; being able to do so is a privilege in modern society, and not everyone’s life allows the break.

“[Throwing] your phone in the river … from an economic standpoint, it's just not realistic for most people,” Begg said. “They have to support their families. There's childcare and things and other things that are wrapped up in the smartphone.”

Short of ditching their phone altogether, Begg said, there are other things people can do to set boundaries with their devices, like sleeping with their phones outside of their bedrooms or switching back to landlines at home.

Some participants said flip phones have everything they need, especially because they now come equipped with basic navigation systems. Others told Gothamist that being without smartphones isn’t sustainable long term, but they want to have new boundaries, like only taking them out to FaceTime with family.

At the East Williamsburg party, the white brick walls were decorated with an alternative to doom scrolling on social media: actual scrolls, made from butcher paper, with musings and reflections from each pod scribbled in crayon and markers.

On a neighboring wall, the group’s constitution, written boldly in Sharpie, was on display.

“We the participants of [Month Offline] declare that we ought to be free and independent human beings, that we are absolved of all dependence on big tech and other attention grabbing algorithms,” the butcher paper read. “Here is to boredom, wrong turns, to fruitful friction, to life beyond the screen.”

Winters, from the Greenpoint pod, stood beside an art installation where people wrote their secrets on slips of paper that would later be set on fire.

He said being off his phone frees up a part of his brain. Some people write books during their phone-free month, he said. Others simply choose to spend more time with their friends and families — or, in this case, their neighbors.

“More and more, I'm realizing the isolating part of New York is really a choice,” Winters said. “You can isolate yourself, but you can also choose to engage with the people nearby you."

Winters said taking a break from his phone has changed the way he moves through a city — even as those around him are glued to their screens. “Every time that someone else is pulling out their phone to check their emails, I'm looking around and seeing what's in the sky,” he said.
