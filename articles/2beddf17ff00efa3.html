Yankees Birthday of the Day: Ownie Carroll

By Peter Brody · Pinstripe Alley · November 11, 2026

For every legendary name recognizable across generations of Yankees fans, there are dozens others who fade from memory after leaving the organization. Thousands of players have come and gone through the doors of Yankee Stadium in the team’s 123-year history, the vast majority leaving just a faint mark in the annals of the franchise. Even during their most storied era in the Murderers’ Row years, far from every player ascended to the status of Babe Ruth and Lou Gehrig

Owen Thomas “Ownie” Carroll Born: November 11, 1902 (Kearney, NJ) Died: June 8, 1975 (Orange, NJ) Yankees Tenure : 1930

Born just months before the inception of the New York Highlanders (who would become the Yankees a decade later), not much is known about Carroll’s family and early life aside from a few facts about his New Jersey upbringing. He emerged as one of the top high school pitchers of the early 1920s in his four years at Saint Benedict’s Preparatory School in Newark, compiling a 49-2 record.

His legend grew during his four years on the varsity squad at the College of the Holy Cross in Worcester, MA, the 1920s sometimes considered the heyday of college baseball. He was widely regarded as the best college pitcher in the country, amassing a 50-2 record, his only losses coming against Princeton and Boston College in his sophomore season. Games in which he pitched would often draw 30,000 or more fans at Braves Field in Boston, his most memorable performances including a 15-inning complete game against Harvard and a 17-strikeout one-hitter against Princeton.

Following his senior year, Carroll signed with the Tigers for $25,000 in 1925 and made his MLB debut later that season, going six innings and allowing eight runs (four earned) on nine hits and four walks in an 11-9 win over the Red Sox on June 20th. Carroll would make ten appearances (four starts) in his rookie season, going 2-2 with a 3.76 ERA and 12 strikeouts in 40.2 innings. Arm issues that would plague his professional career began to appear following that season, causing Carroll to spend the entirety of the 1926 season in the minor leagues with the Toronto Maple Leads.

Carroll returned to the majors in 1927, firmly establishing himself as a big leaguer. That year, he made 31 appearances (15 starts), going 10-6 with a 3.98 ERA and 41 strikeouts in 172 innings. His best season came in 1928, Carroll going 16-12 in 34 appearances (28 starts) totaling 231 innings while finishing in the top-10 in the AL in ERA (3.27) and complete games (19). He followed that with a less impressive 1929 campaign, compiling a 9-17 record in 34 appearances (26 starts), with a 4.63 ERA and 54 strikeouts in 202 innings.

1930 was a year of upheaval for Carroll, the righty pitching six games for the Tigers before being traded to the Yankees for rotation stalwart Waite Hoyt . That swap proved to be disastrous for the Bronx outfit, as Carroll made just 10 appearances in pinstripes. He allowed four runs on six hits and three walks in his team debut — a four-inning start that ended in a 13-7 loss to his former team on June 11th. Carroll was immediately removed from the rotation and would make nine further relief appearances, only one of those games ending in a Yankees win.

Despite stratospheric seasons from Ruth and Gehrig, the Yankees were seriously light in the rotation following Hoyt’s departure and missed the playoffs after finishing third. Carroll would not be allowed to see out the end of the season in New York. The Yankees sold him to the Reds at the end of the summer. In total, Carroll went 0-1 in those ten appearances, with a 6.61 ERA and eight strikeouts in 32.2 innings. His -0.3 fWAR and -0.7 rWAR were among the worst for anyone who pitched for the Yankees that season, Ruth himself accruing more value by spinning a two-run complete game against the Red Sox on the final day of the season — the Bambino’s first pitching appearance in nine years.

Carroll regained his starting pitching role in Cincinnati, going 3-9 in 12 starts with a 5.53 ERA and 24 strikeouts in 107.1 innings in 1931. 1932 saw him make 32 appearances (26 starts), Carroll improving to a 4.50 ERA, though he led the NL in losses (19) and hit batsmen (nine). That precipitated a trade to the Cardinals following the completion of the season, Jim Bottomley heading to Cincy to complete the deal. However, St. Louis flipped him just a few weeks later to the Dodgers for Dazzy Vance. With this trade, Carroll earned the distinction as the only player in MLB history to be traded for three future Hall of Famers in Hoyte, Bottomley, and Vance, a record that still stands to this day.

Following his retirement, Carroll went on to become the head coach of the Seton Hall University baseball team for 25 years. He leaned on advice learned while playing under future Yankees Hall of Fame manager Casey Stengel, back then the manager of the Toledo Mud Hens of the American Association in 1930. He finished with a 341‐186‐3 record, leading the Pirates to four Metropolitan Conference championships and two College World Series appearances.

On April 25, 1973, the university honored their long-time coach by renaming their stadium the Owen T. Carroll Field. Carroll himself passed away on June 8, 1975, at the age of 72.

Carroll’s Yankees career may be a minor footnote in history of the franchise, but there is no doubting the impact he had on the lives and careers of the many young ballplayers who came under his leadership at Seton Hall.
