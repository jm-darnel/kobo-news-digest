Every day is Turkey Day in Battery Park for fans of Astoria the bird

By Ben Feuerherd · Gothamist · November 26, 2026

Stories about New York wildlife can captivate even the city’s most hardened residents. Tales of a Bengal tiger raised in a Harlem apartment and a rat that dragged a slice of pizza down a subway staircase have become part of the city’s folklore.

At Battery Park in Lower Manhattan, another wild animal has charmed New Yorkers. Her name is Astoria, and she is a wild turkey who, according to birdwatchers and fans, was first spotted in her namesake Queens neighborhood, spent a formative year on Roosevelt Island and has now settled in the bustling downtown business district.

“I love it. She brings me so much joy,” said Lauren McPhate, a local resident and partner at a nearby wine merchants. “I guess it’s because it’s so unexpected. You don’t expect to see a turkey in the middle of the city and she’s just kind of doing her thing.”

Wild turkeys have nested and roamed in parks across the five boroughs for decades. Hunters nearly wiped them out of the Northeast United States in the 1800s, according to Jessica Wilson, the executive director of NYC Bird Alliance. Today, hundreds of turkeys are currently settled in parks in the Bronx and on Staten Island, where they’ve occasionally made headlines for blocking traffic. But as far as birdwatchers are aware, Astoria is the only turkey who currently calls Manhattan home.

“She rests at night in the trees in Battery Park. And as long as folks leave her alone, give her a lot of space, she's really happy and successful there,” Wilson said of the wild bird.

From Queens to Manhattan

New Yorkers first noticed Astoria in Western Queens in the spring of 2024, according to the NYC Bird Alliance, a nonprofit that works to protect the city's birds and their habitats. Like many New Yorkers, she moved frequently before finding suitable digs.

In the spring of 2024, she flew across the East River and landed in Midtown, but didn’t stay long. She left for the more affordable Roosevelt Island, just across the river, where locals nicknamed her “Rosie.”

For Astoria, though, the lure of Manhattan remained. She flew the coop once more in the spring of 2025, soaring from Roosevelt Island back to the city. She eventually made her way downtown to The Battery, where she remains to this day, foraging for food and roosting in trees at night.

“She's finding ample food sources, seeds and nuts, but also little insects and occasionally small amphibians. She eats everything, probably, like most turkeys do,” Wilson added.

On a recent morning, Astoria blended in with the crowd in the park, which was bustling with New Yorkers out for pre-work exercise.

“Each morning I sort of cross my fingers that I’m going to see Astoria,” said Arvind Jaggi, 65, a Lower Manhattan resident who was out for a walk in the park. “It is incredible that this small little oasis has become the chosen home of this bird which would otherwise be in the woods."

“It’s so out of place. It makes it weirdly attractive and cute that in the midst of this concrete jungle, we have this creature of wildlife feeling at home,” Jaggi added.

Jessie Attard, another Manhattan resident who was out for a walk, said like other wild birds in New York, Astoria has adapted to city life.

“[She’s] just like a pigeon, but a bigger bird,” Attard said.
