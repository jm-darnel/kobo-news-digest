Where Enrollment Fell in NYC Public Schools This Year

By Michael Elsen-Rooney, Chalkbeat · The City · November 20, 2026

Nearly two-thirds of New York City public schools lost enrollment over the past year, according to newly released school level numbers.

Ongoing enrollment losses have left the city with a growing number of tiny schools, which are more expensive to operate and often can’t provide students the academic and extracurricular options they need. There are 240 schools with 200 or fewer students this year, up from 219 such schools last year, according to the new data. The Education Department has already made five proposals to close and merge schools this year, with more likely to come.

Chalkbeat found that more than 800 schools have fewer students than projected, meaning the city will once again have to spend a hefty sum in order to keep those schools’ budgets steady. Schools Chancellor Kamar Samuels has said a decision will come soon about whether school budgets will be slashed midyear.

Several factors are driving the more than decadelong enrollment decline in New York City, including a decrease in the number of children born each year and an exodus of families with young children from the city.

President Donald Trump’s immigration crackdown starting in 2025 reduced border crossings and brought an immigration-fueled enrollment boom in many New York City schools to a halt. That pattern appeared to continue this school year, with some of the steepest enrollment losses coming in schools and districts that enroll a high percentage of immigrant students.

Queens’ ATLAS, formerly known as Newcomers High School — the city’s largest school for newly arrived immigrant students — has fewer than 600 students this year, down 26% from last year and more than 50% from its peak of 1,428 students in the 2023-24 school year, when the immigration influx was at its height.

Manhattan Comprehensive Night and Day School, which serves many older immigrant students, is down 20% to 548 students, from 687 last year.

Beyond the steeper losses in immigrant-heavy schools and neighborhoods, the enrollment declines were widespread: All but one of the city’s 32 geographic districts lost students. The exception was District 23 in Ocean Hill/Brownsville, which gained nine students.

District 75, which encompasses schools across the city geared toward students with complex disabilities, also grew, continuing a yearslong trend.
