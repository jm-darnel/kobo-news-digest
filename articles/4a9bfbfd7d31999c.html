Looking at why New Yorkers pay so much for utility bills

By Bernadette Hogan · NY1 · November 20, 2026

It’s no secret New Yorkers pay a lot for their monthly utility bills.

More than 70% of New Yorkers surveyed by Siena University said high utility costs hurt their finances.

“Price increases are occurring nationally, but not nearly as quickly as New York,” Joshua Macey, a professor at Yale Law School who studies energy policy, said.

”Between August 2019 and August 2026, the residential price rose almost 56%, while the national price rose 36 and a half percent," he said, citing data from the U.S. Energy Information Administration.

Con Edison data for New York City show that, on average, single-family homes spend $218 a month on energy bills.

But pre-pandemic, they would have spent less at around $144 monthly.

Data from New York State Electric and Gas, which services some upstate cities north and west of Albany, shows those residents fared even worse.

Single-family households spend $147 a month on average — but in 2019, the bill was $71.

“Three drivers are: one, infrastructure costs are high across the country. Two, the cost of energy is getting higher, and this is an especially big problem in places like New York that have made it just difficult to build new supply. And then finally, different energy policies that make their ways into bills,” Macey said.

New York’s energy grid operator monitors consumer demand and dispatches energy to utility companies.

“We’ve got less supply on the system, but the demand is going up, and so the cost of that commodity has also risen,” Kevin Lanahan, senior vice president for external affairs at the New York Independent System Operator, said.

“We did not want to be a state that burned and consumed energy produced by coal,” he said, describing the Empire State’s approach to energy policy over the last two decades.

“Stricter emissions rules on certain aging power plants that burned natural gas and oil, and the regulators said to them, either install equipment that will lower your emissions or stop producing electricity,” he continued.

Now, NYISO’s biggest concern is ensuring New Yorkers have reliable power 24/7.

“Asa result, about 1,750 megawatts of those older generating units decided to either retire or stop producing megawatts for much of the year,” Lanahan said.

But the big hit for city residents was in 2017: when then-Gov. Andrew Cuomo brokered a deal shutting down the Indian Point nuclear power plant in Westchester, citing environmental concerns.

Effective in 2021, its closure took away 25% of all electric power consumed by New York City.

That vacuum was filled by fossil fuel sources.

“[The state] was not building new infrastructure while resources were retiring,” Macey said.

“That’s going to contribute to rate increases,” he added.

Though New York has invested in and attracted solar and wind projects.

Critics point to high costs and sometimes too much reliance on taxpayer subsidies. Like during a recent debate over tax breaks for a new wind turbine project in Madison County.

Fear of blackouts in the summer heat and during the dead of winter looms.

“We need to take action right now in order to meet what we have pinpointed will be significant reliability deficiencies starting in 2032 or 2033,” Lanahan said.

Gov. Kathy Hochul has loosened some barriers.

Pushing off strict state mandates for expensive green energy goals, and approvingthe long-stalled natural gas Northeast Supply Enhancement Pipeline connecting New York to Pennsylvania.

“The state should scrutinize utility capital spending much more carefully,” Macey said.

“It needs to make sure that the upgrades are occurring at low cost and considering viable alternatives,” he added.

Hochul wants to build the first new nuclear plant in decades, and the state is actively assessing bids from companies to operate it.

Spectrum News NY1 toured parts of Nine Mile Point in Oswego County.

“The United States has benefited from a whole lot of nuclear power generation without pollution,” Mark Rodgers of the energy company Constellation said.

“We’ve done that safely as an industry,” he said.

Fear over nuclear disasters in the last century stunted growth, but there’s a new push nationwide largely due to increased demand on the grid.

“Yes, this plant may have been first turned on in 1988, but because of all the maintenance that we’ve done since then and improvements, that it actually runs better today than it did then,” Rodgers said.

Constellation operates three sites upstate. The company says it employs about two thousand people and supports thousands more in construction and service industry jobs.

But it also relies on a state subsidy paid by utility customers, about $400 million a year, which regulators extended through 2049 in January.

Constellation says keeping them running saves customers money in the long run. Critics say it locks in costs for bills that are already among the highest in the country.

“We’re generating 20% of the power supply of New York state and almost half of New York’s clean, emissions free power,” Rodgers said.

Constellation is pitching housing the new site. The catch? These projects not only take years to build — it’s expensive.

“There’s not a great appetite to absorb the risk of developing those first of a kind projects,” Rodgers explained.

“So it’s going to take a number of parties working together, public-private interactions to get that combination right,” he added.
