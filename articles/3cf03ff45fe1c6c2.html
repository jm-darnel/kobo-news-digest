Yankees Potential Free Agent Target: Kaima Taira

By Peter Brody · Pinstripe Alley · November 23, 2026

In a free agent class that falls off a cliff beyond the recently-signed Tarik Skubal, there’s an intriguing cadre of Japanese players entering the market. Teruaki Sato, Hiromi Itoh, and Kaima Taira have all been posted by their NPB clubs. The trio has torn up their home soil and appear ready to test their skills stateside, though the pending lockout means that the players must sign by 11:59pm ET on December 1st. Sato is the name to which the Yankees have been most concretely linked given the need at third base, but Taira also deserves their interest given his unique skillset on the mound.

2026 Statistics (NPB): 23 games (22 starts), 146 IP, 12-5, 1.36 ERA, 2.28 FIP, 2.83 xFIP, 24.8% K%, 7.9% BB%, 0.96 WHIP

2027 FanGraphs Depths Charts Projections: 24 games started, 139 IP, 8-9, 4.46 ERA, 4.39 FIP, 18.9% K%, 9.3% BB%, 1.44 WHIP, 1.4 fWAR

Taira has been a Swiss Army knife pitcher during his eight-year career for the Seibu Lions. He has experience in the rotation, starting 23 games in 2023 and 22 games this past season. He was the team’s principal closer for two seasons, racking up 20 saves in 2021 and 34 in 2025. The other four seasons he operated as a swingman, capable of making a spot start but mostly getting deployed an inning at a time in relief.

There is always a question of how Japanese pitchers will adjust to the jump from facing NPB hitters to those in MLB. Shohei Ohtani aside, over the last decade Yoshinobu Yamamoto stands apart as the lone outlier who seemingly got better after making the switch. Other high-profile names including Imai, Yusei Kikuchi, and Roki Sasaki experienced significant growing pains upon their introduction to the league and have never quite hit the heights of their NPB days now that they are facing much tougher competition (though Kikuchi has at least made a couple All-Star teams).

Taira’s NPB career falls somewhere between Yamamoto and the duo of Imai and Kikuchi — never quite as dominant in Japan as Yamamoto but significantly better at run prevention than Imai or Kikuchi. In eight seasons, he has pitched to a 1.78 ERA in 592 innings between the rotation and bullpen, allowing right around one baserunner per inning and striking out almost three times as many batters as he walks. Taira is significantly better in the strikeout and walk departments than Imai was in NPB and is way more effective at keeping the ball in the park than Kikuchi.

I think there is some added upside here when looking at the Depth Charts projections. Japanese pitchers typically see their walk and home run ratios worsen upon joining MLB while their strikeout rates tend to remain stable. Depth Charts predicts a cratering of Taira’s strikeout rate, which if he’s able to avoid as Yamamoto, Imai, and Kikuchi all avoided would take him from being worth about a win-and-a-half to pushing a three-win pitcher.

This results in a four-seamer that enters the zone flatter than just about any four-seamer currently thrown in the league. The combination of all these factors gives Taira’s fastball that coveted rising illusion that makes it so hard for hitters to get the barrel of the bat to the ball, the pitch entering the hitting zone higher than they are expecting based off the way they are used to seeing four-seamers behave from experience.

This is exactly the type of profile that should intrigue the Yankees. They have prioritized pitchers with unicorn traits in recent years — think Devin Williams’ airbender or Fernando Cruz’s splitter. Hitters see so many pitches over the course of a season, so why not introduce an unfamiliar pitch that you can’t really game plan for if you haven’t seen it before?
