Lenape tribe says NFL owner's NJ development plan would destroy sacred artifacts

By Mike Hayes · Gothamist · November 28, 2026

Last year, the town’s planning board rejected a proposal by billionaire real estate developers, the Wilf family, ruling the plan environmentally unsound and raising transportation concerns.

Opponents of the project have vowed to keep fighting. Now, members of the Ramapough Lenape are joining their ranks, arguing that the property contains sacred artifacts left by their ancestors thousands of years ago.

At a January 2026 planning board hearing, Chief Vincent Mann of the Ramapough Lenape Nation testified that more than 100 ceremonial stone markers — intentionally arranged spiritual rock formations that acted as prayer structures — are scattered throughout the forest.

“Many, many years ago, before first contact, our ancestors are the ones that actually placed them there. They placed them in a prayerful way,” he said.

Sandy Crisafulli, a representative for the Wilfs, declined to respond to questions about whether the family knew of the artifacts or how it plans to address the tribe’s claims. Their attorneys have argued to Judge Aldo Russo that the Ramapough Lenape should not be allowed to intervene because the planning board did not address the tribe’s concerns when it rejected the project.

In his planning board testimony, Mann described not only the stone markers but also three serpent walls — stacked stone formations that wind across the landscape in a snake-like fashion, like a swimming water serpent — and a directional tree, which was intentionally bent as a sapling to grow in a way that marks the route to a path, a sacred area or a water source.

“We're sitting on the precipice of ruining forever the prayers of our ancestors that were placed in a particular way,” Mann testified.

Mann, who lives in Andover, about 35 miles northeast of the West Orange property, declined to be interviewed.

In court filings, the Ramapough Lenape have also argued that the artifacts fall under protections in New Jersey’s State Development and Redevelopment Plan. Afran acknowledged that those protections do not, by themselves, prohibit development.

“Like everything in law, it does get a little more complicated,” he said. “The state plan says these have to be taken into account in zoning, planning, and development decisions. It doesn't in itself bar development.”

Afran said he expects the judge to decide on Dec. 4 whether the tribe can join the lawsuit.
