An expert guide to navigating the Fulton Center subway frankenstation

By Elizabeth Shwe, James Ramsay · Gothamist · November 12, 2026

This column originally appeared in the Looped In NYC newsletter. Sign up here.

Here’s a fun New York City puzzle: If a friend told you they were “getting off the train at Canal Street,” where exactly are they?

For a variety of reasons, it’s not uncommon for two or more subway stations to share the same name but not the same location. “Canal Street” is guilty of this, as is “86th Street” and “Wall Street.” And for much of the 20th century, “Fulton Street” was in that camp. (And it’s still true if you hop boroughs — there’s a Fulton Street station in Brooklyn.)

Fast forward to 2014, when the MTA — which has operated the city’s entire subway system since 1940 — opened the Fulton Center, one massive train hub that unifies all these lines and makes everything simpler. Except when it really doesn’t.

My Looped In co-host, Elizabeth Shwe, recently visited the eight-line, multi-level complex on a quest to cut through the confusion and become a Fulton Center super user.

She explains all the tips and tricks in this week's video, which you can watch here.

I had some questions of my own for her too:

James: Does the Fulton Center factor into your daily commute?

Elizabeth: Only when I have to transfer to the J/Z lines, which is unfortunate because the J/Z lines are the hardest to find. That’s because the J and Z platforms were built stacked on top of each other because Nassau Street they’re under is so narrow.

So, you really have to be careful and take the right stairs to get to the correct J/Z platform. My tip is to start on the A/C platform. If you want to go to the Brooklyn- and Queens-bound J/Z trains, you have to take one of the staircases closer to the west side, where you see a connection to the 4 and 5 trains. If you want to go the Broad Street bound J/Z trains, you have to take one of the staircases closer to the eastern 2 and 3 train side.

Another thing that trips people up: Unlike the rest of the lines at Fulton, the “Downtown” J/Z trains do not take riders to Brooklyn — those lines end at Broad Street. If you want to get to Brooklyn or Queens on those lines, you need to take an uptown train.

I once had a meltdown here trying to find the Brooklyn-bound J train. I kept ending up on the Broad street bound platform … which only takes you farther into the Financial District. That mix-up motivated me to study up on this cursed station.

James: Is the short answer to “why is this so confusing?” that the MTA combined three separate train lines into one superstation, and there’s only so much they move around

Elizabeth: Exactly. Fulton Center we know today is actually four separate subway stations built by three separate train companies at different times. Jodi Shapiro, a curator at the Transit Museum, told me that train companies didn’t want to make it easy for a commuter to transfer to a competitor line, which explains why the transfers feel so awkward.

And the Financial District has some of the oldest and most narrow city streets that twist and turn in ways that other parts of the Manhattan grid doesn’t, which meant train companies were pretty limited in where and how wide they could construct their subway lines. That explains why each subway line sits under different streets at different depths.

All those stations were pushed under one roof when Fulton Center opened in 2014.

James: I’ll admit I had never been to the Fulton Center until you told me about this project. It just never fit the patterns of how I move about town. But I took an inconvenient route one morning this week just to pass through, and my main takeaway was that there’s a Dunkin’ Donuts on one of the lower levels, but don’t fall for it; there’s a Krispy Kreme a couple levels up, if you’re willing to hike.

Elizabeth: Great tip, because I only saw the Dunkin' Donuts, since it’s on the first floor where you pass to get to the other subway stations.

James: Okay back to getting around: At some point I thought I was exiting through a turnstile and was confused when it didn't let me pass. Then I realized that somehow I had already left the subway system and needed to tap again to get back in. Is that a common mistake, or was I just not paying attention?

Elizabeth: You wouldn't know this if you don’t commute here often. Even though you can technically walk from Fulton Center to the World Trade Center station completely underground, it’s not a free transfer. So if you need to get to the E, R or W lines, expect to exit through a turnstile and pay the fare again.

James: What else should I know before returning?

Elizabeth: To me, the most confusing part of Fulton Center is that instead of the traditional mezzanine being the connection point to all transfers like at many other big stations, the A/C platform is usually a better place to orient yourself. My biggest tip is to think of the A/C platform as the backbone of the station. The J/Z platforms sit at their own levels and cut through the mezzanines, so going from the 4/5 trains to the 2/3 means heading down to the A/C platform, walking its length, and coming back up.

That means you might have to walk through a platform for a train you aren’t taking in order to get to the line you’re trying to ride.

James: You talked with people who’ve made 3D maps of the Fulton Center, do you look at one of those and think, "ah, I get it now?”

Elizabeth: Yes, they really did help! You can lose your orientation easily just walking up and down stairs and walking down the long corridor. But seeing a 3D map made it easy for me to conceptualize that the A/C platform is perpendicular to all the other lines, so it’s helpful to think of the A/C platform as your homebase where you can get to any transfer.

James: Is there anything else the MTA could do, in your opinion, to make it easier to navigate? Or is it one of those things where you as a commuter put in the work to really learn it, and then you get to feel smug about it, which every New Yorker loves?

Elizabeth: One suggestion for the MTA is to put a version of 3D maps and a “you are here” sign that may help people better situate themselves. Or even placing stickers on the floors pointing to the right direction for each different transfer.

But when I visited Fulton Center again, I noticed there’s plenty of signage, and they’re technically correct. It’s just that you have to stop and read them carefully.

When I reached out to the MTA about how confusing Fulton Station is to some commuters, they told me signage is present throughout the station and reminded me that there are station agents posted throughout the complex outside of the booths to help confused commuters.
