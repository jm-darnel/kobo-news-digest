What Is ‘Affordable Housing’ in New York, and What Are My Options?

By Samantha Maldonado · The City · November 16, 2026

New Yorkers know the struggle is real to secure housing that doesn’t eat up most of a paycheck — let alone is safe, comfortable and convenient.

According to the city’s 2023 Housing and Vacancy Survey, 18% of renters put between 30% and 50% of their income towards housing costs, and 25% spend more than half their income. And there is a 1.4% vacancy rate across the city, with even less availability for apartments with rents at the low end of the spectrum.

All that means what you already knew: the housing market is brutal. So while finding a cheap place to live requires as much luck as finding a needle in a haystack, there are options — and it’s important to know what those are and where to look for them.

What Is Affordable Housing, Anyway?

Typically, “affordable housing” means housing structured so tenants pay about one-third of their income toward rent. That threshold is determined by federal housing policy, which sets the tone and rules for the vast majority of affordable housing in New York.

“Affordable housing” is also a term often used for housing that is income-restricted — that is, set aside for people whose income falls within certain limits.

But there are many other things that are called “affordable housing.” It can also refer to housing that has lower-cost mortgage or leasing terms, or that receives some form of funding from taxpayers. With that comes regulations about how the buildings are governed — including how much rent can increase — and how people may qualify to live there.

There are many kinds of affordable housing options available in New York City — and many government programs that run them — and they can be hard to navigate.

“The way that it’s organized is driven by the programs, as opposed to, ‘I am a person looking for an apartment. What are the options?’” said Oksana Mironova, a housing policy analyst with the Community Service Society.

Let’s get into some of the big types of affordable housing available in New York City:

Rent-Stabilized Apartments

When you have a rent-stabilized apartment, rent is set and enforced by the government. These are privately-owned and operated apartments in all kinds of buildings.

Unlike rent-controlled apartments (more on those below), rent-stabilized apartments are pretty common. About one million apartments are rent-stabilized, which means they make up about a third of all rental apartments in the city.

For the most part, you find these apartments like any other — through online listings, brokers or by word of mouth.

There are no income eligibility requirements for rent-stabilized apartments on the market — you apply like any other apartment. Some apartments that are rent-stabilized are not advertised as such.

In general, buildings of at least six apartments built before 1974 probably contain rent-stabilized apartments. The only way to know for sure is to request the rent history of your apartment from the state.

If you’re apartment-hunting, you can make a solid guess using this tool, or by looking at a compiled list of rent-stabilized buildings. But remember, rent-stabilization applies to the apartment, not the building.

Rent-Controlled Apartments

You may have also heard of apartments that are rent-controlled, which is different from rent-stabilization. Rent-controlled apartments are vanishingly rare. There are fewer than 20,000 rent-controlled apartments in the city, according to the Rent Guidelines Board.

Rent control generally applies to tenants in buildings constructed before 1947 who have lived in them since 1971. Unless you take over a rent-controlled apartment through succession rights — specific rules that allow a person to stay after the tenant on the lease dies — you’re unlikely to get one. That’s because when the tenant vacates the apartment, the rent may increase to the market-rate price. It may still be rent-stabilized, depending on the size and age of the building.

Affordable Rentals Through the NYC Housing Lottery

This is affordable housing you might get through the city-run housing lottery system, Housing Connect, and is often in newly constructed buildings where most of the apartments have market rate prices. Developers creating those new buildings build according to regulations that require them to build a certain number of affordable units within a for-profit complex.

To win a lottery apartment, you have to qualify based on your household size and income. These apartments are typically rent-stabilized.

You can also find affordable re-rentals without having to apply for the lottery. These are apartments that someone won through the lottery, but later vacated. Through April 2027, these apartments can be filled by qualified applicants on a first-come, first-served basis. You can apply to these like you would to any other apartment on the market, but you need to show you qualify based on your income and household size.

In addition, Housing Connect has lotteries for affordable homeownership opportunities — in other words, chances to buy a condo or co-op at a specific price for those whose income and household size allow them to qualify.

Affordable Rentals Through the New York State Housing Lottery

This is affordable housing you get through the state-run Housing Search. Eligibility is also determined by household size and income. Nabbing this state-financed housing is similar to the process of city-financed housing, and the offerings are similar, too.

HDFC Co-ops

HDFCs, or Housing Development Fund Corporation cooperatives, are apartment buildings where the owners — or shareholders — must make a certain amount of money. The requirements vary by individual building.

Once you get an apartment in an HDFC co-op, you may sell it at an appreciated value, but only up to a limited maximum. The seller won’t make a big profit, but that way, the affordability passes onto the next buyer. Plus, most HDFCs impose a flip tax, which means a percentage of profits from a sale go directly to the HDFC, to be invested in the building maintenance or for reserves.

For that reason, HDFCs are known as limited-equity or limited-profit cooperatives.

“The idea is, you purchase something relatively affordable to you and when you sell the apartment, you also sell for something that is affordable within the building’s guidelines,” said Arielle Hersh, director of policy at the Urban Homesteading Assistance Board. “It is not meant to be an investment.”

There are more than 1,200 HDFC co-ops across the city, according to UHAB.

You can find some HDFCs through Housing Connect, though others are listed like any other apartment, such as on Streeteasy. The Urban Homesteading Assistance Board, which works with HDFCs, posts available apartments on its website.

Mitchell-Lama Cooperatives

Mitchell-Lamas are apartment buildings that, like HDFCs, require income limitations for interested applicants. But the sale prices tend to be even cheaper than HDFCs, since sellers can only sell for the price they originally paid plus capital assessments (and possibly mortgage amortization).

“When the shareholder leaves the apartment, for whatever reason, and they give up the shares back to the co-op, the way it usually works, there is no profit,” said Adele Niederman, president of Cooperators United for Mitchell-Lama. “They only get back what they invested when they purchased it.”

Interested applicants for city-supervised Mitchell-Lama co-ops must enter a lottery to get on a waiting list. Once on the waiting list, it could take many years to be chosen to purchase an apartment.

Cooperators United hosts workshops and informational events for those interested in learning more about Mitchell-Lamas, which you can find here.

NYCHA

You can apply to be considered for an apartment here.

NYCHA may reach out to applicants for an interview. It’s hard to know when or if applicants might get an interview “because of of the extremely large size of the waitlist and low vacancy rate,” NYCHA warns. Over 10,800 families are on the wait list for public housing, according to NYCHA data.

Vouchers

Vouchers provide help in paying for an apartment, and there are many different types, funded by many levels of government. A significant share are for those facing specific challenges or in certain situations, such as for people who are living with HIV or AIDS.

Barika Williams, executive director of the Association for Neighborhood and Housing Development, offered advice for those who need to find housing they can afford.

“If you’re already getting some sort of services, support, aid, consultation from somebody in the city or state government, ask them,” she said. “They’re going to start by giving you the program you’re eligible for based on an issue you have.”

Here are some of the largest voucher programs.

Housing Choice Vouchers – Section 8

Section 8, which is also known as the Housing Choice Voucher Program, is a federally funded rental-assistance program for low-income New Yorkers administered by NYCHA. Over 109,000 families receive a Housing Choice Voucher through NYCHA, which allow tenants to rent an apartment on the private market. Tenants typically pay a third of their income for rent, with the voucher covering the rest.

To be eligible for Section 8 vouchers, you must have a household income below a certain threshold.

NYCHA re-opened the waitlist to get a Section 8 voucher briefly in July. After being selected from the waitlist, applicants must complete an interview before they get vouchers. Nearly 194,000 applicants are on the waiting list for Section 8 housing.

Housing Choice Vouchers are on pause — meaning those on the waiting list can’t be pulled off it — in the wake of federal funding cuts.

Another type of Section 8 voucher is a Project-Based Voucher. The subsidy is tied to an apartment, not a tenant, though the eligible tenant will still pay a third of their income in rent.

Freeze Your Rent

Senior citizens and people with disabilities who live in rent-regulated housing can freeze their rents through programs known as the Senior Citizen Rent Increase Exemption and Disabled Rent Increase Exemption. Through this program, eligible tenants can keep their rents at the current amount and their landlords receive a tax abatement credit.

Find out more here about the programs, including eligibility requirements and how to apply.

CityFHEPS

CityFHEPS is for individuals and families facing eviction and have previously been in the shelter system, or are living in a homeless shelter.

FHEPS — not to be confused with CityFHEPS — is another voucher program, but run by the state. It’s designed for families with children who receive cash assistance who are facing eviction or have been evicted, or lost housing because of a health or safety issue like domestic violence.

Other Programs

There are many other programs to keep rent affordable for senior citizens and people with disabilities.

For example, the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development provides funding to build and subsidize rental housing where rent is capped at 30% of income for qualifying residents through its Section 202 and Section 811 programs. Eligible New Yorkers can apply for these programs through the service providers that manage the housing developments, like New York Foundation for Senior Citizens or the Met Council.
