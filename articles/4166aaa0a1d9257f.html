Plan for Williamsburg business district fuels fight over future of pricey neighborhood

By Willow Higgins · Gothamist · November 18, 2026

When Katie Denny Horowitz became the North Brooklyn Parks Alliance's executive director in 2019, she started thinking about different ways to raise money for parks maintenance in Williamsburg.

Local residents, she said, were complaining that the parks and surrounding streets were filthy, and existing city services weren’t meeting the demand of the neighborhood, which had seen a major uptick in development.

“ You have more businesses attracting tourism, attracting shoppers, attracting diners and you have tens of thousands” of new residents, said Horowitz, describing the neighborhood now known for its eating, drinking and shopping scenes. “All those people are bringing with them things like trash [...] The city's budget isn't necessarily increasing at the same time to be able to pick up the trash faster,” she added.

Horowitz and other local leaders, including Councilmember Lincoln Restler, are leading the push for the proposed Northside Williamsburg BID, which would run from Grand Street to North 15th Street, and Bedford Avenue to the East River.

But getting it off the ground is no sure thing. The proposal has been met with pushback at community meetings, where opponents frame the business improvement district as another sign that Williamsburg is becoming a haven for wealthy and upscale brands.

Property owners have objected to the extra monthly fees. Dues would be based on the size of their holdings and property assessment, which could add hundreds or even thousands of dollars on top of the owners’ existing property tax bill.

“This BID is being developed with the wealthiest landlords and interests that will prioritize privatization and profit over the community’s well-being,” a Change.org petition against the BID states. “Northside BID threatens to alter the very fabric that makes Williamsburg unique and inclusive. The BID will decimate any below-market commercial or residential spaces that exist.”

Restler called the lawsuit “totally frivolous and irrelevant” and said he does not expect it to prevent the BID proposal from moving forward.

If adopted, the Northside Williamsburg BID would be New York City's 79th business improvement district – and larger than many of the others.

For the BID to be enacted, a majority of the assessed properties in the area have to vote in support of the proposal. But the city’s Small Business Services, which green-lights proposals, said it considers votes from other community members as well, and generally looks for “broad-based support.”

In the case of the Northside Williamsburg BID proposal, the steering committee first began distributing ballots in October 2025 and voting is ongoing. If and when enough support is garnered, the proposal will be brought before the community board, the City Council and the borough president for approval. With their sign-off, the measure would then go to the mayor’s office, for one last swipe of a pen.

“Our job now is to try to get the majority of folks on the ground to believe in that solution and to give it a try,” Horowitz said.

But some residents, like Anthony Dassaro, are vehemently opposed. Dassaro said he feels like he’s being forced to pay for a service he doesn’t want.

Dassaro, 58, said he has lived in Williamsburg, in the bounds of the proposed BID, his entire life, long before the neighborhood became a commercial shopping district with luxury storefronts.

“ You would see during the day the skinned cows on hooks hanging in the street,” Dassaro said. “There would be dumpsters of bones from the cows,” and people would “feed their dog with those bones.”

Dassaro said he is now the owner of a residential property he inherited from his mother. The seven units in the building are either rent stabilized or rent controlled, and, accordingly, he said he can’t pass along the potential fee to his tenants.

If the BID is enacted, Dassaro said he would owe between $2,200 and $2,800 annually. Tacking on the BID fee, on top of his mortgage and $20,000 in annual property taxes, could be untenable, he said.

“Everything is just, you know, it's spread so thin,” Dassaro said.

Still, he said he understands why some businesses might benefit from a BID, but says residential property owners don’t operate the same way.

“I would not stand in the way of the businesses wanting this, but residential property owners should not be included in this,” Dassaro said. “It's being forced upon them. It's not like you have an option in or option out.”

Restler, who represents part of the proposed BID district, said the initiative would benefit both residential and business owners.

“The benefits of cleaner streets and improved quality of life and increased property values…all seem like a pretty darn good investment to me,” Restler said. “I think that it's fair to ask residents to pay because residents will benefit.”

All told, commercial properties would cover just over 70% of the assessment, according to Sam Biederman, a spokesperson from the BID.

But only 20 of the city’s 78 existing BIDs include residential properties in their assessment at all, according to Joseph Jourdan, a press secretary for SBS.

“The idea is that this would cover a lot of ground and make a difference for everyone,” Restler said. “That's why we're asking everyone to contribute.”

A.J. Hughes, who owns his own condo in the proposed BID’s bounds, said he isn’t opposed to paying into a BID but has concerns about the potential boundaries.

If the development had been included in the boundaries, Hughes said by his calculations, it could potentially be on the hook for hundreds of thousands of dollars in the assessment.

Two Trees managing director of external affairs David Lombino sits on the BID’s steering committee.

Lombino told Gothamist that the Two Trees development will have a maintenance and operation agreement with the city that functions like the company’s own BID. Paying into the proposed Northside BID would be duplicative, Lombino said.

“These agreements require us to provide the same services contemplated by the BID—and substantially more — including construction of public parks and streets, maintenance, repairs, cleaning and landscaping,” Lombino said in a statement. “We spend many millions of dollars annually fulfilling these obligations and employ a dedicated team of 35 people.”

For his part, Restler said he would look into the exclusion.

“I believe that it should be in the BID, and I hope that we can find a way to modify the boundary or request that the developer pay into supporting the BID,” Restler said.

Biederman, the spokesperson for the BID steering committee, said the issue of River Ring’s exclusion was not brought to their attention until late in the process.

Where the proposed BID’s vote stands — or when it will end — is uncertain. Members of the steering committee said they can’t disclose the percentage of people who have voted for or against the BID proposal because of the outstanding litigation. Still, they said, this service is sorely needed.

“We can make an enormous difference in our shared quality of life in the neighborhood,” Restler said. “The streets of the North Side are often gross.”
