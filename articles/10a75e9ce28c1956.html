NYC public school enrollment drops yet again, even as some schools overflow

By Jessica Gould · Gothamist · November 16, 2026

New York City’s public school enrollment declined once again this fall, a trend that’s already added pressure to school budgets, and accelerated calls for closures and mergers that can be divisive in local communities.

As of this month, 863,100 kids were enrolled in the city’s public schools, including the city's 2K, 3K and 4K programs, according to a preliminary count released by the Mamdani administration on Monday.

That’s a 2.6% decline from last year and part of a downward trend that has continued for more than a decade. The exception to that was in 2023, when public schools saw an influx of migrant students.

According to city data, the school system has lost roughly 200,000 students over the past ten years, even as it has expanded public early childhood programs.

Experts have said the decline is driven by lower birth rates and families with children leaving the city. The decline is not unique to New York City: dwindling public school enrollment is affecting school districts and upending education budgets across the country.

Some of the steepest enrollment declines have been concentrated around central Brooklyn, the Bronx and Washington Heights, according to a report the city comptroller released last month. Schools chancellor Kamar Samuels has said 30,000 students attend small or underenrolled schools.

But enrollment is not declining evenly across the nation’s largest school system. Highly sought-after schools in parts of Manhattan and Brooklyn are exploring additions to accommodate students, even as schools in adjacent neighborhoods see their rosters shrink.

Samuels has called the enrollment decline one of the system’s biggest challenges. Over the summer, he convened working groups in five districts across the city to develop community-driven solutions to the intertwined issues of declining enrollment, class size, and segregation.

“We have to figure out what is the thing that we are going to do on behalf of our most marginalized young people, oftentimes, but on behalf of every single child in your district,” Samuels said at the time.

Samuels has said he expects to form similar working groups throughout the city.

Small schools are expensive because they still need enough administrators and teachers to oversee and instruct students. At the same time, many small schools can’t afford art programs, advanced courses or extracurriculars, putting their students at an educational disadvantage.

Ana Champeny, vice president for research at the Citizens Budget Commission, said the enrollment decline shows a “worrisome trend leading to schools that are too small to be efficient or effective, underutilized buildings, and ultimately, funding inequities between schools.”

Champeny said the city should end its practice of funding schools based on previous enrollment levels – known as “hold harmless” – that’s been in place most years since the Covid pandemic, and that more mergers and closures have to be on the table.

But past efforts to merge and close schools have been explosive, and decreasing funding for schools can cause a political backlash.

Mamdani administration officials did not respond to questions about whether there would be funding cuts related to declining enrollment this year.

City Hall spokesperson Penelope Birnbaum said the enrollment numbers underscore the need to address the city’s affordability crisis, and emphasized the investments made in free childcare, including 2K.

“With this enrollment data, we’re seeing the implications of the affordability crisis in real time – birth rates are down, immigration has slowed, and New Yorkers are leaving the city they love,” said Birnbaum. “We’ve taken major steps towards building a city that is affordable and livable, including through the launch of 2-K this fall, but this data is proof that we cannot stop or slow down.”
