NYC Council bill pits Amazon against Teamsters union in fight over delivery workers

By Walter Wuthmann · Gothamist · November 17, 2026

In the shadow of StuyTown, Gabrielly Sierra-Moreno adjusted her blue, Amazon-branded vest and lifted a box half her size. As she hauled the Amazon packages from a truck with the Prime logo to a push cart destined for the apartment buildings, she weaved around a dozen other workers wearing similar outfits.

“ Since I'm very tiny, it's kind of hard for me,” said Sierra-Moreno, cracking a grin. "But I’m able to manage it. I'm starting to get a little muscle.”

Despite the ubiquitous Amazon branding, Sierra-Moreno, 21, and her colleagues don’t actually work for the online retail giant. They’re employees of Brimela Routes Corp, one of more than 40 delivery service providers bringing Amazon packages to New Yorkers’ doorsteps.

That arrangement — where workers like Sierra-Moreno deliver Amazon packages without officially working for the $2.8 trillion e-commerce giant — is the subject of a major battle pitting City Hall against Amazon and its contractors. The Delivery Protection Act would ban these third-party arrangements and force companies like Amazon to hire its delivery workers directly. A majority of the City Council supports the bill and Mayor Zohran Mamdani has said he’ll sign it if it passes.

Supporters of the bill, including the International Brotherhood of Teamsters, the powerful transport union with about 1.3 million active members, argue the current structure shields Amazon from liability, leads to lax safety standards and dilutes the power of unionized workers.

Amazon, alongside business groups and the National Urban League, say the measure would decimate a network of small businesses, about a quarter of which are owned by Black or Hispanic entrepreneurs. Amazon has already spent millions of dollars lobbying against the proposal.

“There's people today that I employ that will be overlooked if this bill passes through,” said Julissa Alvarez, the owner of Brimela Routes Corp. “ Amazon is not going to be forced to hire my employees. They’re going to have to go through the whole pipeline process, which is going to be devastating for many of our employees.”

Sierra-Moreno said she likes that she knows her boss personally and that they can communicate in Spanish. She said her schedule is flexible and she has easy access to paid time off. “ I wouldn't want them to get shut down,” she said.

But workers for other delivery service providers, or DSPs, feel differently. They allege their employers often cut corners to save money, like deferring maintenance on their vehicle fleet and renting out budget vans.

“ Nine times out of 10, there's something wrong with my van,” said LeAnn Rivera, a driver for Excell Logistics, which operates out of Amazon’s DBK1 warehouse in Woodside, Queens.

Rivera said she recently had a van that kept rolling even after she pulled the emergency brake to park.

Samuel Mangual, another Excell Logistics driver at DBK1, said he’s also dealt with faulty brakes and other issues that can force him to abandon his route.

“I've been on a road where my van door didn't close,” he said. “So now I'm sitting in a van where I can't make the rest of the deliveries because I'm afraid someone might be able to steal it, along with my belongings.”

Some drivers said the contractor model allows both Amazon and the third-party companies to evade responsibility.

“Right now, if any accident happens to one of us drivers, Amazon claims the DSP is responsible,” said José Huerta, a driver with Fast Track Delivery Inc. and union organizer with the Teamsters. “The DSP, on the other hand, says no, that it’s Amazon that’s responsible. In other words, they just blame each other.”

Excell Logistics did not respond to a request for comment. A representative for Fast Track Delivery denied their vans were unsafe. “Vehicles identified with safety-related defects are grounded and removed from service until the necessary repairs are completed and the vehicle is cleared to return to operation,” said Amar Patel, the company’s president.

Many of the workers say that drivers delivering Amazon packages in Amazon-branded vans should be able to make direct demands of the e-retail giant.

In December, Teamsters Local 804 organized with over 200 drivers at Amazon’s Queens warehouse to demand better safety standards and higher pay in a union contract.

Drivers, who typically work 10-hour shifts delivering up to 230 packages a day, say they’d like to see an increase in their average pay of $24 an hour and better protections from layoffs.

“Why do I have their uniform if I’m not their employee,” Huerta said.

Amazon is spending millions of dollars to oppose the bill. The online investigative site New York Focus reported Amazon has spent an unprecedented amount of company money on a lobbying effort, pouring millions into a group called the Five Borough Jobs Campaign. City data shows the Five Borough Jobs Campaign has spent over $8 million dollars lobbying against the bill this year.

In a statement, Amazon spokesperson Kelly Nantel said the company is “committed to the local small business partners who work with us every day to provide fast, reliable delivery for New Yorkers.”

Marc Morial, the president of the National Urban League, the historic civil rights organization headquartered in New York City, said the third-party delivery companies are being treated unfairly as “a political football.”

“ They've taken out loans, they've taken out debt to build these businesses, and they're going to be wiped out,” Morial said. “Where are the reparations for the small business owners, the Black-owned business owners, the Latino-owned business owners that are going to be eliminated because of this bill?”

Amazon is a “diamond partner” of the National Urban League, meaning they’ve given the organization more than $1 million. A spokesperson for the organization said financial contributions do not influence their policy positions.

Council Speaker Julie Menin said her office was working “around the clock” to get the bill to the finish line. At a press conference last week Menin said her staff was working on some changes to address legal concerns.

“To be clear, we are going to be regulating Amazon,” Menin said. “We will be the first city in the country to regulate Amazon in a very significant way.”

Orin Starn, an anthropology professor at Duke University who did fieldwork as an employee at an Amazon warehouse until he was fired, said that if the bill passes, it will likely be seen as a model for other cities.

“That makes something like a giant city [taking] action at the municipal level to try to hold Amazon to account so important,” he said.

Alvarez, the owner of Brimela Routes Corp, said she’d be supportive of reforms that mandate better oversight and benefits but don’t blow up the entire system of third-party contractors. She said she feels swept up in a crusade that the mayor and city councilmembers are waging against big companies like Amazon.

“ What about me? I am also one of your constituents,” she said. “Are you trying to kill my business, my family, my dreams, my opportunity to help the community?”
