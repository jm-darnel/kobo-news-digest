NYC socialists want to be AOC's 'foot soldiers' in 2028. Moderates fear a backlash.

By Brigid Bergin · Gothamist · November 19, 2026

New York City’s Democratic Socialists of America, emboldened by Mayor Zohran Mamdani’s popularity and a wave of democratic socialist victories, want to send Rep. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez to the White House in 2028. And that’s sparking pushback from mainstream Democrats, who caution the socialist label could prove to be more of a liability than an asset.

“ We are excited to be the backbone and foot soldiers of her race,” said NYC DSA co-chair Grace Mausser. “And we want her to know that it's absolutely necessary that a left wing, that a progressive — ideally a socialist — run for president.”

While not a formal endorsement, the resolution sent a strong message following the recent political triumphs of other democratic socialists like Mamdani, Reps.-elect Darializa Avila Chevalier and Claire Valdez, as well as a total of 16 state legislators including a pickup of seven new seats for socialists in office.

New York City’s local DSA chapter also joins 18 others including Los Angeles, Chicago, Boston, Atlanta, San Antonio, Milwaukee and Wichita, which already adopted the Ocasio-Cortez 2028 resolution.

But Democrats are split on whether the socialist label helps or hurts Ocasio-Cortez’s potential candidacy.

One operative in the second camp is Tré Easton, vice president of public affairs at the Searchlight Institute, a Washington, D.C.-based think tank that is working to expand where Democrats can win nationally.

“ My honest opinion is if Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez is serious about wanting to be president either in 2028 or in the future, the best thing that she could do for herself politically would be to let her DSA membership lapse,” Easton said.

He said there are organizational benefits to having DSA in your corner, with strong voter education and turnout operations, especially in a blue state like New York, “but the brand is not good nationally.”

Easton cited the recent experience of Sen.-elect Abdul El-Sayed of Michigan, whose association with the DSA through endorsements from prominent DSA members like Ocasio-Cortez, Mamdani and Vermont Sen. Bernie Sanders – even though El-Sayed is not a DSA member – was seen as a liability.

Roughly a week before Election Day, El-Sayed was still facing questions lumping him in with his democratic socialist allies. “I’m just not a socialist,” El-Sayed said. “I believe in capitalism.”

Ocasio-Cortez – who is weighing a presidential bid, a Senate run or remaining in the House – had hinted the results in Michigan would inform her thinking.

“If Abdul can win in Michigan, anybody can win anywhere,” the New York Times reported she told attendees at a fundraiser for El-Sayed in New York City last month. El-Sayed won by 7 points, which was a smaller margin than his 11-point lead in pre-election polls.

Just ahead of the midterm elections, Ocasio-Cortez’s congressional campaign issued a memo that detailed how she has successfully campaigned for candidates in “rural districts and battleground seats.”

In addition to her support of El-Sayed, the memo cited Ocasio-Cortez’s work on behalf of Sam Forstag, a Montana smokejumper and labor union leader who was trailing in his bid for the House by 20 points before AOC’s endorsement. Forstag ultimately won his Democratic primary by 4 points and went on to flip the Republican-held House seat earlier this month.

Kendall Scudder, head of the Texas Democratic Party that successfully helped elect James Talarico to the U.S. Senate and Gina Hinojosa as governor along with flipping the Texas state house, said there is risk for a DSA candidate in a presidential primary when so many of the other candidates are likely to be moderates.

“ What I fear is that if we do end up in a situation where we have a DSA candidate running for president, all the nominees are gonna campaign on the DSA person being bad instead of campaigning on what solutions are you offering and bringing to the table to make people's lives better?” Scudder said.

But he said this is also where Ocasio-Cortez can distinguish herself from the pack. He said voters want to know how candidates are going to make their lives more affordable, whether that’s helping to pay for their kids' soccer cleats or giving them a pathway to buy a home someday.

Scudder argued labels are the currency of party insiders and that voters care more about robust party primaries that show what different solutions candidates have to offer them. He spoke from experience, after facing blowback “because I didn’t muscle our own primary” between Talarico and former House Rep. Jasmine Crockett earlier in the year.

He said competitive, high-energy contests actually have the effect of engaging more voters.

“ If Congresswoman Ocasio-Cortez feels like she has something to offer the country, she should run and let the voters sort that out,” Scudder said. “ Anyone who wants to run should do that, and we should have a knock-down, drag-out primary where people are forced to put organizations together and forced to earn people's votes.”

“ It'll make us a stronger party, 'cause we're gonna talk about issues in ways we wouldn't have otherwise,” he added.

Alyssa Cass of Slingshot Strategies, who worked on the successful campaigns of Rep. Pat Ryan, a moderate Democrat in the Hudson Valley, said the message that unites Democrats now is one focused on economic populism.

When Ryan and Ocasio-Cortez first campaigned together in 2024, they took a boat ride on the Hudson River and framed their commitment to cleaning the waterway as a fight against big business.

This year, Ryan and Ocasio-Cortez campaigned together again multiple times, and jointly appeared with Gov. Kathy Hochul in support of her one-year data center moratorium, another fight that frames the government working on behalf of local communities against big tech giants.

Cass said the only people who care about the “socialist” label are in the political class.

“I don't doubt that people will try to tag her as ‘DSA’ and make that a thing,” said Cass. “I think that it hurts all of us in the political class who will be subjected to infinity hours of online discourse by Twitter warriors, but it hurts her with voters very little because… it feels like a lazy, and at this point, boring attack line.”

Mausser, the NYC DSA co-chair, said consultants who continue to fear the “socialist” label are missing the point of the “blue tsunami” in the most recent election.

“Of course, not everyone in that tsunami identified as a socialist, but the top line of all Democratic messaging was about affordability, which is something that Zohran put into the zeitgeist,” she said. “And if people can't update their expectations about what that means, I think they may not be successful political consultants for much longer.”
