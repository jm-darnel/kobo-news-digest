What Are Your Odds of Winning New York’s Affordable Housing Lottery?

By Rachel Holliday Smith · The City · November 16, 2026

Good news: You almost certainly have better odds of winning the New York City affordable housing lottery than of getting audited — a 1 in 1,101 chance — or getting bitten by a city dog, a 1 in 2,222 chance.

And you’ve got way better odds of winning a lottery apartment than nabbing one of those Knicks-themed wastebaskets the Sanitation Department gave out in June. Just five people snagged one, out of 125,000 who applied, putting the odds at 1 in 25,000.

Meanwhile, the chances of winning New York City’s coveted and frustrating affordable housing lottery for any one application have been 1 in 510, according to The City Reporter’s analysis of a two-year span using public data. In that time period, the city received 10,250,872 applications and, of that pool, 20,200 households ultimately signed a lease for an affordable, rent-stabilized lottery apartment.

In real world terms, the odds look like this: If everyone in five full subway cars — which carry about 100 people each — applied to Housing Connect, just one straphanger would have won an apartment in the group.

“It’s crazy,” said Brad Greenburg, executive director of the NYU Furman Center, a real estate think tank. “A lot of people apply for more than one [lottery], so it’s not like one person has a 1 in 510 chance necessarily of getting a unit — they might have a much better chance than that.”

In an opaque and complicated system that garners millions of applications a year from New Yorkers desperate to find a stable and affordable place to live, 1 in 510 are the rough odds. (Read our methodology on how we calculated the odds and why we are confident in our findings.)

But the devil’s in the details, and an applicant’s chances can climb or fall steeply based on factors like household income and where they apply.

Frustratingly for those who need affordable housing the most, your chances of winning a lottery apartment were better if you earned more money, according to lottery data compiled between January 2022 and October 2024 and by law submitted by the Department of Housing Preservation and Development to the City Council.

This trend was also true when our newsroom first analyzed housing lottery data in 2020.

Generally, the lower the monthly cost of an apartment, the lower the income of the household that is eligible to rent it, and the greater the number of people who apply to live there. The households with the worst odds of all, in the “low income” category — which at the time of the data analyzed was between roughly $78,000 and $124,240 for a family of four — had a roughly 1 in 639 chance of winning, meaning there were 639 applications for every one apartment available in that group.

But in the higher income categories, dubbed “moderate” and “middle” income, the chances were almost twice as good: 1 in 325 and 1 in 398, respectively. Across those two categories, the incomes for applicants spanned about $124,240 to $256,245 for a family of four, depending on the size of the household.

At the same time, of the 20,200 signed leases in the two years analyzed, the majority were awarded to people who were listed in the lowest economic income groups.

Roughly 30% or just under 6,000 applicants who signed leases were in the extremely low income category. Those tenants earned $32,610 for a single person or $46,590 for a family of four.

Applicants considered very low income made up roughly a third of the overall leases. These applicants typically made up to $54,350 for a single person or $77,650 for a family of four. And nearly 24% or 4,735 applicants who categorized as low income won a housing lottery. They made up to $86,960 for a single person or $124,240 for a family of four.

That means that the majority of housing lottery leases were awarded to New Yorkers who make less than about $90,000.

Amira Willingham, who works in e-commerce marketing, felt like she beat the odds in early 2024 when she moved into a Chelsea studio she’d applied to the year before. She was in the low income category, the group with the lowest odds, according to The City Reporter’s analysis. It took years of trying before her number hit.

“As soon as the clock struck on my 18th birthday, I started applying,” the now 25-year-old said. “I was ready to move to any borough, any apartment size.”

Five years passed without luck, but then in 2023 she got a call back for the Chelsea studio. The rent was $1,329.

“There’s literally no world in which I’d be able to live alone for the rent I have,” Willingham said. She previously paid about the same to live with three roommates, and her current building has a rooftop pool, a gym and outdoor terraces.

More Choice for the Wealthiest

Among the lottery applicants earning the most, the odds of winning were the best.

That’s because there are relatively more apartments available for higher-income applicants, fewer of those applicants competing for the units and more choice for those applicants in the form of traditional, market-rate apartments.

“If you’re a renter of that [higher] income you would have market options in a range of neighborhoods,” said Rachel Fee, executive director of the New York Housing Conference, an affordable housing policy nonprofit. “If you’re below 80% [Area Median Income], you’re really priced out of most available housing in New York City.”

Patrick Boyle, senior policy director at Enterprise Community Partners, a nonprofit that helps arrange affordable housing financing, said the numbers speak to a staggering amount of demand, even when the odds at one income tier appear better than another.

“One in 325 is still pretty long odds,” he said. “Those numbers just highlight that there’s tremendous demand across all income brackets.”

Sometimes, applicants in the higher-income groups make a decision unthinkable to many New Yorkers: They win the housing lottery, then turn it down. Across all income groups, roughly 2,698 applicants rejected a unit, according to the data — most of them by residents in the low income category.

Shay O’Reilly, a nonprofit worker, was one of them. Several years ago, he won a two-bedroom lottery apartment in Flatbush but ultimately said no, in large part because at the time the $2,400 rent was higher than rent-stabilized places on the open market.

“We decided not to go for it just because we were like, ‘We can probably find something that’s cheaper,’ and we did,” O’Reilly said. “In 2020, we ended up moving into a two-bedroom, much closer to Prospect Park.”

Preferences in the Lottery

In spite of the hurdles and the cumbersome process to get approved, when applications to the housing lottery are selected, it’s random.

“There is no favoritism. I keep hearing, ‘You got to know somebody who knows somebody,’ or ‘Pay something under the table’ — all kinds of nonsense. But that’s not true. It really isn’t,” said Charmaine Marizan, affordable housing marketing and leasing manager at Fifth Avenue Committee. “It’s a very stringent, fair, highly supervised process by the city of New York.”

But there are factors that add weight to certain applicants within the random lottery, and those built-in preferences influence the odds.

Lotteries often include preferences for certain groups, including city employees, people with disabilities and public housing residents. Of the more than 2 million municipal employee applicants, 3,730 signed leases, giving them an overall chance of 1 in 564.

For NYCHA residents, the odds are some of the highest out of subcategories. Of the 44,702 NYCHA residents who applied to the lottery, no more than 255 households signed a lease. Those odds are roughly 1 in 182.

Across racial groups, the applicants who identified themselves as Pacific Islanders had the best odds: 1 in 444. Meanwhile, the odds for Hispanic residents were roughly 1 in 591; for Black New Yorkers, it was 1 in 524; while white New Yorkers saw odds of 1 in 481. Asian New Yorkers’ odds were 1 in 467 and Native Americans’ odds were 1 in 570.

Some applicants chose not to disclose their race. Their odds were 1 in 568.

Community preference gives an applicant a much better shot at signing a lease. The City Reporter’s analysis found that, with community preference in the time period analyzed, an applicant’s odds are about 1 in 53, whereas without community preference, the odds are 1 in 1,051.

Speaking of location, there are huge variations among neighborhoods as to where there are affordable apartments.

Part of this has to do with where developers are building apartments. Neighborhoods where land-use rules have changed to accommodate more housing may see a boom of buildings that contain affordable apartments, especially if the developers take advantage of tax credits that require them.

For example, the community districts that include Long Island City, Williamsburg and Greenpoint Downtown Brooklyn, East New York and the South Bronx were home to the greatest number of affordable apartments since 2020, according to an analysis of HPD advertised lottery data.

“That’s where all of the affordable housing is being built, and then places like Bay Ridge have not historically produced very many income-restricted units, if any,” Greenburg said.

Lower Incomes, Greater Demand, Less Inventory

From July 2020 to November 2, 2026, just 4% of the 56,271 advertised apartments available on Housing Connect were designated for extremely low-income households. Meanwhile, advertised lotteries for middle-income households made up 39%.

Those numbers didn’t surprise Jumelia Abrahamson, director of programs at University Neighborhood Housing Program, which has helped applicants successfully navigate the affordable housing lottery. The clientele of the organization, which is located in the Northwest Bronx, is typically extremely low income, making under $25,000 per household.

“One of the biggest complaints we continue to hear: most of what’s available on Housing Connect is not available for me,” Abrahamson said. “We’re working with people that are at the bottom of the [income] column.”

That means there is great demand for inventory built for households on the lower end of the income spectrum.

Take one lottery for 45 affordable apartments in Park Slope, Brooklyn. The new building was located on bustling Fifth Avenue and close to the Barclays Center, and its affordable apartments had rent set between $654 for a studio and $2,872 for a two-bedroom. (That is, geared toward very low-income to moderate-income households.)

More than 100,000 applicants entered the lottery — most in the first two weeks of the 60-day application period, which closed in December 2025.

“I’ve been doing this a long time, and I’ve never seen those numbers that fast. I was amazed,” said Marizan, of the Fifth Avenue Committee, which oversaw the lottery.

While only qualified applicants — those whose household size and income match the requirements — should apply, the process of whittling down from the full applicant pool to the people who are verified as eligible to then sign leases can be complicated.

All the information an applicant includes in their application must be manually confirmed when and if an applicant’s number is picked in the lottery.

That process is onerous, time-consuming and frustrating for both the potential tenant and the lottery specialists trying to match apartments with people, as The City Reporter detailed in our guide on what happens when you go through the lottery.

Lots of people get that first call back, but very few actually sign a lease. In the case of the Park Slope building, Marizan’s team looked at 500 applicants within different categories. All 45 households they selected signed leases.

When It Works

Other reasons for missing out on an apartment can be maddeningly slight: A missing document, or income just over or under the limit.

That’s what happened to Dutch de Carvalho, an arts educator, professional dancer and content creator who had been applying to the housing lottery for years; at one point, he counted filling out 196 applications. When he finally got a call back for an apartment, he waited for months to be told he was $400 short on income — and ineligible.

“I got rejected at the last minute,” he said.

But later that year when the native Manhattanite got a different call back for another lottery apartment — after five months of paperwork — he signed a lease and moved in.

“It’s really life changing,” he said. “I’m not worried every month about if I can pay my rent. I’m able to help out my family.”

When he shared the news about the apartment on his feed it went super-viral. His first video drew 1.1 million views; a subsequent apartment tour has drawn 9.8 million.

“People who live in our city are desperate for affordable housing and our city government does not make it easy to understand the affordable housing process,” de Carvalho told The City Reporter. “I think when people are able to hear first-hand accounts of what it is like to apply, the steps involved, etc. and actually see someone receive a unit, it’s going to spark interest.”

Content about the lottery, including tips, tricks and advice from those who won, has an avid audience. The Reddit community for discussing it, r/nychousinglottery, has 25,000 visitors a week. The hashtag #nychousinglottery on TikTok has more than 20 million views.

In Chelsea, as thrilled at Willingham is with her affordable studio, she thinks maybe she can hit the lottery twice. She’s continued applying to other Housing Connect apartments in the hopes of leveling up: her sights are on a one-bedroom in Brooklyn.

“It doesn’t always work for everybody,” she said, “but when it works, it really works.”
