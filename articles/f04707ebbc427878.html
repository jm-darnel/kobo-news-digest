NY to delay enforcement of new Medicaid rules, potentially keeping thousands insured

By Caroline Lewis · Gothamist · November 19, 2026

New federal work requirements for Medicaid take effect in January, but New Yorkers insured through the program won’t have to worry about losing coverage until July. The state is taking advantage of a six-month reprieve offered by the Trump administration, Gov. Kathy Hochul announced Thursday.

Hochul said New York is taking advantage of a recently announced option the Trump administration has given states to delay kicking anyone off the Medicaid rolls for noncompliance with new federal work rules for six months.

"New Yorkers should not lose their health coverage because of new federal red tape," Hochul said.

During the six-month respite, states are still expected to check whether Medicaid recipients are either meeting the new requirements or qualify for an exemption, and to work toward full implementation of the new rules, according to a letter from Daniel Brillman, the deputy administrator and director of the U.S. Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services.

Hochul also announced Thursday that the state has submitted its request to the federal government for an exemption from the work requirements for Bronx residents, based on the borough’s high unemployment rate.

New Yorkers’ concerns about the upcoming Medicaid changes — and discussions of how to protect their access to care — were front and center at WNYC and Gothamist’s “In Conversation: The Affordability Series” forum on healthcare at Fordham University in the Bronx Wednesday evening.

Many New Yorkers on Medicaid are still figuring out what the coming changes will mean for them. The rules will require adults on Medicaid to either work 80 hours a month, engage in an equivalent amount of community service, enroll in an educational program, or prove they qualify for an exemption.

“I did a lot of research on this because I was very nervous to lose my Medicaid coverage,” Sarah Whitlock, a Brooklynite on Medicaid, said at the event. “We rely so much on it in my family.”

Whitlock, a part-time social worker, said she doesn’t always work 80 hours a month, but has children under 14, which likely qualifies her for an exemption.

That’s a “huge relief,” Whitlock said. But she added, “I still don't know how to prove that or where to send this documentation.”

Alaila Abreu is a community healthcare worker on the city’s new Health and Affordability Resource Team, which seeks to inform New Yorkers about the coming Medicaid changes while also helping them with existing affordability issues related to health, food and housing.

Abreu said she works with New Yorkers on tight budgets that don’t leave room for unexpected healthcare costs.

“I've been hearing a lot about how they're gonna pay their medical bills, how they're gonna be able to afford to pay these bills while still trying to make rent and while still going through food insecurity,” Abreu said. “So it's a lot of things all bunched up into one.”

About 2 million of the roughly 6 million New Yorkers on Medicaid are at risk of losing coverage because of the new rules, according to the governor’s office. Hochul said the state will do everything in its power to prevent people from losing access to care.

New Yorkers who lose insurance coverage as a result of federal cuts are likely to turn to the city’s healthcare safety net, healthcare providers said.

“You could get much longer waits for specialty care because we are the only group that provides specialty care to people who can't pay,” Dr. Mitchell Katz, president and CEO of NYC Health and Hospitals, said at the Fordham event.

And those who put off care could end up in emergency rooms, noted Dr. Alister Martin, the city health commissioner, who is also an ER doctor.

But he said the city is partnering with community groups, healthcare providers and others to ensure that city residents have the support they need to stay on Medicaid so that doesn’t happen.

Several healthcare providers and advocates at the event said the federal Medicaid cuts only renewed their energy to fight for universal forms of health coverage — such as federal Medicare for All legislation and a state version known as the New York Health Act.
