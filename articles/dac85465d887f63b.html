Greenpoint man accused of groping jogger stands trial after years of neighborhood concerns

By Samantha Max · Gothamist · November 17, 2026

A Greenpoint man with severe mental illness who has faced repeated accusations of harassing, assaulting and groping people in the neighborhood stands trial this week in Brooklyn.

Christopher Boissard’s allegedly aggressive behavior has divided the North Brooklyn neighborhood for years. Gothamist previously spoke with more than a dozen community members who expressed deep ambivalence about how to balance Boissard’s mental health needs with their own safety.

According to city officials and court records, Boissard, 35, has repeatedly cycled between psychiatric hospitals, substance abuse treatment and incarceration over the years. He is currently detained on Rikers Island.

Boissard’s trial comes just weeks after a man with a documented history of mental illness allegedly stabbed and killed a woman running on a path in South Brooklyn. While it is rare for people with psychiatric conditions to commit violent crimes, the cases that do occur often raise concerns about the city’s ability to properly care for people who need mental healthcare.

Boissard stands trial on sexual abuse and forcible touching charges. He faces up to four years in prison if convicted of the most serious offense.

Assistant District Attorney Taylor Tesher said in her opening statement Monday that Boissard grabbed a runner’s breast as she jogged past him on July 5, 2024. The touch was brief, the prosecutor said, but “it still stripped away her sense of safety.”

“She could not believe this happened to her,” Tesher said.

Greenpoint residents previously told Gothamist that Boissard’s physical aggression made them feel deeply unsettled in their own neighborhood. Similar, random acts of violence or belligerence by people seemingly in mental distress in public places like sidewalks, parks and subway cars have stoked concerns from the East Village to Bed-Stuy.

Amanda Gauthier, the jogger, had never seen Boissard before that day, the assistant DA told jurors. She reported the assault to police to ensure it didn’t happen to anyone else, the prosecutor said. Tesher then told the jury that they would learn other information about Boissard that would make his intent “all the more clear to you.”

Boissard was convicted of forcible touching in 2021, according to the state’s sex offender registry, which classifies him as a “sexually violent offender and predicate sex offender.” Whether jurors find him guilty in this case will likely depend on whether they believe he intended to touch Gauthier in order to gratify himself or degrade her. He has pleaded not guilty to all the charges against him.

Defense attorney Natalia Mata didn’t deny that Boissard touched the runner. But she said it was a “hit,” “swipe,” or “swat,” and not a grope.

“On July 5, 2024, Christopher Boissard had no sexual intent. Not to degrade. Not to abuse. Not to fulfill his sexual desires,” the Brooklyn Defender Services attorney said.

Mata said Boissard can’t verbally communicate because he is deaf. She said the runner was moving very quickly and that the encounter happened “in the blink of an eye.” She also told jurors not to judge what Boissard’s intentions were that day based on any testimony they may hear from people who have had encounters with her client in the past.

“No one is saying you have to like Mr. Boissard,” she said.

Boissard, dressed in a checkered, button-down shirt and blazer, followed the proceedings through two American Sign Language interpreters, occasionally groaning or gesturing animatedly. His mother sat behind him in the gallery.

Gauthier, 30, breathed deeply and occasionally dabbed her face with a tissue during approximately three hours of testimony Monday afternoon. She told the jury that she started running in college to cope with anxiety — that when she runs, she feels like she can breathe.

On the day she encountered Boissard, she said she was training for the New York City marathon. It was an “easy run” on a path she knew well, she said, so she was jogging at a slower than usual pace and listening to a podcast.

As Gauthier turned onto Manhattan Avenue near the north end of the neighborhood, a man “put his arm up,” she said. She ran into his arm, she said, and then his hand touched her left breast “almost like the pledge of allegiance.” The jogger said it felt “like his hand was palming my breast” and that she felt “intense pressure afterward.” She recalled shouting “What the hell?” and swatting his hand before running away.

Defense attorney Johnny Castellanos questioned whether Boissard made any sexual gestures, spit at Gauthier or exposed himself before, during or after touching her chest. She said she didn’t remember seeing that.

“To me it wasn’t an accident, because it seemed like he intentionally put his hand up,” she said.

Since the incident, Gauthier said, she has continued to run regularly — both on her own and with a team. But she said she wears headphones less and is more vigilant than she used to be.

“Two years ago, before this happened, I guess I was a little more naive,” she said. “Now I am definitely more attentive.”
