Yankees Birthday of the Day: Ricky Ledée

By Matt Ferenchick · Pinstripe Alley · November 22, 2026

When it comes to the 1990s and early 2000s Yankees’ dynasty, there’s probably a core groups of names you can put in their respective positions. I’m not just talking about the “Core Four” — as they came to be termed after the 2009 championship — but the likes of Bernie Williams, Paul O’Neill, and Tino Martinez. I’m talking about the guys that were regulars across those teams.

There are some positions that had more changeover throughout that time. Wade Boggs was at third base in 1996, but Scott Brosius was there for the three-peat. Chuck Knoblauch was acquired to play second base ahead of 1998 and then ended up in left field later on. Even among the “Core Four,” Jorge Posada played during the ‘96 season, but didn’t become the regular catcher until later on.

Then there was left field. Whereas every other position in the dynasty has one or two people most associated with playing there, left field was an open door for much of that run. However, the one person that tends to stick out most in my mind out there is Ricky Ledée.

Ricardo Alberto “Ricky” Ledée Born: November 22, 1973 (Ponce, Puerto Rico) Yankees Tenure: 1998-2000

Born in Puerto Rico, the Yankees got Ledée when they took him in the 16th round in 1990 . He made his professional debut as a 16-year-old in the Gulf Coast League that year and began to make his way through the Yankees’ system.

Ledée was a bit of a slow burn as a prospect, but by the time 1998 came around, he was cracking Top 100 prospect lists. The year prior to that, the Padres wanted him as one of the key pieces in the Hideki Irabu trade, but the Yankees managed to hang on to Ledée and also acquire Irabu.

After slugging 18 home runs in the first half of the 1998 Triple-A season, the Yankees called Ledée up. He played 42 games for the Yankees down the stretch that year, but didn’t light the world on fire. However, he turned it on in the postseason. In the ‘98 World Series against the Padres, Ledée hit .600/.615/.900 in the four-game sweep. That included a big two-run double to open the scoring in Game 1 of the series. Brosius deservedly won series MVP, but Ledée had an outside case himself.

Following that effort, Ledée became a regular for the 1999 Bombers. That would end up being his best in pinstripes and one of his career best. He put up an .822 OPS and 0.6 fWAR in 88 games. That included his performance in David Cone’s perfect game, where he hit a grand slam and made this crucial catch in the ninth inning.

In the postseason, Ledée again came up pretty big. He had an RBI in each of the first ALDS games, and then hit a grand slam in Game 4 of the ALCS against the Red Sox.

In 2000, Ledée was again a regular for the Yankees, but this time around, they eventually decided to cash in on his potential for a more proven talent. In June, he was one of the players sent to Cleveland in the David Justice trade. Justice, of course, went on to have a big season for the Yankees in 2000, winning ALCS MVP, and helping them to a third-straight title.

Ledée spent the rest of his career mostly as a journeyman. His best post-Yankee years came with the Phillies from 2002-04. In all, he played 10 major league seasons, ending in 2007, and finishing with a career WAR of 1.5.

He seemingly fell off the map after his career, as you might expect for a random name from the late-90’s/early-00’s. Then a Twitter account appeared in 2013. It’s unclear if the actual, real life Ricky Ledée is in charge of this Twitter, as it’s not verified or anything. ( Buster Olney seemingly thinks it’s him .) However, for the last five years, @ricky_ledee has provided some of the best, possibly unintentional comedy you’ll find on the internet.

He is a big fan of gazpacho, but no, he will not give you his recipe.

He does often tell it like it is, though.
