What is Area Median Income? The Magic Number Behind Affordable Housing Math in NYC

By Samantha Maldonado · The City · November 16, 2026

A Murray Hill two-bedroom for $4,518. A Marble Hill one-bedroom for $3,180. A Flatbush studio for $2,022.

These were some recent affordable housing offerings in the city’s lottery system.

But those rents aren’t exactly cheap. You might ask, affordable for whom?

In New York City, affordable housing — at least what’s in the housing lottery — is housing where the rent is set at about one-third of household income and receives some sort of government subsidy. The affordable housing in the lottery is rent-stabilized, meaning its price can increase only in increments as approved by the Rent Guidelines Board.

But affordable housing is not necessarily housing only for low-income people.

“Affordable housing is for all income levels and sizes,” said Shaheem Taylor, a lottery marketing outreach coordinator at the Department of Housing Preservation and Development, during a Housing Connect webinar earlier this year. “Regardless of whether you make $10,000, $0, six figures, $50,000 — there are lotteries that are allocated to everyone.”

Read on to understand why some of the city’s affordable housing may appear rather expensive, and what it means for your Housing Connect application:

How the Rent Is Set: Understanding AMI, or Area Median Income

Affordable housing in New York City may be tied to your household’s income, but the measure used to determine an affordable building’s income categories is based on a federally set figure that’s based on rents, not the actual money a household earns.

Confused? Stick with us, because that figure — the area median income, or AMI — is really important to understanding your next application to New York’s housing lottery.

Each year, the federal Department of Housing and Urban Development sets the area median income, or AMI, for the New York City area, which includes the five boroughs as well as Putnam, Rockland, and Westchester counties. The AMI is a federal measure based on rents in the area, not on actual household earnings. Elsewhere, AMI is based on actual incomes, but in our area, HUD considers rent in the calculation because with high housing costs, more people — at higher incomes — would need access to affordable housing.

There is one AMI for the whole city, so the AMI for, say, the Upper East Side or Riverdale is the same for Elmhurst or Brownsville.

Why is the AMI so important? Because when beginning to develop new affordable housing, the city housing agency creates categories of income ranges as a percentage of the AMI, then sets the rents based on them. (Remember, affordable housing means residents spend no more than 30% of their income on housing costs.)

That’s why on Housing Connect, you see listings that say things like, applicants for a certain apartment must not earn more than, let’s say, 60% of the area median income, or 100% of the area median income.

So what does that look like? Here’s where the AMI is set in 2026:

• 100% of New York’s AMI was $118,800 for an individual and $169,600 for a family of four.

• 30% of the AMI was $35,640 for an individual and $50,880 for a family of four.

• 130% of the AMI was $154,440 for an individual and $220,480 for a family of four.

If those numbers look high to you — remember, they do not reflect actual incomes in New York City. They reflect market-rate rents in city and nearby counties.

Based on census data, the actual median income for a household in New York City is about $81,000. A family of three with that income would fall somewhere between 50% and 60% of the AMI.

Why Are Some ‘Affordable’ Apartments So Expensive?

Remember how AMI in the New York City area reflects rents rather than actual incomes? The thinking behind that, as HUD sees it, is because rent is so expensive here that more people across a wider range of incomes should be eligible for affordable housing.

But it also can mean that sometimes the affordable housing is not exactly accessible for lower-income New Yorkers — and the prices can rival traditional, market-rate units for potential tenants who have higher incomes.

Chris Walters, a land use policy coordinator at nonprofit Association for Neighborhood and Housing Development, pointed out that as the AMI figures rise each year, so do the rents for affordable apartments.

“As the AMI numbers have increased every year, at the higher end you start to get into rents that border on market-rate in some neighborhoods, or even exceed them,” Walters said.

That means rents go up at both the top income levels and at the bottom, which means the rents can feel out of reach for the lowest-earning households.

Let’s look at some numbers.

Housing Connect advertised a studio for $2,022 in Flatbush — reserved for people making 80% AMI, or between $75,772.00 to $95,040 for an individual. It’s a new building with air conditioning, a dishwasher and in-unit laundry. But on the same block, you could find a studio on the open market listed for $1,875. It’s in a pre-war elevator building and lacks the previous amenities, but there are no income restrictions.

North of there, in Crown Heights, one building on Housing Connect offered two-bedrooms for $4,518 — affordable at 130% of AMI, or for a family of four earning between $162,446 and $220,480. A two-bedroom apartment in the same building that’s not considered affordable is listed on the open market for about $5,000. But a household looking for a two-bedroom just a couple blocks away could find one on the market for $2,750 or $3,300.

Lowest Rents, Biggest Squeeze

Because affordable housing means paying about a third of income towards rent, what that means in practice feels different for different household budgets.

Take a household that makes $100,000 a year. That family would pay about $30,000 annually for rent, with $70,000 left over for other expenses. But if a household makes $45,000 and pays $13,500 annually for rent, that household has just $31,500 left over for all other expenses.

“There’s a big discrepancy between what’s left over for everything else, and that can also make something feel less affordable even if it is ‘affordable,’” said Emily Kurtz, chief housing officer at affordable housing provider RiseBoro Community Partnership.

That math may change, if City Hall under Mayor Zohran Mamdani keeps its promise. His administration in May committed to changing the calculation for rent in affordable housing for New Yorkers at the lowest income levels. For those at 30% of AMI — or $50,880 for a family of four — the rent will be set at no more than a quarter of household income. That’d be around $1,060 per month for that family.

If ‘Affordable’ Apartments Are So Expensive, What’s the Point of Trying To Get One?

Some New Yorkers may see the rents of some lottery units and say, no thanks, that’s not a deal.

But experts point out that affordable apartments are rent stabilized, so while the rent may be expensive in the first year or two, it won’t grow at the same rate as market-rate rents could. Over time, that really adds up.

“The value of having regulated apartments is that the rents stay relatively low as income grows, as other rents grow. But year one, your rent might feel very tight,” Kurtz said.

Also: Rent-stabilized tenants have some of the strongest rights against eviction in the country.

Who Decides On Levels of Affordability and Why?

Developers take multiple factors into consideration as they plan their affordable housing projects. It’s a complicated process because each financing source has different restrictions based on what kind of housing is being developed. Developers can also take advantage of incentives, whether tax abatements or being allowed to build bigger, in exchange for including a certain number of affordable apartments for prescribed income levels

And lots of affordable housing is subsidized, meaning the government throws in taxpayer funding.

“You start to layer, and that’s why affordable housing is so complex, because you’re layering, layering, layering to get as many sources as you can so that you can have very low-income units and workforce housing units,” Kurtz said.

Apartments set at lower rents need more subsidy, and HPD must figure out how to use its money to balance creating as many affordable apartments as possible.

Developers must consider how much it costs not only to build the project, but also to operate and maintain them as costs — like insurance and utilities — rise. Developers must make sure the projects pencil out. In other words, the math needs to math.

Developers also consider the project’s income. The lower the affordability — so AMIs of, say, 30% or 50% instead of 100% or higher — the more limited the rental income a developer can take in.

Taking in more rent means the project can take on more private debt and lean less on public subsidy. And the opposite is true: when a project becomes more affordable to renters it usually needs more public subsidy.

Public subsidies are limited, and require tough choices.

“It’s a public policy decision of how to use subsidy resources and and how to both make those go as far as possible, but also reach the levels of deep affordability that the city clearly needs,” said Patrick Boyle, senior policy director at Enterprise Community Partners. “Reaching lower-income people and making housing affordable to residents takes more subsidy. That’s the trade-off in the calculation.”
