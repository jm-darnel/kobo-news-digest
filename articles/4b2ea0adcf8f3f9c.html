Digging up a secret world under a Brooklyn graveyard, one teaspoon at a time

By Rosemary Misdary · Gothamist · November 24, 2026

Researchers are digging-up Green-Wood Cemetery with teaspoons, looking for new species of fungus.

Since last spring, Green-Wood environmental fellows Whitney Bauck and Lauren Wilson have been sifting through the dirt as part of a larger effort to catalogue potentially thousands of unknown species of fungus.

While fungus is a vital part of the ecosystem, essential for carbon capture and the survival of plant life, researchers have only identified nearly 10% of existing varieties. Identifying the many species is the first step in studying and conserving the foundation of the planet’s ecological system – as well as ensuring that Green-Wood continues to thrive.

”It's really hard to manage and conserve and preserve an ecosystem if you don't even know what's there,” Wilson said.

Researchers are working to establish a comprehensive database in order to weed out the invasive species and protect the native species in Green-Wood from climate stresses. It’s part of a bigger effort to catalogue more types of fungus as part of a broader effort to fight climate change and protect local ecosystems from invasive species.

“We're looking for technology solutions to help fight climate change, we're looking for conservation and restoration opportunities that have a climate angle,” said Michael Van Nuland, SPUN’s data science lead.

He calls fungus a natural technology “that has evolved on our planet to move carbon from plants into the soil that we need to be focusing more on.”

Bauck and Wilson have performed about a half-dozen digs, mostly in Brooklyn’s largest graveyard.

To obtain a sample, they measure out a square spot and mark it with mini orange cones. Then, they remove the vegetation with their fingers to expose the dirt. They use a rubber mallet to tap a 3-inch diameter pipe into the ground. When it’s 6 inches deep, the metal tube is removed gently by hand with the dirt inside.

With a sanitized plastic teaspoon, a sample is removed from the center of the tube. The specimen is sealed and sent to a lab for DNA analysis.

Along with the sample, they note location, surrounding plants, insects and soil characteristics such as color and moisture. A single teaspoon of cemetery soil contains DNA from hundreds of fungal species.

“When we get the data back from the lab, from the soil cores, it will be hundreds of fungi there,” Bauck said. “ We've found hundreds of species in New York City that had not been located here before.”

Healthy soils are chock-full of fungus. It’s an intrinsic part of soil, essentially acting as the glue that stabilizes dirt and decomposes organic matter into nutrients sent directly to trees and vegetation. Without it, most plants could not survive.

“The soil is so integral to how ecosystems work, and the fungi specifically are a key piece of that,” Van Nuland said. “If we start losing fungi, really, really serious collapse situations could start happening.”

Van Nuland said that plants are dependent on fungi for water and nutrients.

“Fungi are helping build our soils,” Van Nuland said. “They're helping all the plants that we see, the trees, the grasses, the flowers to obtain the nutrients they need.”

Identifying native species is important in monitoring the introduction of invasive species, which can outcompete the existing fungus and endanger native trees. The most dramatic example is the extinction of the American Chestnut tree by a non-native fungal blight, which was first documented at the Bronx Zoo more than a century ago.

Native trees continue to face threats from invasive species. Oak Wilt is a fungus that disrupts the transport of water and can kill red oaks in a matter of weeks. Dutch Elm and Laurel Wilt are among the lethal fungus impacting regional plant life.

”We can only protect what we've named,” Nuland said. “There are some areas where we are seeing the spread of an invasive fungus in the community, and we're watching it in real time disrupt the soil food web or disrupt the intact fungal community that exists there.”

Climate pressures are also impacting underground mycelium. Fungi have already responded to climate change. Scientists have noted that fungal species are migrating out of native ranges along with the plants they share a symbiotic relationship with. Many species are moving towards the poles and to higher elevations with the temperature shifts.

Van Nuland said that fungus is also potentially an early warning signal for climate change and desertification. It’s possible that close-monitoring of fungus can help ecologists manage and conserve plant life on the surface and mitigate catastrophic ecological impacts.

“The plants might be still persisting in an area even with intense climate pressure, but we might start seeing the fungal community start to shift even though the plants are still technically holding on,” Van Nuland said.

Wilson and Bauck have found at least 20 species that have not been documented anywhere, but expect that number to grow into the hundreds as they continue to sift through the lab data this winter. They plan to release their results next spring.
