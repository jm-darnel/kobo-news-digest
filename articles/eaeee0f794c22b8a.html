Rikers Monitor Details 14-Hour Delay in Finding Overdose Victim, Slams Slow Pace of Reforms

By Reuven Blau · The City · November 25, 2026

“The glacial pace of reform has been frustrating to all stakeholders and is far less than the urgency of the situation demands,” said monitor Steve Martin, noting violence is still far above levels the jail system reported in 2016, when he was first appointed.

“The high risk of harm is ongoing and pervasive,” the 432-page report said.

Martin credited them with establishing a reliable baseline of staffing practices for the first time in more than a decade, reorganizing the Correction Department’s deeply flawed investigations division, and winning court approval for a comprehensive action plan to guide reforms.

Jail staff appear to have curtailed some of the most extreme uses of force, the report said. But Martin described that as a baseline expectation, not proof of compliance, and wrote that the city’s jails reflect “a system that is generating avoidable force events and then responding to them.”

Conflict Over Solitary Confinement

Martin and Deml said the city’s jails lack the operational foundation needed to safely implement key provisions of what is known as Local Law 42.

They warned that eliminating managerial discretion in certain situations could increase the risk of harm. Martin also flagged potential conflicts between the law’s requirements and existing court orders governing jail operations.

Still, the city and the monitoring team are continuing to develop a pilot program for restrictive housing where detainees who act out would be placed as an alternative to solitary. That includes finding a location, the right staff, security protocols and rehabilitative programming.

Martin and Deml’s concerns come after city lawmakers spent years crafting the new rules following the death of Layleen Polanco. The 27-year-old transgender woman was in the ninth day of a 20-day solitary confinement sentence when she was found lifeless inside her Rikers cell. She was being held in lieu of a $500 bond for misdemeanor sex work and drug possession charges.

Overall, Martin’s review used almost identical language from his prior report, in January, to describe the slow pace of meaningful changes.

“The reform effort continues to progress at a glacial pace,” he said, citing entrenched resistance and “pervasive poor practices.”

Martin and his team, led by Anna Friedberg, a lawyer with no previous jail experience, have billed the city $22 million since 2024, the latest year of available data. Martin rarely visits Rikers himself and primarily relies on Friedberg and some city jail staffers assigned to assist.

For all the damning findings in his reports, Martin has seldom pressed U.S. District Judge Laura Swain, who oversees the case, for immediate action. Instead, he has repeatedly chided jail officials for ignoring his advice and dragging their feet.

Violence and Mayhem

In the latest report, Martin cites one eight-day stretch, Oct. 1 to 8, as an illustration of the ongoing danger. Over those days, the Correction Department reported 148 uses of force by correction officers, six stabbings and slashings (including two attempts), seven fires, 42 incidents of detainees hurting themselves, and 35 assaults on staff. There were also 16 serious-injury reports from fights or reported assaults, seven incidents in which Narcan was administered, 38 discoveries of contraband, and 28 emergency lock-ins.

In both cases, staff hit the men, who were trying to take their own lives, with pepper spray.

Martin called the incidents “especially troubling” because the department’s own directive expressly prohibits using chemical agents on someone trying to hang themselves. The department revised that directive in 2025, but Martin wrote that his team “continues to identify incidents” in which staff use pepper spray in those dire mental health crises.

In one July 5 incident at the West Facility on Rikers, the report says an officer grabbed a detainee by the neck, pulled his hair, kneed him in the head and struck him with a chemical-agent canister. The officer then sprayed him while he was restrained and also used racially charged language during the violent encounter, according to the report.

A captain had to order the officer to step away. He was suspended for 30 days and resigned in September while facing formal charges.

As for deaths in custody, nine people have died behind bars this year so far.

That includes Barry Cozart, 39, who was found unresponsive inside the George R. Vierno Center on March 25.

Cozart had a history of substance abuse and was seen on surveillance video looking intoxicated the night before his death, according to the report.

Staff repeatedly failed to conduct proper tours, left their posts unsupervised for extended periods, and a logbook entry falsely recorded that a tour-tracking device was working. Cozart’s body wasn’t found until roughly 14 hours after he was last seen moving, the report said.

Another detainee, John Price, 49, suffered from clear signs of medical distress as he waited for roughly 3 ½ hours in a clinic holding pen without being seen because jail officers didn’t tell medical staff he was having a medical emergency inside the Eric M. Taylor Center on March 28.

No jail or medical staff have been disciplined but the investigation is ongoing, Martin said.

“The deaths of people in DOC custody are tragic and continue to expose the serious consequences that occur when basic security, supervision, and correctional health practices break down,” Martin wrote.

Martin urged better communication between DOC and Correctional Health Services, which oversees medical care, “to improve prompt identification of people in distress, and improve emergency response times.”

“The conditions at Rikers Island remain dangerous and unacceptable,” said Mary Lynne Werlwas, director of the group’s Prisoners’ Rights Project, and Debbie Greenberger, a partner at the law firm Emery Celli, who issued a joint statement.
