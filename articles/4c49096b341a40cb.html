Can talking to protesters help the NYPD avoid clashes? One expert sees promise.

By Ben Feuerherd · Gothamist · November 16, 2026

This dialogue-based approach to protests has been implemented in London, Columbus, Ohio and Portland, Oregon. New York will be the largest U.S. city to try the approach.

Scholars who have studied and designed such strategies say the change could build trust between police and protesters, help police get more reliable information if trouble starts, and help avoid heavy-handed policing tactics.

“I think it's a very positive move. It is a reform that I think is grounded in logic,” said Professor Clifford Stott, a British scholar whose expertise is crowd behavior and public order policing.

Mayor Zohran Mamdani and NYPD Commissioner Jessica Tisch announced Monday the department would disband the Strategic Response Group, a controversial unit that responds to mass demonstrations but is also trained in counterterrorism response. With the announcement, Mamdani made good on a campaign promise to rejigger how the NYPD responds to protests.

If implemented correctly, the new approach could be an important step for the NYPD to more effectively police demonstrations, but there are inherent risks involved, Stott said.

Critics have long complained that officers assigned to SRG are trained in counterterrorism operations while they also respond to protests protected by the First Amendment. Mamdani said that will no longer be the case.

“The need to combine an anti-terrorist response with a protest response is no longer needed,” he said. ”I think that the move towards a dialogue-led approach is absolutely in line with what needs to be done.”

In addition to civilians and officers tasked with communicating with protest organizers, the plan will also create a new unit of NYPD officers who will make arrests at demonstrations if necessary.

‘Community dialogue’

Stott, who helped develop the model in Columbus, said if police are actively communicating with demonstrators, officers will likely not have to rely on tactics like encircling protesters or deploying gas to disperse crowds.

Those tactics have been prohibited under the plan Mamdani and Tisch unveiled Monday.

By communicating with protesters, police are also better able to identify a small number of people in a crowd who might veer into committing crimes, Stott added.

“It certainly makes it easier because there is a more cooperative relationship between police and crowds, protest organizers,” Stott said. “And through that collaborative relationship, it becomes much easier for the police to understand where people are present who are beginning to disrupt the harmony.”

He added that if protesters trust police more, they’re more likely to share valuable information with officers.

“So the quality of their information goes up exponentially, and their awareness of potentially disruptive situations occurs much earlier than it might otherwise,” Stott said.

He added that dialogue with protesters could also prompt demonstrators to de-escalate situations on their own.

An overhaul of the practices has strong support from some city lawmakers, including Councilmembers Chi Osse and Lincoln Restler.

Restler told Gothamist Monday that the plan laid out by Mamdani and Tisch on Monday is modeled after best practices in cities like Columbus.

“Unfortunately, on too many occasions, there is an antagonistic dynamic between the NYPD and protesters,” Restler said. “I really am hopeful that having civilian leads kind of managing the engagement with the folks protesting can avoid antagonistic dynamics and prevent issues from escalating.”

New unit

In the statement announcing the changes Monday, Tisch said she backs the reforms in part because they will keep both New Yorkers and officers safe.

“I would never support changes that diminish the NYPD's ability to protect New Yorkers, or that put our officers in a position where they lack the resources necessary to confront a serious threat,” Tisch said.

In addition to banning the practice of police encircling protesters, the new rules block the use of tear gas and long guns by officers. The department will also create a new unit called the Patrol Support Division, staffed with trained officers who will be tasked with making arrests at demonstrations if necessary.

“We have serious questions and concerns related to whether what’s being called 'disbanding the SRG' may turn out to be rebranding and reshuffling SRG officers and functions,” Loyda Colón, the executive director of the Justice Committee said.

Police unions who represent officers in the Strategic Response Group are also skeptical of the shift, but for different reasons.

“Mayor Mamdani is showing his true colors: He's willing to put your safety and police officers' safety at risk to appease his radical base,” Patrick Hendry, the president of the Police Benevolent Association said in a statement. “So the next time your life is disrupted by demonstrators who are blocking bridges and roadways or causing chaos on our streets, remember: Mayor Mamdani is on their side, not yours.”
