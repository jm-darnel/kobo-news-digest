Fans are paying the price for baseball’s lack of vision

By Joshua Diemert · Pinstripe Alley · November 21, 2026

I need to start this article with the caveat that I know baseball is a business, I know Rawlings is a business, I know that businesses are driven by profitability ( ehhhhhhh ) and all of the other things a certain kind of commenter is going to sound off on, so let’s get that out of the way.

I don’t know why that Rawlings story—of a Swiss PE firm taking over controlling interest in one of the exclusive-to-baseball brands—is getting to me so much. I’m a Wilson guy, my black and gold A2K is lying on the couch three feet from me as I type. There were and are concerns about MLB owning the managing block of shares in the company that makes MLB baseballs, while folks like Bradford William Davis doggedly try to establish how much the league tweaks their balls. I still … kinda liked that the organization most responsible for this company’s existence was responsible for stewarding that company forward, finally vertical integration that felt right.

Rawlings has been making sporting equipment since 1887, a near-complete throughline of the life of baseball itself. When I think of Gerrit Cole, I can’t separate the image of him from the red patch at the bottom of that black glove. That image, that company and lineage, has now been eaten by the same ghouls that have ruined your fast food and the housing in your city and your parents’ long-term healthcare, the maw that only ever takes, the AB+ of capitalism.

And none of this is new and I know that I’ve spent too much time talking about it — MLB failing to have any kind of vision, any desire to shepherd the game forward, to preserve what the sport gets right so that my five-year-old nephew watches as many games when he’s 30 as I do. Instead, they slash and burn, eight different channels or streams to view a game, private equity goons brought in to build real estate in a holding company’s name rather than improve the team, a lockout with no goal beyond long-term cost control and the appreciation to asset values that will bring.

No goal.

It wears on you, after a while.

I quote Roger Angell’s Game Time to people, that bit about caring, really caring, so often I’m sure my non-sports-affiliated friends have it at least somewhat memorized. The fans are always going to care the most, more than even the players because I have to break it to you, some players don’t really care about baseball and simply are leveraging their skills for a paycheque. The fans are always going to care the most, but is it too much to ask for ownership to care a little more?
