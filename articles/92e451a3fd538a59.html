New York's biggest trash dump is releasing even more methane than thought

By Rosemary Misdary · Gothamist · November 15, 2026

New York’s largest landfill is pumping a lot of methane into the atmosphere — as much as 90 times the federal limit.

“I certainly knew that the methane emissions were a big problem, but that is an enormous problem,” New York State Assemblymember Emily Gallagher, who chairs the state’s Solid Waste Management Legislative Committee, said in response to the report’s findings.

In a statement, Department of Environmental Conservation spokesperson Andrea Pedrick-Bird said landfill operators are required to “take appropriate measures and conduct re-monitoring” to ensure any methane leaks or excessive emissions are resolved.

Methane is a greenhouse gas with a global warming potential that is around 80 times more potent than carbon, according to the EPA. Exposure to the gas is also harmful to humans, contributing to respiratory-related deaths.

Seneca Meadows, New York’s largest active landfill, is the state’s biggest source of methane, according to aerial estimates published in a peer-reviewed science journal, Copernicus.

First opened in 1983 near Seneca Falls — the birthplace of the women’s rights movement — the 400-acres landfill is almost as tall as the Statue of Liberty. About 6% of its annual refuse, or 75,000 tons, comes from commercial sources in New York City, according to DEC’s most recent records.

Locals have strongly opposed the expansion.

“If you could imagine sticking your head into a dumpster in the middle of an alleyway in New York City on a hot, humid July day, it is like someone sucker punched you in the face,” said Mark Pitifer, an employee at Waterloo Container, a nearby business.

Federal law requires landfill operators to self-report methane releases. But, according to Full Circle Future, it’s a law with no teeth.

“The most shocking thing for me is that all of this is actually within federal regulation,” said Kimenthrie Finlay, Full Circle’s Director of Research and Analytics and a coauthor of the report. "Most of the federal actions don't really require too much.”

Between February 2024 and June 2025, the Department of Environmental Conservation recorded discharges at the same locations six times. According to DEC correspondence, Waste Connection was told to fix the leak and retest the spot within 120 days.

“These quick patches and fixes don't really stay in place,” Finlay said. “In several instances these fixes didn't work and that same spot kept recurring.”

Finlay said the group’s analysis is likely an underestimate since as much as one-third of the Seneca landfill is not monitored, according to the mapping of testing areas.

Those calling for Seneca to be shut down said that the report is further proof that the pollution outweighs any benefits.

“The only way that we can start to recover from this heinous mountain of trash is to close it right away, cap it, and start dealing with the problem, not consider letting it grow,” said Yvonne Taylor, cofounder of local advocacy group Seneca Lake Guardian. “We need to close this thing. And if we need any more signals, this is it. Time is now. Do it. Close it.”

Seneca Meadows has been running on an expired permit for nearly one year, but continues to operate legally. An administrative loophole allows the facility to continue accepting trash while its expansion application is under review. The site is nearly at full capacity under its original limits.

More than 11,000 surrounding residents are exposed to the emissions from the landfill.
