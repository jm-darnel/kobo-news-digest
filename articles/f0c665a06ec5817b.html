State Probes Alleged Amazon Safety Violations at Staten Island Warehouse

By Claudia Irizarry Aponte · The City · November 25, 2026

Amazon employs approximately 6,000 people at the 855,000 square-foot facility. In 2022, JFK8 became the first Amazon shop to unionize anywhere in the United States, and its workers have since voted to affiliate with the International Brotherhood of Teamsters, which also represents a number of drivers around the country who deliver packages for Amazon.

Sherri Raponi, a top official at the Labor Department’s occupational safety division, sent the Teamsters and other groups who submitted complaints on behalf of workers a letter on Nov. 3 informing them that it was launching an official probe of potential violations of a law known as the Warehouse Worker Protection Act at JFK8.

“Please be advised the Safety and Health and Essential Rights (SHER) program of the Division of Occupational Safety and Health (DOSH) has received the complaints you referenced in your letter,” wrote Raponi. “Please be assured SHER has opened an investigation of this facility regarding compliance with the above provisions of the New York State Labor Law.”

Though the letter does not go into detail over what exact claims the agency is following up on, workers have sent numerous written complaints to the agency over Amazon’s alleged refusal to comply with rules governing the warehouse logistics sector.

According to the complaints, Amazon has not submitted ergonomic safety plans known as “worksite evaluations” or provided workers with productivity quotas in writing, while refusing to work on safety issues with the union.

“We comply with all laws and legal requirements, including the Warehouse Worker Protection Act, and we don’t use fixed quotas,” Amazon spokesperson Sam Stephenson said.

Warehouse Laws

That law requires managers to create and implement injury reduction programs to identify and minimize the risk of ergonomic on-the-job injuries, which JFK8 workers claim the company has not done.

When staff presented a proposed safety plan this summer, managers refused to meet with them and did not respond to written requests for a meeting, according to several complaints reviewed by The City Reporter.

“I have had supervisors tell me I need to maintain a rate of 150 units per hour in shipdock, but I have seen in internal communication and managers that 250 units per hour is the ‘new normal,’’” one worker wrote. “None of this is ever explicitly communicated with us in writing.”

“In June 2026, JFK8 workers requested several documents from Amazon that the company must provide including the written evaluation of the on-site medical program and summary of treatment protocols,” wrote another employee. “To date, Amazon has NOT given us these documents…”

Department of Labor spokesperson Jack Lamson confirmed the Raponi letter’s accuracy and declined to comment further, saying the agency “does not comment on open investigations.”

State Sen. Jessica Ramos (D-Queens), the labor committee chair, was the lead senate sponsor on both the WWPA and the WWIRP, and she made warehouse worker safety a signature issue. Ramos, who was the subject of a recent New York Times investigation into complaints of alleged campaign finance violations related to her doomed 2025 mayoral campaign, could not be reached for comment on Tuesday.

First Test of New Labor Rules

“No matter what your strength is, your size, your gender, no matter what your physical makeup is — if you are working at full speed, doing the same task day in and day out, you are going to have some kind of physical ramifications for doing so,” said Charlene Obernauer, executive director of the New York Committee for Occupational Safety and Health.

There are no federal standards governing ergonomic safety and warehouse injury prevention, said Debbie Berkowitz, a former policy chief at the Occupational Safety and Health Administration during the Obama administration. Rather, states are left to come up with their own rules. The law in New York was the subject of fierce debate before lawmakers ultimately approved the WWPA in 2023, and the Warehouse Worker Injury Reduction Program two years later.

Berkowitz, a worker safety and health policy fellow at Georgetown University, applauded the Labor Department’s decision to investigate safety concerns at JFK8. “This is exactly how things should work,” she said.

“As we test these laws, one of the questions that’s going to come up is the penalties, and whether they are enough to prevent the employer from breaking the law,” said Obernauer. “And if they are not enough, then what would be enough — is it the court of public opinion? Is it issuing press releases?”

JFK8 workers who spoke with The City Reporter said they had not received on-the-job injury prevention training at all.

Whenever workers walk in to seek treatment for stress injuries and other ailments they are, at most, given an over-the-counter pain medication like ibuprofen and allowed a few minutes to lie down, JFK8 employees said.

But most troublingly, workers say, is what they describe as Amazon’s apparent failure to provide a safety plan as required by the 2025 law. Anthony Sparrow, the union’s grievance director at JFK8, said management has “not acknowledged” the union’s requests for a written safety plan.

Sparrow and Xavier Villaroel, a shop steward, have said that when workers experience sprains, strains or other stress fractures, they are told to “walk it off.”

Villaroel said staff are told to stretch for a few minutes at the beginning of each shift, which helps prevent injuries — but workers routinely get hurt on the job anyway. Amazon and the JFK8 facility have been in the crosshairs of workplace safety watchdogs and federal regulators for years.

A 2022 study commissioned by the Teamsters and other groups found the Staten Island facility ranked among the workplaces with the highest number of injuries in the state, and federal prosecutors in Manhattan are investigating “potential fraud” related to claims that Amazon keeps its injury data artificially low.

A 2024 investigation by the U.S. Senate Health, Education, Labor and Pensions Committee, then chaired by Independent Sen. Bernie Sanders of Vermont, found that nationally Amazon discouraged workers from seeking medical care.
