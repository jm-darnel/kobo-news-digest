As Thanksgiving Approaches, Will Hochul Replace Stolen SNAP Benefits?

By Rosa Goldensohn · The City · November 23, 2026

Last holiday season was not a happy memory for Oybek, 19, and his family in Bensonhurst.

“The whole month sucked,” he said.

His mother, a home care attendant, tried to pay for groceries at the supermarket — only to find that the family’s food assistance for the month had been stolen. They were among the thousands of New Yorkers who rely on the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program to eat and who’ve been swindled by a now-common form of fraud that targets benefit cards.

That meant a food squeeze for the whole family: no more apples or bananas, a pared-down celebration for their big holiday, New Years. The soup they ate often that month required few ingredients, he noticed — half a carrot, a potato, barely any meat and a whole lot of water.

“I’d just, like, open the fridge, looking for some snacks, and yeah, just go for water at the end,” he said.

“All of us had to like, keep it up, because my sister is still six, and no one wants a kid to know that their parents are financially struggling,” he said.

In a letter to Gov. Kathy Hochul this month, more than 150 groups called on the state to create a compensation fund to repay families whose SNAP is stolen in its upcoming budget, among other interventions. The Food Bank for New York City, New York State American Academy of Pediatrics, United Way of New York City and YMCA of Greater New York were among those who joined that call, as did many city service providers who have been pressing for reimbursements in Albany — as yet, to no avail.

Asked whether the state would take up such a fund and what families who lose benefits should do in the meantime, the governor’s office indicated that it would look to Washington for solutions. “EBT skimming is a national problem, and we look forward to working with the incoming Democratic majority in Congress to ensure New Yorkers get the assistance they deserve,” spokesperson Jonah Allon said in a statement.

“EBT skimming is a national problem, but with H.R.1 cutting SNAP, New York is best positioned to protect its own residents,” said Wayne Ho, the group’s president and CEO. While the governor deserved credit for moving the state to chip cards this year, Ho said, “New York should pair that prevention with a fund that makes families whole whenever their benefits are stolen.”

California, under state law, does reimburse benefits lost to theft, and has already instituted credit card industry-standard chip-and-tap EBT cards that prevent fraud in the first place, along with algorithmic fraud detection. But even with those improvements, some fraud persists. Chip cards are supposed to roll out in New York some time in the first quarter of next year, the governor’s office announced this summer.

Queens Assemblymember Jessica González-Rojas tried for a state-backed compensation fund in the last legislative session, but neither the Assembly nor the governor made it a priority. She plans to try again in the next session, which starts in January, when she will become a state senator.

“Families in the past two years have not been made whole, and you know, that’s real loss, that’s literally food that’s not on their table,” González-Rojas said in an interview.

“It’s a policy failure that we’re still dealing with this, and they should have acted much quicker.”

New York City no longer collects reports of stolen SNAP benefits, but when the federal government did reimburse the claims and the city had that data, the refunds totaled on average $2.6 million a month. Earlier this year, in a survey of 827 city SNAP recipients conducted by the nonprofit Community Service Society, 31% of respondents said they had been a victim of such fraud in the past three years. The Department of Social Services received an average of 7,000 claims a month in 2024.

A new report from the city’s Robin Hood foundation also recommends the fund. Asked if Robin Hood could itself step in and fill the gap, director of policy research and initiatives Chloe Sarnoff said it was a job for government. “We cannot backfill the social safety net,” she said.

At a press conference earlier this month, asked whether the city would consider refunding the lost food money, Mayor Zohran Mamdani demurred. “That’s something we’re going to continue to work with the state on, to ensure that we’re protecting New Yorkers to the best of our ability,” he said.

Robin Hood and Columbia University’s Center on Poverty and Social Policy found in their new report that nearly a third of city residents experienced food hardship last year, meaning they “sometimes or often ran out of food or worried it would run out before they had money to buy more.” About 15%, nearly 1.3 million people, turned to free food providers like food pantries, the report said.

At a grocery store on Myrtle Avenue in Brooklyn, cashier Kim braced for one of the least festive parts of her holiday season: assisting customers who try to check out — only to find that their SNAP benefits have been pilfered.

Just last month, she said, she helped a man check his $300 balance, and by the time he had finished shopping, all but $9 was gone.

It seems a more frequent occurrence than ever, Kim told The City Reporter, and “it’s heartbreaking.”

“And now, they don’t want to return the money,” she said. “Before, when it first started, they would return the money.”

Ron Roberg, 72, of Pelham Parkway in The Bronx, was at the checkout at Key Food when he found his full $306 food benefit had been stolen. “The cashier told me, ‘Ron, you got 13 cents,’” he recalled.

Roberg was already unsure about Thanksgiving plans this year. After his wife’s death a year and a half ago, holidays are in flux.

But at least, if nothing else panned out, the retired electrician and lifelong New Yorker could cook himself a special dinner. And he still will, he resolves, even though he’s not sure how the rest of the month will go.

“It leaves me constricted,” he said. “I was hoping that there would be some way of getting recompensated, considering it was stolen.”
