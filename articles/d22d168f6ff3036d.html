Rikers detainees were routinely strip-searched in violation of policy, monitor finds

By Ryan Kost · Gothamist · November 25, 2026

Detainees held in restrictive housing on Rikers Island were routinely strip-searched when leaving and returning to their cells — in violation of the Department of Corrections’ own policy — according to a new report from the federal monitor overseeing reforms at the jail.

Though department policy requires strip searches when a person enters or leaves the unit, the memo, issued in July 2023, went further, instructing correction officers to search detainees whenever they moved between their cells and the dayroom or restraint chairs.

Martin’s team only learned of the memo in October, even though it had been circulating for more than three years, according to the report. Under federal court orders, the department should have consulted the monitor before establishing the policy. The department told the monitor that various leaders had not been aware of the memo, according to the report.

Nicholas Deml, the court-appointed remediation manager tasked with driving reforms in the jails, joined Martin in asking the department to rescind the memo and issue new guidance in line with existing department policy. The department has agreed, according to the report.

Allegations that staff have used strip searches “to intimidate, harass and/or demean” detainees in the unit also come up in the report, along with concerns that some searches included visual body cavity inspections when not necessary or permitted. The correction department’s internal investigation team has opened an inquiry into the unit's use of strip searches, according to the report.

Detainees held in RESH have complained about routine strip searches before, including in written testimony posted on the Board of Correction’s website, which is routinely anonymized.

One person said they would sometimes stay in their cells for days “so that I don’t have to get strip-searched.” Another person described the setting: “You are strip-searched in front of the officers: the officer desk is right there, so there are multiple officers walking by.”

“I understand you have a job to do, but we shouldn’t be strip-searched every single day,” they added. “People aren’t animals.”

Natalie Fiorenzo, a senior corrections specialist with New York County Defender Services, said the finding “was not shocking.” She’s heard from detainees across the island — and in the new Bellevue Hospital medical facility — that they are “getting strip-searched excessively or seemingly randomly.”

These findings all appear in the latest report filed this week in the long-running federal case over violence in the city’s jails. The more than 400-page report covers the first half of the year and states that the city’s jails remain both dangerous and dysfunctional. The monitor once again used the term “glacial” to describe the pace of change.

At least nine people have died in the Department of Correction's custody this year. After reviewing the circumstances of those deaths, Martin wrote that long-standing security lapses appeared to contribute to many of them. The report describes officers skipping tours or leaving their posts, drugs moving openly through housing units, and detainees exhibiting distressing symptoms for hours before anyone noticed.

Yonah Zeitz, advocacy director at the Katal Center, a reform group, called the searches “just one of many examples of violations that have been recurring over and over and over across many years.” He noted that the jails are now overseen by a federal monitor, a remediation manager, a reformist mayor and commissioner and both the Board of Correction and the State Commission of Correction.

“[They] are all responsible for oversight, yet these types of violations continue to persist,” Zeitz said. “And it's clear that left alone, these violations will continue to happen again and again and again.”

Still, the monitoring team wrote that the arrival of Mayor Zohran Mamdani’s correction commissioner, Stanley Richards, and Deml’s team earlier this year has “altered the dynamics within the agency,” allowing for better cooperation.

A consultant working for Deml has also produced what the monitor described as the first reliable census of staff posts across the department in more than a decade. Martin said the count was critical for figuring out how to properly staff the city’s jails.
