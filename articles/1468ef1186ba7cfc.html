Busted NYC streetlights wait longer for repairs after brutal winter

By Carol Chen · Gothamist · November 27, 2026

New York City is taking longer to fix its broken streetlights after last year's nasty winter.

Department of Transportation data shows that city inspectors took more than 10 days in fiscal year 2026 to inspect a quarter of complaints about streetlight outages. During that period from July 2025 to June 2026, there were 36,291 complaints about broken street lights — a 16% increase from the prior fiscal year.

In interviews, some residents of Bay Ridge in Brooklyn said broken streetlights have taken a toll on community safety. Josephine Beckmann, the district manager of Community Board 10, said she is tracking at least seven streetlights that have been out for four months or more, including one near 91st Street and Ridge Boulevard that went dark in October 2025.

"In recent weeks there seem to be many more," she said. "And it feels like it's taking a lot longer for repairs to be made."

Jack Ho, a lifelong resident of Bay Ridge, said the lack of light makes it difficult to see on some side streets, making for a treacherous journey.

"You'll randomly come across a bump, and there's no light to help you see it," he said.

Wilson Mejia, 22, said his mother, who is in her 50s, has stopped wanting to leave the house after sundown. "She always says she doesn't really want to go out because it's dark," he said.

Yiana Green said she’s seen raccoons, rats and a skunk on an unlit street.

An analysis by Vital City showed that the top 20 hotspots for recurring complaints about streetlights are concentrated in Queens, Brooklyn, and the Bronx. A single intersection in Ridgewood near Cypress and Myrtle avenues had 14 complaints lodged with 311 since January 2024. A stretch of the Bronx in Norwood drew 40 streetlight complaints, according to the data.

Transportation department spokesperson Vincent Barone attributed the increase in repair time in part to "the historic winter that brought heavy snowfall, extended freezing temperatures, and more street lighting repairs."

In Crown Heights and Prospect-Lefferts Gardens, another hotspot for streetlight outages, residents say most of the illumination comes from local businesses.

Paco Abraham, 45, a member of local Community Board 9, said a corner of Rogers Avenue was almost entirely dark for most of a year. When a new bodega opened nearby, its signage became the de facto streetlight on the block.

“We shouldn’t be relying on a store to light the street when it’s the responsibility of the city,” he said.

Whether a dark light makes a block more dangerous depends on just how dark it gets and how long it's out for, said Aaron Chalfin, a criminologist at the University of Pennsylvania who has studied lighting and crime in New York and Chicago. In Chicago, a single failed light produced no measurable change in behavior. When two or more lights on a block went out, people stopped walking on it, and crime rose on the lit blocks nearby.

"It's a signal that these are not places that are cared for," he said. "These are not places that are prioritized."
