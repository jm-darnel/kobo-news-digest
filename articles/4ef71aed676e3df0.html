Yankees Birthday of the Day: Dave Righetti

By Estevão Maximo · Pinstripe Alley · November 28, 2026

Entering the final days of the second-to-last month in this Birthday series, where we’re celebrating a Yankee a day, our next selection is Dave Righetti, to whom we wish a happy birthday turning 68 this Saturday. Dave is enjoying retirement to its fullest, we hope.

David Allan Righetti Born: November 28, 1958 (San Jose, CA) Yankees Tenure: 1979-90

Baseball was in the DNA of the Righetti family, and as luck would have it, Dave followed in the footsteps of his father, spending the majority of his career in the Yankees organization. Dave’s dad, Leo Righetti, was a shortstop who played in the Yankees minor league affiliates from 1944 through 1949; he later switched organizations and continued playing until 1957, unfortunately never being able to make the big leagues, a challenge his son didn’t face.

As is common in baseball, Righetti’s career had a sliding-door moment early on, as a seamless random decision might have affected his whole trajectory. An outfielder for much of his high school career, Righetti didn’t nurture great expectations of a big league career and was already planning what he was going to do in life when he was encouraged by Paddy Cotrell—a scout who was a friend of his father—to try his luck as a pitcher.

Righetti excelled on the mound towards the end of his high school career. And although we don’t have his specific statistics in junior college, he continued to rise up the rankings, even winning Junior College Player of the Year honors in San Jose. Interestingly, if San Jose City College had a nickel for every outfielder turned all-star pitcher it had on its team in the late 70s, it would have two nickels, which is not a lot, but it is weird it happened twice. Believe it or not, Righetti wasn’t even the latest late bloomer starter on the San Jose City College team—he shared the field with then outfielder Dave Stieb, who would only make his transition to the mound in the minor leagues.

Righetti’s exciting work on the mound in a short sample and the ringing endorsement of Paddy Cotrell, by 1979 a scout of the Texas Rangers, prompted Texas to select him 10th overall in the 1977 MLB January Draft. He was one of three selections from the first round of that draft to make the big leagues, and easily the most productive of the bunch. An interesting story regarding Cottrell, Righetti, and his brother Steve—who also played with Dave at San Jose City College as a third baseman—is that due to their connection, the Rangers selected his brother with their next-to-last pick. Dave was told that they would only sign his brother if he also put pen to paper, as Texas was only really interested in his potential as a left-handed pitcher.

Righetti’s career is filled with these little quirks and almost symmetrical occurrences. The Yankees’ all-time leader in saves among humans—we leave Mariano Rivera in a class of his own—who would have thought that the only reason why Righetti was with the Yankees in the first place was because of a closer controversy of sorts.

Righetti had impressed in the Rangers minor league system, and by the end of the 1979 season, he had already earned a promotion to the Big Leagues with the Yankees. Then a starting pitcher, Righetti got three starts for the Yankees in a disappointing 1979 campaign that saw the team switch managers mid-season and finish in fourth place in the ultracompetitive AL East despite winning 89 games.

Righetti made his MLB debut at Yankee Stadium against the Detroit Tigers, and despite walking six batters in just five innings, he kept the Yankees in the game, allowing just three runs on as many hits. He would pitch better in his second outing on the road against the Twins but fall victim of an offense that performed below league average that season, taking the loss in seven innings of three-run ball with just four baserunners.

Despite looking good in a short sample in 1979, Righetti spent the entire following season in the minors—not only because of the lack of opportunities on a loaded squad that won 103 games before getting swept in the ALCS, but also because Righetti himself regressed significantly with a 4.63 ERA in 142 innings in Triple-A.

In 1981, though, the opportunity came, and this time Righetti seized it with both hands. Righetti had a 1.00 ERA in 45 innings with Triple-A when the Yankees called him up in late May to take the rotation spot of Tom Underwood, who was dealt to the Athletics after getting off to a terrible start to the season.

Righetti was phenomenal in 1981, completing at least seven innings in 12 of his 15 starts and leading the team in bWAR (3.5) in the strike-shortened season, also leading the entire American League in ERA+ at 174. With those numbers, Righetti received 23 of 28 first-place votes to edge out Rich Gedman in the race for AL Rookie of the Year. To this day, Righetti is one of four Yankee pitchers to win that award, and one of 10 players overall.

Despite a lousy performance in the second half of the season, finishing with a below .500 record, the Yankees earned a postseason ticket for their record in the first half. And they went all the way to the World Series, ultimately bested by the Dodgers in six. Despite a long career with the Yankees that lasted all the way until 1989, that would be the only postseason Righetti would be a part of, not only as a Yankee but in his entire career.

Righetti earned the win in his first three postseason appearances, shining in the hard-fought ALDS against the Brewers with six innings of one-run ball in Game 2—he also came out of the bullpen to cover three innings and get the win in a winner-take-all Game 5, allowing just one run. Righetti held the A’s scoreless in Game 3 of the ALCS, but in the World Series he couldn’t carry on the run of dominance Ron Guidry and Tommy John had established in the first two games. Righetti struggled in Game 3 in what turned out to be a 5-4 Yankee loss that spelled the beginning of the end as the Dodgers won four straight to secure the championship.

Righetti became a pillar of the Yankees’ rotation, averaging 200 innings over the following two seasons, but the team struggled, leading to him playing under four different managers throughout that period. Walks were a problem for the left-hander in 1982, issuing an AL-high 108 free passes, but while that limited his production, Righetti remained competitive even at his worst.

Speaking of walks, four of them weren’t enough to derail Righetti’s historic efforts on the Fourth of July in 1983. Facing the Boston Red Sox, the southpaw out of San Jose ended a near 30-year drought of Yankee no-hitters by demolishing the Red Sox in the Bronx. Wade Boggs, Jim Rice, and company could not figure out Righetti, who struck out nine.

Interestingly, this no-no came in the middle of a three-start stretch for Righetti in which he averaged over nine innings per start, twice going the distance and once taking the loss in a game he covered over 10 frames, allowing two runs. Also adding to the folklore, to this day this is the last no-no thrown on the Fourth of July, only one of three all-time, next to Hooks Wiltse in 1908 and George Mullin in 1912.

If Goosage’s arrival later led to Righetti joining the club, his departure after the 1983 season resulted in Righetti’s move to the bullpen to serve as the Yankees’ new closer. Righetti’s first career save on the road against the Royals early in the 1984 season covered eight outs, representing a preview of the type of reliever the left-hander would be, taking advantage of his starter background to routinely cover multiple innings.

Between 1984 and 1987, Righetti averaged north of 100 innings out of the bullpen, making his first All-Star Game in 1986, a season in which he would go on to lead the American League in saves with 46. Not only that, but those 46 saves represented an all-time record that stood until 1990, and to this day, Righetti is the only reliever with 46 or more saves in a single season in which they went over 100 innings pitched.

Unfortunately, the Yankees as a team took a step back from 1985 and couldn’t really hang with the Red Sox, finishing five and a half games out of first place. Righetti once again made the All-Star Game in 1986 and remained the Yankees closer until the end of 1990, always with an ERA hovering around the mid-3.00s.

Righetti first reached the open market after the 1987 season and re-signed with the Yankees, but in his second trip to free agency, the left-hander elected to return home, inking a deal with the Giants. He had one productive year in San Francisco before the wheels came off in 1991. Righetti stayed with the Giants for a few seasons before bouncing around the AL and ultimately retiring in 1996 after a year with the White Sox.

Righetti worked with the Giants in some capacity or another in the late ’90s before becoming the team’s official pitching coach in 2000, a position he held until 2017, being a part of three championship-winning squads and earning a spot in San Francisco lore. Righetti later worked on the staff for Team USA in the World Baseball Classic in 2023.

For the younger generation, he is probably one of the first names that come to mind if asked the question of notable pitching coaches of the 21st century—equally successful in both of his careers in the majors. But for older Yankee fans, he will always be “Rags,” the Rookie of the Year who no-hit Boston and became a dominant closer.
