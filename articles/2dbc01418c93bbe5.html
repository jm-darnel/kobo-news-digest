MLB, college sports, and the sports propaganda moment

By John Griffin · Pinstripe Alley · November 20, 2026

When we’re in school, we hear a lot about propaganda. American history textbooks show us political cartoons from the late 19th and early 20th century about the abolition of slavery, the rise of industrial robber-barons, and the progressive movement. European art historians discuss how Louis XIV used paintings and sculptures to present himself as the “Sun King” as he strengthened the power of the French crown over the First and Second Estates. In my own Roman history classes, I spend multiple weeks demonstrating how the emperors employed literature, history, sculpture, and construction projects to assert the supremacy of both Rome and the imperial household. Whether in middle school, high school, or college, students regularly grapple with propaganda in their history classes, learning how people with access to communicative resources have been able to present their own version of reality to sway public opinion in their favor.

Once we’re out of school, though, propaganda falls by the wayside, and ceases to be a concern. It exists, in the public consciousness at least, not as a part of our lives, but as a historical oddity, the behavior of a bygone era — or, when it’s acknowledged in the modern world, as something that happens in the nebulous “over there,” not “here.” And yet, what else can we call the flood of political advertisements in every commercial break that made the World Series borderline unwatchable if not propaganda? After all, are they not trying to sell their version of reality, as opposed to that of their opponents, in an effort to sway voters?

Propaganda is most often understood in terms of politics, but it doesn’t have to be. And right now, we’re living in what I call a sports propaganda moment, as two major entities — the NCAA and Major League Baseball — have gone on the figurative warpath, spending millions of dollars in advertising not to get people to watch their product, but to generate outrage from fans on their own behalf against that of their players.

Whether you’re a fan of a power conference school who competes for the national championship every year or a small liberal arts college who celebrates when their team gets blown out during the March Madness tournament because that meant they made it in the first place, you’ve almost certainly been exposed to commercials sponsored by the Saving College Sports organization in support of the Protect College Sports Act. Since the start of the 2026 college football season (and in truth, since last bowl season, as commercials in support of the bill ran even before it was officially introduced in Congress back in June), they have run a massive ad campaign encouraging fans to contact their congressional representatives and pressure them to vote yes on the bill. Backed by multi-million athletic programs and employing household names such as Deion Sanders and Nick Saban (a current coach and a former one, respectively), these ads center the conversation about the bill on the legitimate concerns fans have about today’s Wild West NIL era — namely, the constant transfers — while ignoring the fact that the bill attempts to, as much as legally possible, reassert the extreme levels of control that the conferences and schools used to have over their athletes.

Starting in the middle of the summer, Major League Baseball has rolled out their own ad campaign in preparation for the upcoming lockout, which is now just a week and a half away. Focused on MLB.tv and social media, the “Level the Playing Field” campaign directly argues for the implementation of a salary cap and floor, claiming that the league cannot have competitive balance due to the payroll disparities that exist between small- and large-market teams. These ads ran all summer, during the All-Star Game festivities, the postseason, the World Series, and the awards season. While, ironically, this meant that t he league plastered everywhere a chart in which the team with the second-highest payroll, the Mets, missed the playoffs as the team with the second-lowest payroll, the Rays, won the pennant, the very fact that the league spent time and energy publicly fighting the players during what should be the biggest annual celebrations of the sport is a testament to their priorities — and the important role that these ads played in their pre-lockout preparations.

Now, obviously, these two situations aren’t 100 percent comparable. Decades of corruption and a refusal to negotiate common sense solutions with its players forced college athletes to turn to the courts for assistance, and once the courts got involved, federal involvement and oversight was all but guaranteed, and all the political machinations that entails. Major League Baseball, meanwhile, is attempting, for the second time this decade, to use the expiration of the Collective Bargaining Agreement as an opportunity to break the power of the players’ union and implement the salary cap that they believe will see franchise values skyrocket . Sure, the almighty dollar may be the driving force for both the NCAA and MLB — both the Protect College Sports Act and the salary cap are, at their core, attempts to rein payrolls in and ensure cost certainty — but each organization is so fundamentally different from the other that their financial systems are incomparable.

Nonetheless, the fact that both collegiate athletics and professional baseball have adopted the same tactic, large-scale propaganda campaigns meant to turn the fans against the players, reflects the unique historical moment we are in. As we saw during the last CBA negotiations, social media has allowed players to interact with fans directly, allowing them to, for example, directly argue against the league’s propaganda, much like Jameson Taillon did last week.

Last time around, this direct connection between players and fans appeared to help the players in the negotiations, as the players were able to successfully mock the league’s insistence on removing all evidence of players’ likenesses on all media. That being said, should there be a disconnect between the fans and the players, this has the potential to backfire on the players, and perhaps this is what Major League Baseball is going for here. Baseball may be in the midst of a renaissance, but that doesn’t mean the fans don’t think that some things need to be fixed. Deferred money, especially when done to the extent the Dodgers have in recent years, has been regularly mocked in baseball-related online spaces. The rise of gambling has resulted in real, concrete ethics concerns. Blackouts, TV restrictions, and an ever-increasing stable of required streaming services have made watching the games a veritable chore. Solving none of these problems requires a salary cap, and yet, the league has tried to use their media machine to tie all of them to it, to limited success. In fact, it may have backfired just a bit: the frequency with which they have said “this proposal is contingent on the players accepting a salary cap” has sparked quite a bit of mockery online.

Of course, what has sparked such a large push for a cap at this time is the fact that this CBA negotiation might be the league’s best guaranteed opportunity for one in the foreseeable future. The Dodgers and the Mets have giftwrapped the league two bogeymen to use to sway fans to their side in the negotiations: between the former’s willingness to exploit deferred money and their acquisitions of Shohei Ohtani, Yoshinobu Yamamoto, Rōki Sasaki, Blake Snell, Kyle Tucker, Edwin Díaz, Tanner Scott, Tyler Glasnow, Teoscar Hernández, and Tarik Skubal over the last three years, and the latter’s willingness to ignore all luxury tax thresholds and common sense to sign whatever hot free agents they can, there’s quite a bit of ammunition to turn fans of small- and medium-market teams against the owners. The Players’ Association, meanwhile, has been in a bit of disarray this year, with Tony Clark one of several union executives shown the door within the last 12 months; while it’s unclear whether this will actually harm the union in negotiations, turmoil at the top can at least give the impression of weakness to an ownership group looking to make major gains.

Last, and certainly not least, is the wild card represented by the United States government. As far back as February, The Athletic floated the possibility of the President getting involved in the negotiations , and, well, even if he hadn’t already come out in support of a salary cap this past summer, it’s hard to imagine his administration ruling against ownership and in favor of labor (which, in case it wasn’t obvious, would be the players in this situation). This, in fact, may be what the league is even planning for, as Rob Manfred did when he stressed the fact that the National Labor Relations Board will be run by “ a Republican, election results or not .” There’s no guarantee the league will have an ally in the White House during the next CBA negotiations.

Together, these scenarios have things ripe for Major League Baseball to push all their chips to the center of the table and try to get their salary cap, as it might be their best opportunity for a while. And so, the propaganda machine spins on.
