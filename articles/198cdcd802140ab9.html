FDNY grapples with lithium-ion danger, EMS shortages and a building collapse

By Tiffany Hanssen, Jill Webb, Elizabeth Shwe · Gothamist · November 21, 2026

It has been a busy year for the FDNY. Hundreds of firefighters responded to a building collapse in Flatbush that killed eight people this month. Fire deaths across the city have nearly doubled since last year, with lithium-ion batteries driving many of them. And a recent city comptroller report showed that chronically underfunded and low paid EMS teams are taking longer to get to people in distress as life-threatening emergencies spike.

FDNY Commissioner Lillian Bonsignore and First Deputy Commissioner Daniel Flynn joined WNYC’s Tiffany Hanssen this week to discuss some of these challenges and the department’s response.

Tiffany Hanssen: Commissioner Bonsignore, you are the first EMS member to lead the FDNY in its history. EMS response times for life-threatening calls are still at a record high. Last month, Councilmember Joann Ariola, the chair of the Council's Committee on Fire and Emergency Management, told us that low pay is a big reason the FDNY struggles to retain its EMS workers; some making as little as $18 an hour. Do you think the councilmember is right? And what changes are you making to address the problem?

FDNY Commissioner Lillian Bonsignore: Yes, I actually really appreciate Chair Ariola's advocacy for EMS. She is definitely somebody who understands where some of the trouble lies. In this circumstance, she is correct that EMS are some of the lowest paid first responders, and that is making it somewhat difficult to recruit and retain talent. It is a very difficult job that requires a high level of medical training. And, you know, after COVID, I think some of the first responders really who were exposed to this were impacted very significantly by this event. You know, they were front and center in taking care of a lot of people. They were exposed to a lot of fatalities and overall, across the nation, it's been more and more difficult to recruit people who want to work for EMS.

With a shortage of EMS workers, I'm curious how you decide which neighborhoods might lose coverage or where to respond on a given day, how those decisions are made.

Bonsignore: It's very dynamic. We keep a close eye on response throughout the five boroughs, and if we have to redeploy and move resources around, we do. We also prioritize the calls that are coming in. Some are very, very serious and life-threatening, and some aren't. We focus our energy a lot on making sure we have a lower response time to those calls that are more serious. It is a constant monitoring of the system.

I want to switch gears here and talk a little bit about these lithium-ion batteries. The city has spent years educating people about lithium-ion batteries, encouraging them not to charge them indoors because of the risk of fires. Some progress has been made, but concerns over battery-fueled fires are back. So for folks who are charging and storing these batteries, what warning signs should they be aware of?

First Deputy Commissioner Daniel Flynn: Well, I think they definitely need to heed our safety warnings, which you can find at fdnysmart.org. That covers a lot of the messaging that we've done in the past. You certainly don't want to put one of these devices between you and your primary means of egress of your apartment. Make sure that you have a plan to get out. When possible, always keep these outside, store them outside, charge them outside. I think it's unfortunate that this has come back into the public eye 'cause I don't think it should have ever left. This isn't a problem that has ever gone away since we've been reporting on this. Since 2021 our numbers have continued to rise. This year we're at 423 fires so far this year.That is compared to year-to-date last year at 256. So we have a significant increase, but I will say that our informational campaign has been effective. Structural fires this year are at 161, whereas last year they were 149, so not a big change, although the total number of fires has changed. So people are listening to us. People are keeping them outside. Unfortunately, this year we have nine fatalities related to these devices. Last year we only had one. Also, one thing you didn't mention is legislation. We are working to craft legislation and support legislation on the federal level as well to prohibit these bad devices from being imported into our country. We think we've made a lot of progress. We've been down to DC several times to support some of these bills. We think we're close to getting some federal rules in place that would prohibit dangerous devices from being imported to our country in the first place, and I think that'll make a big change. We have passed some good legislation here in the city that prohibits the sale of non-certified batteries. That means batteries that have not gone through the testing process with a nationally recognized testing laboratory. And we go out and conduct enforcement with our partners in [the Department of Consumer and Worker Protection] every single day to make sure the shops within the city are not selling dangerous products.

Speaking of that, I'm wondering if you think that has something to do with the rise in the number of cases that we've seen of fires involving these batteries?

A lot of the rise has to do with some better reporting processes and by us raising the attention. I think everybody in the city now at some point has heard our message and is aware of these devices and the fires that they can cause. As I mentioned, our non-structural number has gone up very, very significantly, and we think that has a lot to do with maybe the improper disposal of these devices. We're seeing a lot of fires garbage truck hoppers. So we wanna make sure also that people understand how best to dispose of these devices. You can go to the sanitation's website for information on where to find a disposal site. Do not throw these in the garbage. Do not put them down your compactor chute.

I'd like to pivot now to talking about the building collapse in Flatbush. First Deputy Commissioner Flynn, I'm wondering what you think the department has learned from that tragedy.

Flynn: That our training works and our coordination within this department and with the other agencies cannot be any better. You know, we are able to communicate effectively with the NYPD and other rescue agencies in the city. [New York City's emergency management office] was out there coordinating our efforts. The mayor's office was on scene immediately.

Bonsignore: I think it's important also for the public to know that if you feel nervous or you feel like the place where you are is in jeopardy, you should make that known. You know, you can call 311, put a complaint in if it's non-emergent. If it's something that you feel is an emergency, you can call 911 and somebody will get out there to assess that situation. I know some people are very nervous now about could this possibly happen again. There are people there that will answer those questions for you if you just reach out.
