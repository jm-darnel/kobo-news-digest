PARTNERED CONTENT | The Harlem Angels: The Story Behind the Story

By Partnered Content · Amsterdam News · November 23, 2026

It began with a bright young member of my family who attended a private school, earned A’s and B’s, spoke beautifully, and was doing very well academically.

But the school only went through sixth grade. And, like so many youngsters, my young relative wanted to be with the neighborhood kids, to fit in, to be “like everybody else.” So, seventh grade would begin in public school.

At the time, I was in Los Angeles on an extended business trip. We even managed a wonderful month together in California, hiking, visiting amusement parks, and generally having a ball. Thanks to a few of my entertainment-business connections, I even arranged a small part as an extra in a popular movie.

At the end of July, the visit concluded. I remained in L.A., while my young relative headed back east, just in time for the new school year.

We spoke by telephone about twice a week. This was back when long-distance calls actually meant something to your telephone bill!

But as the school year progressed, something began to trouble me.

During one of our long-distance conversations, we discussed the new school. I was told that the other students weren’t particularly motivated. (The actual description was considerably less diplomatic. LOL!)

“Well,” I replied, “you could become valedictorian with one hand tied behind your back!”

Unfortunately, instead of rising above those negative influences— RISE UP , as I would later come to call it—my young relative was being pulled down by “friends.”

Speaking properly, I learned, was considered “being white.” Getting good grades was also “being white.”

That’s nuts, I thought.

But peer pressure is a powerful thing.

During another conversation, I mentioned that the once-excellent speech had deteriorated considerably.

And that’s when I heard it.

“I don’t need no good grammar ‘cuz I know I be smart.”

I couldn’t believe what I was hearing. My heart sank.

By the time I returned to New York, the transformation was astonishing—and not in a good way. This formerly well-mannered youngster had become virtually incorrigible. Disrespectful. Defiant. Even the grandparents weren’t spared.

I’ve long believed that language helps shape the way we think. And I was watching what seemed to be a downward spiral: poor speech, deteriorating attitudes, worsening behavior, and declining academic performance.

Then came the news that seventh grade had been failed.

I was informed that I would be taking charge. IMMEDIATELY.

That summer, I had a new roommate.

I found a summer school because this was a bright kid who was being left behind.

I needed a different approach.

So I devised a simple tactic: I would not reward bad behavior with attention.

Bad grammar? No response.

Disrespect? No response.

At dinner, for example, I might hear, “Yo! Gimme da salt.”

Nothing.

A moment later, a throat would clear.

Then, in the beautifully articulated voice I remembered:

“May I please have the salt?”

Without comment, without even looking up, I passed the salt.

No lecture. No congratulations. No fuss.

Proper speech and respectful behavior got results. The alternative did not.

And gradually, things began to change.

But remnants of those newly acquired bad habits persisted.

What could I do? What could I possibly do to counteract the influence of peers who seemed determined to make academic achievement something to be embarrassed about?

Then … inspiration.

I was in the music business. I knew how to create and develop entertainment.

Why not create some new role models?

I’d develop a trio of young performers singing hip songs—with proper grammar, positive messages, and a beat!

And that’s how the idea for The Harlem Angels was born.

It soon became more than an entertainment concept. It became part of our everyday lives.

We’d watch troubling stories on the television news, and I’d ask:

“What would The Harlem Angels do?”

That simple question redirected the conversation away from problems and toward solutions.

Instead of dwelling on bad behavior, my young charge was given agency and began imagining better choices.

And the turnaround was extraordinary.

After failing seventh grade and falling a year behind, this once-struggling youngster made such remarkable progress that most of high school was completed in an accelerated fashion. College began at seventeen—a full year early—and with a small scholarship, to boot. In effect, four years of academic progress had been accomplished in roughly two.

Not bad for someone who supposedly didn’t need no good grammar!

For years afterward, I put The Harlem Angels aside.

A family tragedy intervened, and the project became a reminder of something too painful to revisit. I needed time before I could return to it. It took another decade.

Then, almost as though a dam had burst, the creativity came flooding back.

Eleven original songs. Eight music videos. Three books. Seven episodic television scripts. A website. And more.

What began as an effort to help one youngster resist negative peer pressure had grown into an entire world of entertainment built around three remarkable girls from Harlem: Angelina, Angelika, and Angelala.

They have personalities, talents, ambitions, disagreements, adventures, and a shared determination to rise above whatever life throws their way.

They’re not intended to lecture young people. They’re meant to entertain them, engage them, and perhaps inspire them to make better choices along the way.

And although The Harlem Angels really did become angels, they are very much alive.

Their story is only beginning.

A Christmas Gift With Wings

With Thanksgiving approaching and Christmas not far behind, I’d like to make a suggestion to grandparents, parents, and anyone who has a young person in their life.

Instead of another gift that might be forgotten by New Year’s Day, how about a book that could entertain, encourage reading, and perhaps inspire a few conversations around the family table? What was once meant to be a rescue mission for one is now available to be a fun and positive influence for many. No heavy lifting required.

Grandparents, especially, might enjoy giving a book that can be shared with their grandchildren—and perhaps reading it together.

Order by Thanksgiving to allow time for delivery before Christmas, and you could have three very special visitors waiting under the tree.

Angelina. Angelika. Angelala.

Three girls from Harlem, ready to open every door.

RISE UP! RISE UP! Watch Us Soar!
