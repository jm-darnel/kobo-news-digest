NY, the 'Freedom Summer' murders and an improbable election win in Mississippi

By Amanda Rozon · Gothamist · November 16, 2026

In 1964, a 20-year-old student from New York City was murdered in Neshoba County, Mississippi, along with two other civil rights workers.

Andrew Goodman was there trying to help Black Mississippians register to vote. A member of the Ku Klux Klan was eventually found guilty of directing the killing of Goodman, James Earl Chaney, who was from Mississippi, and Michael Schwerner, who was from Pelham, New York.

Now, 62 years after the deaths, Mississippians have elected their first Black senator in 150 years. Democrat Scott Colom defeated Republican incumbent Cindy Hyde-Smith in a closely contested race.

David Goodman, a New Jersey resident and the brother of Andrew Goodman, serves on the board of the Andrew Goodman Foundation.

He spoke with Morning Edition host Michael Hill in the aftermath of Colom’s victory.

Here is a transcript of their conversation, which has been edited for length and clarity:

Michael Hill: David, good morning, and welcome to Morning Edition.

David Goodman: Good morning, Michael. Thank you so much for inviting me. I really appreciate it.

Tell us briefly how your brother Andrew, who you called Andy, came to join what's known as Freedom Summer in the 1960s, and how it eventually led to his murder.

We lived in New York City, and we were brought up by our parents to believe in this concept of fairness. I gotta tell you a funny story, because it sort of set the pace. My mother would take me to the butcher. And this is before big supermarkets. You had to buy your meat fresh. She said during one trip, “Watch so-and-so." They had 10 butchers behind the counter, and I did, and she's looking at him, and he's looking at her. And l asked, "What's this about, mom?" She said, "Well, he had his thumb on the scale and was cheating us, right?” So that became kind of a password in our family, and many families take it to mean being cheated. And, my brother — we all had that kind of sense of fairness. Things should be fair, and you shouldn't be cheated, and you shouldn't cheat anybody else.

And in the spring of 1964, I was about to graduate high school. He was three years older than me. And he announces to the family, "I'm going to Mississippi." And I don't even know where Mississippi was, and I said, "Why?" He said, "Well," he heard that African Americans weren't allowed to vote by the white power structure in Mississippi. I didn't know what that meant. And then he says, "You know, the ‘thumb-on-the-scale thing.’" I said, "Oh, not fair." And he said it that way. He said, "It's just not fair." And then he said something that was strange. He said, "And I wanna do something about it." And that was just his nature. He was really a big proponent of living a fair life. It almost sounds naive. I don't think we really realized the danger, but this is how we were brought up. So he went to Mississippi and joined the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee. John Lewis was the national chair at the age of 24, and Andrew got murdered being fair. That's what happened.

What do you think of the election of Sen.-elect Colom? And do you see a nexus, a link to Andrew's advocacy some six decades ago?

Absolutely. We have 50 states in the union, and none of them are perfect. Specifically in Mississippi, this had a more than a rough justice kind of feel to it. Mr. Colom is a shrewd guy — a prosecutor and lawyer, and he had his eyes out for things that were unfair, and he turned on enough voters to see some folks had their thumb on the scale. And he said something about it, and he worked hard and took a big risk, and he won. The people of Mississippi have spoken.

But as I say that, I wanna make something really clear. You know, we're New Jersey residents. Mississippi isn't the only state where powerful people have their thumb on the scale. I mean, in New Jersey recently, we put a Democratic senator in jail for stealing from us, right? And we have to keep in mind that Mississippi is just one of 50 states, and we all have a responsibility to keep our eyes on the prize, and when we see something wrong, do something about it. That's why we, my parents, formed the Andrew Goodman Foundation.

As the civil rights movement came under attack in Congress in the 1960s, your brother Andrew said, “the senators could not persist in this polite debate over the future dignity of a human race if the white northerners were not so shockingly apathetic.” Does what he said still apply today?

Oh, absolutely. This is the exact point I'm making. Every state has some people who have their thumbs on the scale of justice. And we the people, we have to watch that. He wrote a paper that I think you're quoting from, that if you're in New York and you don't say anything about something bad going on in Montana or Maine or Mississippi, it's hurting you in New York even though you may not know it. We're all part of the same country. You know, Sen.-elect Colom is going to the U.S. Senate, and he votes on stuff that affects us in New Jersey, and vice versa. We're all in one country, and if someone has their thumbs on the scale of justice in New Jersey or Mississippi or Montana, it hurts all the other states.

So what he was saying was we all gotta pay attention, which is what we do at the Andrew Goodman Foundation today, whose mission is making young voices and votes a powerful force in democracy, and you do that by voting. And if you don't like what someone is doing who is in power, you fire him, vote him out, and you vote somebody else in. And that's what my brother meant in what he wrote, "New York, pay attention. New Jersey, pay attention. Mississippi, pay attention, and get rid of the people who got their thumb on the scale, and put in people who are gonna represent us."

Our guest has been David Goodman, brother of Andrew Goodman, a New York City student who in 1964 was murdered helping Black Mississippians register to vote. David, what an honor.

Thank you so much.
