Fate of long-stalled NY bills could be decided by 27 new Democrats

By Jon Campbell · Gothamist · November 26, 2026

This column originally appeared in The Politics Brief, our weekly newsletter on the people, power and policies that shape New Yorkers' lives.

When New York state lawmakers return to the Capitol in January, Democrats will control at least 158 of the 213 total seats in the Legislature — up from 144 at the start of 2024.

All those fresh faces present a major opportunity for supporters of bills that have long stalled in the Legislature’s lower house. With 27 new majority members, that’s 27 new opportunities for advocates and their lobbyists to pick off votes for their cause.

“I started talking to some of the people who won Democratic primaries a few weeks after the primaries,” said Judith Enck, founder of Beyond Plastics. “We have to educate every incoming member and just see if we can get our numbers up.”

Enck, a former regional administration for the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, has spent countless hours working the halls of the state Capitol in support of a bill that would force many companies to dramatically reduce their plastic packaging. The state Senate has passed the bill more than once, but the Assembly has never put it on the floor for a vote. She’s hoping 2027 is finally the year that changes — perhaps with the support of many of the Assembly’s new members.

Assembly Speaker Carl Heastie, a Bronx Democrat, says he picks which bills come to the floor for votes based on whether there are enough Democratic votes to pass them. A bill needs 76 votes to pass in the chamber — but, Heastie told WNYC’s “The Brian Lehrer Show,” he likes to have a bit of a buffer. And he won’t rely on Republican votes, either.

“People say, ‘Well, 76 sponsors can pass a bill,’” Heastie said earlier this month. “Yeah, that's great. But what happens if that one person is not there? People change their mind. So yeah, I usually like to have a little bit of a cushion.”

A bill to change state sexual assault laws to make it easier to prosecute cases in which victims knowingly consumed drugs or alcohol has also drawn renewed attention in recent months.

In September, a Cornell University student filed a civil lawsuit alleging she was gang raped at a frat house. The local district attorney claimed current New York law — which, as in 18 other states, only considers someone incapacitated if they’re drugged or became drunk without their consent — was an impediment to bringing charges.

That bill also passed the Senate but failed to get an Assembly vote. Heastie said Assembly Democrats discussed the bill in private twice. There weren’t enough Democratic votes either time, he said.

Now, with 27 new Democrats, supporters of the measure are hoping they can convince enough of the new lawmakers to tip the scale their way. But opponents have the same opportunity. Public defenders, in particular, have long argued the bill is overly broad, unconstitutionally vague and would lead to arbitrary enforcement, which helped derail efforts to pass the bill in years past.

Assemblymember-elect Brian Romero, a Queens Democrat, said he supports adding a voluntary intoxication clause to the sexual assault law, so long as there are protections in place to protect any one community from being disproportionately affected. He’s one of the 27 new Assembly Democrats taking office next year.

“I have to see what the bill looks like that is introduced in session next year,” he said. “It is very true that anything on the penal code will likely disproportionately impact Black and brown New Yorkers — and we have to figure out a compromise and solution because campus sexual assault and sexual assault in general is horrific and deeply traumatizing.”

In hopes of striking a compromise, Heastie tapped a group of 14 Assembly Democrats to examine the issue earlier this year. It’s due to deliver its recommendations to the speaker before the end of the year.

Soon-to-be-former Assemblymember Jessica Gonzalez-Rojas, a Queens Democrat who is heading to the state Senate next year, said she’s hopeful the working group will come up with a solution that all lawmakers can support.

“ I do think it's critical that we pass something and that we get to a place where maybe not everyone's going to be happy, but we're addressing the horrors that came out of Cornell and that happen all the time,” she said.
