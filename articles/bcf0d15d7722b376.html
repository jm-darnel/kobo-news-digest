Paved-Over Black Cemetery Could Qualify as Landmark, Commission Says

By Greg B. Smith · The City · November 19, 2026

Researchers at the city’s Landmarks Preservation Commission have determined that an African burial ground in Elmhurst, Queens, that currently sits under an illegal parking lot is likely eligible for landmark status.

“We believe the former cemetery has cultural and historic significance related to the 19th Century Black and abolitionist history of New York City,” Margaret Herman, the commission’s director of research, wrote last month. “Research staff finds that the former cemetery may merit consideration for designation as a landmark.”

After The City Reporter revealed that tow trucks hired by the NYPD to remove abandoned vehicles from the streets were dropping cars and trucks there, a host of elected officials called on the administration of Mayor Zohran Mamdani to purchase the site and turn it into a memorial. The lot was soon shut down by the Buildings Department.

In August the Elmhurst History and Cemetery Preservation Society filed a request with Landmarks after the revelation about the parking lot’s use. Herman responded to the group’s president, Mariaelena Giampino, in October revealing that the commission’s research unit agreed the site was likely eligible to be landmarked.

Mamdani Administration Cool to City Purchase

On Thursday, Matthew Rauschenbach, a spokesperson for the mayor, said the city has no current plans to purchase the burial ground. He declined to comment on the Landmarks letter. All of the commission’s 10 members are mayoral appointees.

In September, state Attorney General Letitia James also got involved, filing a motion in a pending foreclosure lawsuit demanding that, whatever happens to the burial ground, the bodies be treated respectfully under the state’s Unmarked Burial Site Protection Act.

“The New Yorkers laid to rest at this historic burial ground worked to build this city even while it denied them their freedom,” James said in a statement Wednesday. “Their final resting place deserves respect. I welcome any effort to offer these souls the permanent protection they are owed, including this first step toward landmark designation.”

In her letter, Herman cited that law in noting that the fate of the lot will likely be complicated.

Landmark status, she wrote, “may not be the only or most appropriate tool to achieve that community’s goals.” Landmarked burial sites are usually either on public land or are privately-owned cemeteries that are still in use. She said that moving the remains to another site “would not typically be considered appropriate.”

Herman noted that the Unmarked Burial Site law provides for the involvement of the state’s archaeologist in consultation with the church about “appropriate next steps.”

A spokesperson for the state archaeologist on Wednesday did not respond to a request for comment.
