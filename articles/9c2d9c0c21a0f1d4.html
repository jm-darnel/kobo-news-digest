NYC parents are leaving work over childcare costs. Free care could help, report says.

By Karen Yi · Gothamist · November 17, 2026

Working moms are disproportionately leaving their jobs or cutting back their hours because they can’t afford to pay for childcare, a new report says. And that’s hurting businesses and New York City’s economy.

About 15% of New Yorkers surveyed this year had to change their work situation after becoming parents, the report said. Most changed their work hours and 25% left the workforce altogether, costing about $1.7 billion in annual income. Of the parents who left or lost their jobs, 73% were women, half were Black or Hispanic, and 43% earned under $50,000 a year. The report was based on surveys with about 3,000 parents so did not offer historical comparisons or similar statistics in other cities.

“We felt like it was really important to make sure all New Yorkers understood that public investments in childcare benefit the entire city by benefiting the economy,” said Grace Rauh, executive director of Citizens Union, a good-government watchdog group.

Rauh said when parents are offered free childcare, “that's money that would've otherwise been going out the door to pay for childcare that is now in their pocket. The savings would allow them to spend money on housing, food, transportation.”

It also allows parents the option to return to work. The survey asked unemployed parents if they would return to work if they had free childcare, and 22% said they would. Of those who said they would, 95% were women. Many of them said they wanted to work in healthcare, food services and education, the report said.

“If they had the choice, these women would rather be working,” Rauh said.

“We know that women disproportionately bear the responsibility for a lot of child-rearing, a lot of domestic activities in the home. I think this figure really bears that out and shows how disproportionate the impact of having a child is on women in particular.”

Ruth Vargas, 25, said she would have gone back to work and enrolled in school if she were offered 35 hours of free childcare for her twin 2-year-old boys. She was their primary caretaker until they were about 18 months, which prolonged her return to work. She was finally able to secure a childcare voucher for their care and is working as an administrative assistant.

“I wanted to go back to college, and I kept trying to look for alternatives on how I could go back, maybe online, and then I realized that my kids needed me 24/7, and I was very frustrated because I wanted to just do something,” she said. “I wanted to feel human again when it came to Ruth as just me and not Ruth as being a mom and a caregiver all the time.”

Childcare costs parents an average of $21,000 a year in the city and many parents go into debt to cover the cost, the report said.

Federal guidelines suggest childcare costs shouldn’t exceed 7% of a household’s budget.

But it can make up more than a third of the budget for New York families earning less than $50,000 a year. Even for families making more than $200,000, childcare can cost 13% of their income, the report said.

Rauh said when parents leave the workforce, “ their earnings leave, the money that they spend in our local economy leaves with them.”

One in two surveyed parents who left the city in the last three years cited the cost of living and childcare as a driving factor.

High childcare costs don’t just harm parents, but businesses, too, the report said. More than half the parents who were surveyed missed at least one day of work in the past three months due to childcare issues and on average lost three to four days of work and up to five partial days in the same time period.

For workers who get paid by the hour, that can come out to 10.9 million missed hours of work or roughly $360 million in lost earnings a year, the report estimated.

“If you have an employee who you've invested in, you have trained them, they are rising up the career ladder, they're becoming more valuable to you as an employee, and then they're pulling out or pulling back, reducing their hours or leaving the workforce entirely,” Rauh said.

“That's a real loss to you as a business and an employer.”
