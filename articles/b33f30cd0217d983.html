Matchbooks are on fire at NYC restaurants

By Hannah Frishberg · Gothamist · November 23, 2026

Cigarette smoking is on the decline, disposable lighters are cheap and easily available, and vapes are self-contained, and yet the humble matchbook has lit a fire in New York’s fine dining scene. Indeed, the retro collectible has made a full-fledged return as a restaurant giveaway and as an opulent piece of pocket-sized art.

At Manhattan’s buzzy small chain Dante, where the large format matchbooks boast gold leaf inlay, custom interior artwork and cost approximately $4 each to make, they had to stop handing them out at the front door. “ It was financially becoming quite burdensome,” said the restaurant’s co-founder Linden Pride. The matchbooks are now only available as a free add-on with purchase of their Negroni fragrance candle.

In addition to their classic appeal as miniature memorabilia from a nice meal, modern diners also find them attractive as tangible objects in an increasingly digitally-based world.

“I think in the digital age where, like, everything is online, people really want something to hold onto physically,” said Connor Pacharis, who late last year founded the website Matchbook Directory so he and his fellow phillumenists could have a place to congregate and swap sticks.

On social media and within the directory’s now nearly 900-user strong community share and trade matchbooks, which are prized for their aesthetic appeal and as compact reminders of real world experiences.

Indeed, most directory users are looking to acquire matchbooks from places they’ve visited but failed to nab a book from, or to determine which restaurants have matchbooks, so they can dine there.

“ I can go on my camera roll and I have 10,000 photos from one vacation,” said Pacharis, a San Diego-based product manager who recently visited New York, by far the most popular location in the directory. “But when I think about my New York trip, I don't really think about the photos I took. I think about my New York matchbook collection, and what a good time I had.”

City restaurateurs are aware of this burgeoning market of significantly young people, and have entered something of an aesthetic design race to keep up and stay competitive in a tough business.

When 19-year New York City restaurateur Joshua Boissy opened now-shuttered Greenpoint eatery Sauvage in 2016, they made multiple matchbooks, a choice which was “was totally psychotic, but, like, a really fun thing to do at the time; it was very unusual,” despite it not being the most cost effective. Although he’s yet to see anyone else do the multiple style shtick, luxury matchbooks are now far less of a surprising marketing stunt.

“The matchbook game has evolved a lot. We've been doing it for a while at each of our places, and I remember, you know, from 2011 when we opened Maison, matchbooks were not treated this way. They were all kind of very similar,” said Boissy, co-founder of the Lower East Side’s swanky cocktail lounge Tigre.

But in New York City fine dining “there’s always an upping of the game,” and others have since gotten in on the luxury matchbook craze, he said.

In addition to being a trendy and in-demand piece of branded merchandise, the fact that they are physical reminders of a good meal long after the bill has been paid makes them quite ideal from a marketing perspective.

"They serve as a lasting extension of our branding,” said KwangHo Lee, owner of fine-dining institutions One Bryant Park and Momoya, both of which boast delicately gilded books carefully tailored to evoke the same upscale aesthetic as their corresponding restaurants.

“In a digital world, having a physical keepsake to say ‘Hey’ with makes a lasting impression and keeps us top of mind,” added Jacob Hadjigeorgis, owner of the Upper West Side Southern food establishment Jacob’s Pickles, which boasts matchbooks inspired “by a little nostalgia for a time when restaurants had more personality in the small details.”
