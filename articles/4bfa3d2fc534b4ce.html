City officials and housing advocates say discrimination against voucher holders remains a chronic problem. The city’s Commission on Human Rights consistently reports source of income discrimination as its most frequent complaint, deeming it a proxy for other forms of bias, like discrimination based on race or against families with children. Landlords counter that government programs are plagued by onerous rules and requirements that delay the time before a tenant can move in.

“Today’s decision is a major victory for New Yorkers who rely on rental assistance to find and keep a home,” said attorney Diane Houk from the firm Emery Celli, who submitted a brief to the court on behalf of fair-housing advocacy groups.

Tenants qualify for voucher programs like Section 8 and the city’s CityFHEPS program based on their earnings. They typically pay no more than 30% of income on rent, while the subsidy program covers the remainder. About 123,000 households in New York City use the Section 8 program to pay a portion of their rent, while more than 60,000 New York City households use CityFHEPS, according to city data.
