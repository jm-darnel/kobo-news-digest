Advocates' vision for discounted NJ Transit fares could be an uphill climb

By Michael Sol Warren · Gothamist · November 27, 2026

Jasai Howard-Wright has always relied on public transit to get around.

But the bus isn’t free, and a cash-strapped college student can feel the costs add up quickly. Howard-Wright estimates at least a quarter of his expenses are transit-related.

“A lot of the money that I earned over the summer is going towards these transportation costs,” Howard-Wright said.

And that cost is constantly rising, with 3% automatic annual fare increases for NJ Transit.

“It used to only cost $10 to get from Somerville to Newark Penn Station. Now it costs almost $15 just to do it. You know, it's crazy,” Howard-Wright said. “It's a $5 increase, and other people might say, ‘Oh, it's only a $5 difference.’ But it adds up these days, especially when everything else is so expensive.”

Howard-Wright is just one of thousands of low-income New Jerseyans transit advocates say should have access to discounted rides on NJ Transit buses, trains and light rail lines.

A coalition of advocacy groups in the Garden State launched the Fair Fares NJ campaign earlier this year. Their goal is to convince state leaders to expand NJ Transit’s reduced fare program to make some people eligible based on income thresholds. It’s currently open to seniors, people with disabilities and military personnel, and offers half-price fares on trains, buses and light rails.

Howard-Wright said a discount would go a long way for him.

“It would just put less stress on our day-to-day lives and help us focus on more essential things such as tuition and, you know, food and stuff like that,” Howard-Wright said.

They argue that making transit more accessible would spur other benefits in society.

“Transportation's the game board upon which all other games are played,” said Zoe Baldwin, the vice president of state programs for the Regional Plan Association, which advocates for improving public transit throughout the New York City region. “When you're talking about public health, if you can't reliably access or afford to get to healthcare, that's going to impact public health. It's going to impact unemployment. It's going to impact our traffic. It's going to impact our air quality.”

Many other transit systems around the country already offer reduced fares for low-income riders. New York City’s Fair Fares program offers a 50% discount on subways and eligible buses for people with incomes up to 150% of the federal poverty level. That program will expand to include people making up to 200% of the federal poverty level at the end of November.

More than 370,000 New Yorkers are enrolled in the city’s program, though about 1.3 million will be eligible under its new rules. To try and boost enrollment, the New York City Council passed a law allowing people to simply check a box and enroll in Fair Fares when renewing with SNAP.

Baldwin said the campaign has had talks with NJ Transit and Gov. Mikie Sherrill’s office about expanding New Jersey’s reduced fares program to include income-based eligibility.

NJ Transit President Kris Kolluri said he agrees with the general goal of expanding transit access. But he said if it were to come to fruition, he’d want to see another agency in charge of administering enrollment and handling the fare subsidy because NJ Transit is facing a structural deficit that fluctuates between $100 million and $200 million year-to-year.

“What I would not advise is for someone to impose this laudable goal onto the existing transit balance sheet because that doesn't do anything to help us get out of the hole,” Kolluri said. “But if it serves as a separate program organized by the Legislature, if they think it's the right thing to do, separate and apart from New Jersey Transit, I think it's something I'm sure everybody would be interested in at least hearing about.”

“We need to make sure that we're creating a program that works for how the agency works,” Baldwin said.

Alex Ambrose, a senior policy analyst for the New Jersey Policy Perspective think tank, said it's likely the state could tie enrollment in NJ Transit’s reduced fares with enrollment in other programs, like SNAP or utility bill relief efforts.

“There's lots of other programs that people qualify for, and this can be just one more thing we add on top of it,” Ambrose said.

“We're going to have to discuss how transit is funded broadly,” Baldwin said. “I think that's the most likely vehicle.”

New Jersey is coming off a tight budget season that saw Sherrill pressure lawmakers to reign in spending across state government. Next year’s budget is expected to be similar. That’s a big hurdle for the Fair Fares campaign to overcome. But Ambrose said if state lawmakers are serious about making New Jersey more affordable, this is the kind of policy they need to find the political will to support.

“It could happen during the next budget cycle if our lawmakers were willing to actually raise revenue instead of just cutting programs,” Ambrose said. “We keep hearing that it's going to be a tough budget year, but it's only a tough budget year if they decide it's going to be. We can raise revenue by closing corporate tax loopholes, raising taxes on the wealthiest New Jerseyans and the wealthiest corporations without actually harming everyday working New Jerseyans.”
