Two PhDs, Three Classes and One EBT Card: A CUNY Adjunct’s Struggle to Make Ends Meet

By Aisha Baiocchi · The City · November 17, 2026

At 37, and with two PhDs, Juliana Nalerio relies on SNAP and Medicaid to get by while teaching as an adjunct professor at the City University of New York.

Nalerio is one of the 11,000 part-time faculty, some earning as little as $7,000 a course, who make the country’s biggest urban public university run.

She teaches one class in the History department at City College, and two English courses at Queens College. This semester, that’s 70 students across three classes. Between Harlem, Flushing and her home in Astoria, Nalerio spends eight hours each week on the commute alone.

“I keep telling myself that next year will be better, and if I keep teaching well, and trying to write, it will turn into something more stable,” she told The City Reporter.

In the last two decades, universities across the nation have become more reliant on non-tenure track part-time professors, mostly working on contracts that span less than a year. CUNY is no exception; in fact it is at least 10% ahead of the national average, with part-timers making up 52% of instructional staff.

Glenn Colby, a senior research officer with the American Association of University Professors said he’s observed a steady increase in contingent hires since the 2008 financial crisis, one that’s only continued through the COVID-19 pandemic and the Trump administration’s research budget cuts and attacks on universities.

“The driver of it all is institutions trying to deal with the uncertainty around finances, which is tied to student enrollment and state funding,” Colby said. “And as those issues fluctuate, institutions try to deal with it by saying, well, we’re gonna hire people on contingent appointments because we’re not certain about the future.”

“Adjunct faculty play an indispensable role at CUNY, enriching the educational experience of students through their teaching, expertise and dedication,” university press secretary Noah Gardy said in a statement. “Recognizing the vital role they play, our current labor contract with the PSC significantly increased compensation and continued investments in greater stability and access to health insurance.”

At CUNY, enrollment numbers are rising, but still haven’t passed pre-pandemic highs. Fundraising drives have been heralded as successful, but the university still has a massive $7 billion maintenance backlog as colleges endure floods, leaks and broken elevators.

Against that backdrop, adjunct professors make financial sense for cash-strapped universities. Part-time faculty can also include working professionals who bring on-the-ground experience into the classroom, a significant bonus for CUNY’s pre-professional programs and schools.

‘I Do Unpaid Labor’

But adjuncts like Nalerio may be left out by this model.

Last year, packing advanced degrees from Stanford University and the University of Salamanca in Spain, Nalerio made just $36,000. It is less than a third of what the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics says is the average annual income for someone with a doctorate.

According to the most recent Poverty Tracker report by the nonprofit advocacy group Robin Hood, Nalerio’s income puts her well within New York City’s “low income” range, experiencing similar material hardship to those living below the poverty line.

“My goal was never to make a lot of money. I spent a lot of time learning to teach, research and write. But now I’m presented with this problem: the work I’ve trained to do doesn’t line up with the reality of the work right now. So I can do unpaid labor, and hope it gets better, as I do, or leave it all together.”

While dividing her time between campuses in Flushing and Harlem might seem like a burden, this semester is actually easier than last semester, Nalerio says, because her classes in the two boroughs are stacked on different days. The Spring semester had her rushing from her morning Harlem course out to Flushing by the late afternoon, every Tuesday and Thursday, at the mercy of the 7 train and the weather.

Each of the three classes Nalerio teaches this semester is different, and she designs the coursework herself. She has an introductory women and gender studies class, a composition class for international students learning English, and a civilizations after 1500 class at City College, which she’s been teaching the longest.

Brutal University Math

But that accounting is ludicrous, adjuncts say. Preparing course materials and grading student work takes far longer than an hour a week.

“If you have 70 students on two campuses it is literally impossible,” Nalerio said. She barely has time to eat between classes, much less find another part-time job.

Nalerio’s workload is determined by a union-negotiated contract: adjunct faculty can teach up to nine hours on one campus, and one additional section at another campus, which cannot exceed six hours. She teaches as many course hours as she can, always hoping to pick up more work to pay her bills — but is still working full-time while living on a part-time salary.

“My benefits are also dependent on my course load each semester,” she said, “but I don’t have time to keep track of the 75 students and the Tetris that they’re playing with my pay and my benefits and my life.”

Gardy, the spokesperson, said “CUNY adjunct pay remains highly competitive for a public university” and will be “nearly 75% above the national average” when a scheduled increase goes into effect next summer.

Roommate Life

A full-time assistant professor at the City University teaching the same course load as Nalerio would earn a starting salary of at least $57,000, with generous health and retirement benefits.

Jasmine Bayron, a Hunter College adjunct with a PhD, said it isn’t uncommon for part-timers to struggle to find time for other jobs. Bayron has a number of side gigs, which she tries to set up a year in advance in case any of her courses falls through. Like Nalerio, she also has roommates.

“It’s kind of crazy to spend your entire life in school and then you can’t afford to live by yourself,” Bayron said.

Colby suggested universities underpay adjuncts because the job pool for contingent labor is so large and little attention is paid within higher education to working conditions for part-timers.

“It’s a challenge to get a full-time faculty position if you don’t have experience teaching and so one thing people do is say, ‘Well, I’ll do this so that I have it on my CV that I’m an experienced teacher.’ But then it becomes a permanent situation for a lot of people,” he said.

‘Their Education Matters’

Five years into adjuncting, Nalerio is still figuring out if it can be a permanent situation for her.

She started teaching at CUNY during her last semester at Stanford in 2022, and says she continued after receiving her PhD because she has New York roots and “drank the Kool Aid” of the university’s mission to serve working class students.

“These people are relying on me — they’re young people with lives,” Nalerio said. “Their education matters. It is an important step, and they’re really important. Serving them is a full-time job.”

Nalerio customizes courses to her students, this past week changing a lecture for her history course at Queens College to better include the large contingent of Latinos in the classroom and showing a documentary about white masculinity in her gender studies class after noticing the students seem disconnected from the original course material.

“I feel a sense of purpose doing it,” she said. “And I know I need more stability, but I love it, and I love the students.”

But Nalerio hasn’t found stability in years. Since coming to New York in 2022, she’s only been able to sublet since because her income is so low and inconsistent that she hasn’t qualified to rent an apartment in her own name.

She can’t always afford to eat. She got a dog, for her mental health, and enrolled in SNAP to lift some of the stress off, but she’s still “just getting by.”

While representatives at CUNY’s Professional Staff Congress have suggested Nalerio apply for administrative jobs at the university, and she’s thought about leaving academia altogether, her heart is set on teaching. She loves ideas, her students, and the institution.

“It’s a one-sided affair,” she said. “Its very clear CUNY doesn’t love me back.”
