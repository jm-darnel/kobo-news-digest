Yankees Potential Free Agent Target: Randy Arozarena

By John Griffin · Pinstripe Alley · November 23, 2026

Over the last few years, the Yankees have entered every single winter needing to add at least one outfielder. Following the disastrous 2023 season, where Isiah Kiner-Falefa, Billy McKinney, and Franchy Cordero combined for 141 games (and that’s just three of the many disastrous outfielders rotated through that season), the team rebuilt just about their entire outfield corps in two days by swinging deals for Trent Grisham, Alex Verdugo, and Juan Soto. With the departure of the latter two in free agency, the team traded for Cody Bellinger the following year, and with both Grisham and Bellinger free agents after last season, the team’s No. 1 priority last winter was to keep the band together.

This year, that has changed. Aaron Judge and Bellinger are under contract for the foreseeable future, Grisham is a goner, Spencer Jones acquitted himself well in 86 games, and despite his troubles in 2026, the Yankees don’t appear to have completely given up on former top prospect Jasson Domínguez. And let’s not forget Heliot Ramos, who is currently slated to serve as a platoon bench bat.

That being said, the team desperately needs to add some right-handed bats this winter. While the Yankees did, technically, hit better against lefties than righties this past year — a 105 wRC+ against southpaws, compared to 100 against righties — the lineup leaned left-handed dominant last season. And so, although third base and the bullpen are undoubtedly the Yankees’ priorities this winter, they have been doing their due diligence, checking in on a number of right-handed bats. The top of that list? An old divisional nemesis, Randy Arozarena .

2025 Statistics: 154 games, 670 PA, .281/.381/.484, 26 HR, 34 doubles, 79 RBI, 26 SB, 148 OPS+, 151 wRC+, -2 DRS, -5 OAA, 5.7 rWAR, 5.5 fWAR

2026 FanGraphs Depths Charts Projections: 156 games, 672 PA, .252/.353/.432, 23 HR, 30 doubles, 80 RBI, 20 SB, 125 wRC+, 2.4 fWAR

Yankees fans are, undoubtedly, familiar with Arozarena. Born and raised in Cuba in 1995, he defected to Mexico in 2015 and signed with the St. Louis Cardinals as an international amateur the following year. After three years climbing the St. Louis farm system, he made his MLB debut in August 2019. That winter, he was traded from the Cardinals to the Rays, and although he missed the first half of the COVID-shortened season with COVID, he exploded onto the scene during the expanded 2020 postseason. In 20 games played, he posted a .377/.442/.831 slash with 10 homers and 14 runs batted in as he became the first position player to win ALCS MVP as a rookie; he particularly terrorized the Yankees in the ALDS, with eight hits (including three homers) in five games.

Over the next few years, he racked up accolades in Tampa Bay, winning the 2021 Rookie of the Year Award, starting the 2023 All-Star Game, and recording three straight 20/20 seasons. At the 2024 Trade Deadline, the Rays flipped him to the Seattle Mariners, with whom he has spent the last two-and-a-half seasons. In that time, he has posted a .255/.357/.443 slash line, hit 58 homers, stole 61 bases, and went to two All-Star Games.

Now, off a career year in which he boasted a career-high 151 wRC+ (ignoring the COVID-shortened 2020, that is), Arozarena hits free agency for the first time. In what is admittedly a weak free agent class, he arguably represents the cream of the crop among position players: The Athletic ranks him second on their big board, behind only the just-signed Tarik Skubal, and MLB Trade Rumors puts him fifth overall (and beyond only Seiya Suzuki, among position players). FanGraphs is the lowest on him, ranking him 10th — but even then, he clocks in at fourth among position players.

Players with his combination of speed and power don’t exactly grow on trees, and the 2026 version of Arozarena would have been the best position player on the Yankees — his 151 wRC+ was two points higher than Ben Rice’s 149, his 5.5 fwAR more than a win better than Rice’s 4.2, and his 5.7 rWAR slightly more than a win over Bellinger’s 4.6. He has consistently put together strong batted-ball profiles on Statcast, overcoming a declining bat speed and a tendency to hit the ball into the dirt, to remain an offensive force who would look good near the top of any lineup. Even if he declines to his career norms in 2026 — very likely, given both his FanGraphs projections and his Statcast data — that is still a player who has posted a wRC+ below 120 just once in his career (and even then, that was “just” 114) and who has accrued at least 2-3 fWAR annually.

Nonetheless, there are reasons for the Yankees — and any team, really — to be cautious. Arozarena is, to put it bluntly, a liability with the glove: he has accrued -7 Defensive Runs Saved and -23 Outs Above Average over the course of his career, and since an above-average rookie campaign (he had 5 DRS and 1 OAA in 2021), he has put up negative numbers for at least one of the two major defensive categories in each season. Opposing teams, meanwhile, can run on his arm with relative ease. On the basepaths, he puts up large stolen base totals, but his overall baserunning profile has been inconsistent year-to-year.

In a vacuum, none of these would rule out Arozarena as a top target, as he has consistently hit more than well enough to make up for his shortcomings at the plate and his drunk-like baserunning. But he will be turning 32 in February, and is well on the wrong side of 30. If he seeks anything beyond MLB Trade Rumors’ projected four-year, $100 million contract , well, teams will be understandably hesitant to pull the trigger.

Contract and offensive production, though, are not the only areas of concern for the Yankees. As it currently stands, he’s a bit of an awkward fit. Arozarena has almost exclusively played left field in recent years, but the Yankees have arguably the best defensive left fielder in the sport in Bellinger. He has made only a couple of cameo appearances in center field over the years, but with his poor range, that’s not a true long-term solution. The team could try to hide him with the short porch in right, but that would expose his weak arm and necessitate putting Aaron Judge in center full-time (while the team won’t rule out using him there, I doubt they’re eager to put him there full-time to accommodate anyone less than a Soto-level superstar).

Ultimately, Arozarena is an imperfect player who would be an imperfect fit in the Bronx. Unfortunately, that’s about par for the course this winter — unless we get a sudden and unexpected revelation that a player like Elly De La Cruz or Fernando Tatís Jr. truly on the block, there’s no obvious golden fit. And adding Arozarena would open up the possibility of using Jones, Domínguez, and/or Ramos as trade chips to upgrade the infield or pitching staff. With the right mix of surrounding moves, the three-time All-Star could play a major role in reshaping a Yankees roster than needs an influx of offensive talent — if the price is right, that is.
