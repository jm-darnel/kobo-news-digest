Over 6K NYC apartments for homeless people sat vacant in September, city data shows

By Karen Yi · Gothamist · November 20, 2026

There were more apartments reserved for homeless New Yorkers sitting empty in September than the number of people sleeping outside, city data shows.

That month, there were 6,159 vacant supportive housing units in the city, according to the most recent report released by the Department of Social Services, while the city’s yearly tally of New Yorkers living on the street counted nearly 5,000 people.

It’s the highest number since the city began releasing a monthly vacancy report last November. The abundance of empty space is causing homeless advocates to question whether city and state leaders are doing enough to prioritize housing homeless residents as colder temperatures approach.

“It is a number that should really frighten everyone,” said Craig Hughes, a social worker for Legal Services NYC, which offers low-income New Yorkers free legal help.

“These aren’t numbers that are bureaucratic. They are numbers of places that people could live, which could potentially save their lives,” he said.

Supportive housing units are reserved for homeless New Yorkers who have a mental illness, substance abuse disorder or other special needs. The units receive a mix of city, state and federal funding. Tenants pay no more than 30% of their income toward rent. They receive additional services to help them stay housed, like counseling or case management.

There are more than 42,000 such apartments across the city.

“Having a vacancy rate is not inherently a bad thing, if we were at a zero percent vacancy or 100% occupancy then the 10,000 people who were approved for supportive housing would have nowhere to go,” said Tierra Labrada, director of policy and advocacy for the network.

Of the more than 6,100 vacant units in September, 4,270 did not have anyone assigned to them, nor anyone in the process of moving in, the data shows. Some of those units are undergoing repairs or renovations after a move-out.

“If we simply focus all of our energy on renovating these supportive housing units, bringing them online, and prioritizing people who are in Safe Havens and living on the streets, we could make an enormous difference in addressing the street homelessness crisis that is plaguing us today,” said Councilmember Lincoln Restler of Brooklyn, who championed the law making vacancy reporting mandatory.

Nearly half of the empty units are overseen by the state’s Office of Mental Health, which controls a large share of the city’s supportive housing stock. Of the 2,753 vacant units overseen by the state, 400 of them had a tenant waiting to move in.

Agency officials said there were various reasons for the vacancies. Residents move out, and it can take a while to fix the unit to ready it for a new tenant. Some people also reject placements that come with intensive services or shared rooms, and there can be bureaucratic delays in the admissions process.

Hughes said providers can often reject applicants for vague reasons and it can take months to move a person found eligible into an empty unit. He said the city also needs to do more to prevent unnecessary evictions that lead to turnover of units.

Justin Mason, a spokesperson for the state Office of Mental Health said officials understand the need to fill as many available beds as possible and are working to reduce delays in the process. “We are taking measures — including regulatory change — that will benefit New Yorkers living with serious mental illness and in need of housing,” he added.

Officials with the city’s Department of Health and Mental Hygiene, which had more than 20% of the vacancies, said about 200 units were available and empty, while others were either involved in legal disputes, had to address violations or were new and waiting for a certificate of occupancy.

“The vast majority are occupied, with less than 2% vacant and available for someone to move in,” said William Fowler, a spokesperson for the agency.

Vacancies in city supportive housing have long plagued the industry, even as the city’s homeless rates remain elevated. In addition to the 5,000 New Yorkers sleeping on streets or in parks and subways, more than 83,000 New Yorkers are sleeping in shelters.

In February, Mayor Zohran Mamdani told Gothamist he would prioritize filling the empty apartments after a brutal stretch of cold killed 29 New Yorkers, some of them homeless. The number of vacant units has nearly doubled since then.

“This administration is committed to addressing the underlying causes of vacancies, cutting unnecessary red tape and streamlining processes so more New Yorkers can access the stable, supportive housing they need,” City Hall spokesperson Sneha Choudhary said in a statement.

A spokesperson for the Department of Social Services said the agency is more quickly making repairs and moved more people into apartments this year than last. Nicholas Jacobelli said the agency maintained a low vacancy rate for the units they oversee.

Hughes said both the city and state needed to look at the vacancy rates as “crisis numbers.”

”You have 6,000 empty apartments, and you have thousands of people on the street and almost record numbers of people in the municipal shelters, it's really hard to really make that make sense,” he said.
