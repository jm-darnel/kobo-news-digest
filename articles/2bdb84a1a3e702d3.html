Does an opera have to make sense? 'Einstein on the Beach' at BAM is a chance to find out.

By Sonia Rao · Gothamist · November 18, 2026

Earlier this week, Michael Riesman, the 83-year-old music director of the Philip Glass Ensemble, sat at a piano in an upstairs rehearsal space at the Brooklyn Academy of Music. His fingers flew furiously across the keys as he played nonstop arpeggios. The sound echoed through the room.

Riesman sat with his back to nearly a dozen people whose chairs were arranged in a neat semicircle. They were singing in perfect unison, but the sounds floating from their mouths were odd: They sang a series of numbers. “One, two-two, two-three, two-four, two,” they sang staccato, lifting their chins to project the nonsensical lyrics toward the airy room’s ceiling. Riesman, still playing his arpeggios, gave them live feedback: “The twos are a little long,” he said. Then, “the twos are a little late.” Eventually, the twos sounded right. Phew.

This Thursday through Saturday, Riesman, the ensemble, and the chorus will perform the cacophonous number as part of BAM’s 50th anniversary concert of composer Philip Glass and director Robert Wilson’s 1976 opera “Einstein on the Beach.” This is the fourth time a version of the groundbreaking work will be performed at BAM, following previous, fuller-scale productions in 1984, 1992 and 2012. The opera’s four acts are connected by “knee plays,” or intermezzos named after the connective joint. In its original form, “Einstein” lasts over four hours without an intermission, though audience members have been encouraged to wander in and out of the performance at will. BAM will put on a truncated concert version, which runs 2 hours and 35 minutes with an intermission.

Before the rehearsal, Riesman, a tall, silver-haired man who has been part of the Philip Glass Ensemble since 1974, said that he is proud to continue leading a production that “opened the door to a whole new concept of what you could do in an opera house.”

“Einstein” lacks a traditional narrative. Though the German-born physicist appears onstage in the opera, the performances unfolding around him offer largely abstract interpretations of his life and achievements. At one point, a soloist — choreographer Lucinda Childs in the original production — delivers a speech about visiting a “prematurely air-conditioned supermarket.” Over and over, she recalls that “there were all these bathing caps that you could buy.” Somehow, you might connect these happenings to Einstein’s theory of relativity. (If you don’t understand it, don’t worry: Wilson has said over the years that he didn’t know what “Einstein” was really about, either. This clip might help paint a more vivid picture.)

As music director of the Philip Glass Ensemble, Riesman has been tasked with carrying on the legacy of Glass’ work, renowned for its minimalist and repetitive qualities. He said he maintains a deep connection with the hypnotic music of “Einstein.”

“It’s had such a profound effect on people,” Riesman said. “I’ve had people come up to me and say, ‘I just saw “Einstein” and it changed my life. It opened my eyes to something. It made me want to get into this kind of thing.’”

But has there ever been anything else of this kind? As early as the mid-1980s, roughly a decade after “Einstein” premiered at Festival d’Avignon in France, filmmaker Mark Obenhaus heralded the production in a televised documentary as one that “changed forever the image of opera.” In the film, Glass says he and Wilson are offering audiences a “profoundly different way of relating to music theater” than, say, a production of Rodgers and Hammerstein’s “Oklahoma!”

“It’s really only an opera in terms of how it relates to the history of opera,” Riesman said. Wilson, also credited as the show’s designer, establishes early on in the documentary that he refers to “Einstein” as an opera simply “because it means opus, or work.”

“I never had a regular job,” he said. “Philip basically became a full-time job, paid my rent, and that’s worth a lot to me. Maybe it took away from my own creative work to some extent … but I did manage to slip in a few pieces.”

Several singers performing at the upcoming BAM shows are veterans who sang in the “Einstein” revival a dozen years ago, and even back in the 1990s. But a few of them, in their early 20s, are new to the production. That keeps with the “Einstein” tradition, according to Riesman, who noted that many of the original cast members weren’t trained dancers or singers.

“Sometimes it feels like I happened to be scratching my head when somebody said, ‘Raise your hand if you want to,’” Bora said, laughing.

Neither he nor Riesman seems afraid of putting their own spin on things; in fact, Bora said, he doesn’t consider it his responsibility to preserve what Glass and Wilson created in 1976.

“Einstein” is a meticulously written opera, full of “ones,” “one-twos” and “one-two-three-four-five-six-sevens” that hit exactly when they are supposed to. But it remains permutable as a show. The songs are written to be condensed if needed, as they are in the concert version. The monologues read aloud throughout the four acts have been tweaked over the years. In 1993, one was scrapped entirely when an actor replaced the scripted version with a new speech called “All Men Are Equal.”

“‘Einstein’ is a tabula rasa. It's an empty canvas,” Riesman said. “It’s never going to be Bob Wilson’s performance again, and it’s never going to be the [original] Philip Glass Ensemble’s performance again. And it shouldn’t be.”
