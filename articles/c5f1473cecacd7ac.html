Betty Rauch: Sharing secrets to strong first impressions

By Pat Kiernan, Chelsea Katz · NY1 · November 16, 2026

For Betty Rauch, the most important part of a job interview happens at the beginning.

"If people are comfortable and, and, and about those first 45 seconds, the rest of the journey is going to be smooth," Rauch said.

Her advice: Greet someone with the appropriate time of day, clearly state the job you're seeking and treat your resume and your name with reverence.

For the students in her class, the emphasis on their names is especially important.

"When you're incarcerated, you're a number. You're not an individual," Rauch said. "And to talk to them about saying their name with pride and conviction and to love their name again is really telling them to love themselves."

"Imagine if you've been incarcerated and you are going to have to talk about your life and convince somebody that you're out there, you know, willing and eager to work," Rauch said.

Rauch has volunteered with The Fortune Society for more than 50 years. She has served on its board of directors, taught computer skills and taken on other roles.

Around 2012, she realized clients weren't getting a true interview experience when they practiced with familiar faces.

"That was not a helpful situation because they knew the staff and they were therefore comfortable," she said.

Rauch recruited other volunteers to conduct mock interviews while she focused on those vital first moments. Her program is known as "First Impressions."

"They have had the opportunity to practice what it's like to do a handshake and often to have a smile," said Carler Dinnold, associate vice president of education and employment at The Fortune Society.

"Betty told me that I was extremely charming but to have some dignity," said Naseer Shaheed, a graduate of the career development program. "Don't throw yourself out there like that."

Rauch said her goal is to help participants feel better about themselves as they work toward their goals.

"There's so much hurt in, in these participants and I just want to make them feel better about themselves and get where and to get where they want to go," she said.

For helping people who have served time navigate those first moments of a job interview, Betty Rauch is NY1's New Yorker of the Week.
